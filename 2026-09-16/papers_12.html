Abstract

Substrate surface preparation is a key step in the fabrication of electronic devices. In III--V semiconductor platforms, e.g. used in HEMTs and lasers, removal of the native substrate oxide is extremely important, where improper removal will negatively affect end-of-line device performance. However, the impact of substrate preparation in hybrid superconductor--semiconductor (S--Sm) systems relevant to quantum information applications remains poorly understood. This study compares three surface preparations for the deposition of sputtered Nb films on InP: (i) no intentional oxide removal (control), (ii) \textit{in-situ} $\mathrm{Ar}^{+}$ milling, and (iii) S-passivation. \textit{In-situ} $\mathrm{Ar}^{+}$ milling reduces the O concentration at the metal--substrate (MS) interface, but also roughens the InP surface, increasing the effective thickness of the Nb--InP interface and promoting O incorporation through extended defects in the Nb film. S-passivation suppresses interfacial O more effectively while preserving a sharper and smoother buried interface. It also yields Nb films with higher superconducting transition temperatures and less structural damage than the $\mathrm{Ar}^{+}$-milled samples. Despite these materials improvements, the microwave response is comparable across the three preparations. At single photon powers, the highest internal quality factors, $Q_i$, are approximately $1.30\times10^{5}$ ($130\mathrm{k}$) for the control resonators, $9.7\times10^{4}$ ($97\mathrm{k}$) for the S-passivated resonators, and $8.4\times10^{4}$ ($84\mathrm{k}$) for the $\mathrm{Ar}^{+}$-milled resonators. These results suggest that the present devices are not primarily limited by dielectric loss at the buried Nb--InP interface.

Abstract

This paper investigates the holographic Casimir effect in AdS/BCFT with a brane-localized scalar field. The scalar field takes different values at the strip's two boundaries, leading to mixed boundary conditions. The massive brane-localized scalar field typically breaks the boundary conformal symmetries, allowing us to study the holographic Casimir effect in non-CFTs. In two-dimensional spacetime, we apply the holographic g-theorem to prove the holographic bound on the Casimir effect for general brane-localized matter fields. In higher dimensions, we find that the brane-localized scalar field reduces the Casimir amplitude when the mass squared \(m^2=0\), but can increase it when \(m^2 < 0\). Additionally, there is a no-hair theorem for the cases where \(m^2>0\). These findings indicate that relevant boundary deformations can enhance the Casimir effect. As a byproduct, we obtain a gravitational dual of the repulsive Casimir force under mixed boundary conditions and argue that it aligns with the cosmic censorship conjecture.

Abstract

Swift electrons in scanning transmission electron microscopy transfer both linear and angular momentum to nanoparticles, underlying electron-beam-driven nanoscale manipulation ("electron tweezers"). Prior theory relied either on the small-particle (dipolar) approximation, valid only well below experimentally relevant sizes, or on frequency-integrated multipolar calculations that leave the spectral structure of the interaction unresolved. Here we present a fully retarded, causal, multipole-converged electrodynamical methodology for the angular momentum transfer from a swift electron to an isolated spherical nanoparticle, based on a closed-surface Maxwell stress tensor formulation whose angular integrals reduce analytically to a small, material- and trajectory-independent set of irreducible integrals over associated Legendre functions. This lowers the cost of the double multipolar sum from O(l_max^4) to O(l_max^3), enabling convergence up to l_max=51 for nanoparticles as large as a=50 nm, nearly four times the order of the largest previous calculation at this size and previously unreached for an optically complex material, at three to four orders of magnitude lower cost. Applied to aluminum and gold nanoparticles up to a=50 nm, the method resolves the transfer's spectral density across the full frequency domain, showing it is set by interference between the electron and scattered fields, dominating at essentially every frequency; the electric contribution exceeds the magnetic one by two to three orders of magnitude, though spectral resolution reveals sign changes in the magnetic term hidden in the frequency-integrated transfer. At a=50 nm, gold transfers substantially more angular momentum than aluminum despite its more intricate response, by a factor growing with velocity from about 2x at v=0.5c to more than 6x as v->c (fixed b=51 nm from the nanoparticle center).

Abstract

This approach reveals their spectral behavior in terms of electric far-field dipole intensities when modeled with an analytical dipole model. This comprehensive spectroscopic method improves the accuracy of dipole orientation measurements and enables modern machine learning models to interpret potentially complex features of nanostructures.

Abstract

Here we analyze two strategies for measuring the spin-torque efficiency acting on an insulating magnet, focusing on the torque from Pt acting on thin films of the low-damping magnet lithium aluminum ferrite (LAFO). This becomes relevant for insulating magnets in which the magneto-optic coupling coefficient is very weak.

Abstract

We reassess the semiclassical route to integer quantum Hall transport around one idea: the separation of fast, quantised cyclotron motion from a slow, self-consistently screened guiding-centre landscape. Bohr--Sommerfeld quantisation of the canonical action fixes the Landau spectrum; smooth confinement turns it into Born--Oppenheimer surfaces with adiabatic parameter $\ell|\nabla V|/\hbarω_c$; screening supplies the self-consistent field. Transport is quasilocal, $R_H=\intρ_Hj_y\,dx/\int j_y\,dx$ with $j_y\propto1/ρ_l$, so within the regularised local Ohm model the incompressible strips attract the current by weight and the computed Joule-power profile follows the current profile. In linear response the intrinsic electrostatic AC correction of the frozen-profile model is reactive, $\mathrm{Im}\,R_H\proptoω$, with $\mathrm{Re}\,R_H-R_H^{\rm DC}\proptoω^{2}$ below $10^{-9}$ up to $2$~MHz. With the current fed back into the electrostatics at $1$--$5\,μ$A, where the Hall drop is one to five cyclotron gaps, the driven boundary condition breaks the reflection symmetry of the bar, the Hall drop concentrates in one strip (interchanged to $10^{-9}$ under current reversal), and the plateau narrows from its low-field flank while, for the GaAs parameters studied, its high-field edge stays at $ν(0)=2$. A local transverse thermal closure inspired by Akera, evaluated as a post-process on the frozen profile with two phonon-loss laws, indicates that the narrow current-carrying strips of the low-field flank may heat by of order the lattice temperature; with the activated-heat-capacity closure the cold strip loses its stationary solution at model-dependent currents of $0.3$--$2\,μ$A. The thermal conclusions are bounded by the loss law and by the frozen profile, and are presented as estimates.

Abstract

Superconducting boron-doped diamond (BDD) films were grown on 4-inch silicon wafers by microwave plasma chemical vapour deposition using gas-phase B/C ratios ranging from 6536 to 36421 ppm. Surface morphology, boron incorporation and superconducting properties were investigated as a function of gas-phase boron concentration. No systematic variation in apparent lateral grain size was observed across the series. Superconductivity was observed in all films except that grown at a B/C ratio of 6536 ppm within the measured temperature range down to 2 K. The superconducting transition temperature initially increased with increasing B/C ratio, reaching a maximum T$_c$ of 4.03 K at 24691 ppm, before decreasing at higher gas-phase B/C ratios. The corresponding resistive upper critical field at 2 K reached 3.091 T. Raman spectroscopy showed an increase in boron incorporation with increasing gas-phase B/C ratio up to 30303 ppm, followed by a slight decrease at 36421 ppm. Comparison with previous growth on 2-inch wafers showed that substantially higher gas-phase B/C ratios were required to obtain comparable boron concentrations and superconducting properties on 4-inch wafers, indicating reduced boron incorporation efficiency during large-area growth. These results demonstrate the feasibility of producing superconducting BDD over a substantial area of a 4-inch silicon wafer while identifying boron incorporation and radial uniformity as key parameters for further wafer-scale optimisation.

Summary. This theoretical work shows that while topological insulators with warped Fermi surfaces possess the ingredients for a nonlinear Hall effect, residual symmetries forbid it at equilibrium. By applying linearly polarized light in the off-resonant Floquet regime, the authors successfully break this symmetry, generating a controllable Berry curvature dipole. This provides a practical, light-induced mechanism to activate symmetry-forbidden quantum transport.

Why it may be interesting. This work demonstrates a precise method of 'Floquet symmetry engineering' to activate a transport phenomenon (NHE) that is otherwise forbidden by fundamental symmetries, which is a key concept in open quantum systems and light-matter interaction.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The nonlinear Hall effect (NHE) in topological insulators (TIs) with warped Fermi surfaces is intrinsically symmetry-forbidden in equilibrium.

Main result. Linearly polarized light can break the necessary rotational symmetry, generating a finite, tunable Berry curvature dipole (BCD) that activates the NHE without breaking time-reversal symmetry.

Method. The authors used Floquet theory to model the interaction with light and calculated the BCD, which governs the NHE, by analyzing symmetry constraints.

Model / system. The system is a topological insulator (like Bi2Te3) with a hexagonally warped Fermi surface, modeled by a warped Dirac Hamiltonian. The optical activation is introduced via a time-dependent perturbation from linearly polarized light.

Key observables. The Berry curvature dipole (BCD), which dictates the nonlinear Hall current density (J_y proportional to BCD_x E_x^2).

Important parameters / regimes. Light intensity ($A_0$), light polarization angle ($ heta$), and the warping strength ($\lambda_h$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is performed in the off-resonant Floquet regime and relies on the relaxation time approximation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the evolution of conduction band contours, the Berry curvature under light, and quantitatively show how the BCD becomes non-zero and tunable by light polarization and intensity.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes that equilibrium warping leads to zero BCD due to threefold rotational symmetry. It then introduces LPL to break this symmetry, deriving an effective Hamiltonian and calculating the resulting finite, controllable BCD, culminating in quantitative predictions for measurable currents.

Abstract

Topological insulators (TIs) with hexagonally warped Fermi surface are natural platforms for the nonlinear Hall effect, as warping breaks inversion symmetry while preserving time-reversal symmetry (TRS). Here we show that this inversion breaking alone is insufficient: although warping generates a strongly anisotropic Berry curvature, the preserved threefold rotational symmetry forces the equilibrium Berry curvature dipole (BCD) to vanish identically. We demonstrate that linearly polarized light removes this symmetry obstruction: in the off-resonant Floquet regime, it lowers the rotational symmetry while preserving TRS, thereby generating a finite BCD whose magnitude, orientation, and sign are continuously tunable by the light intensity and polarization. For realistic Bi$_2$Te$_3$ parameters, we show that the induced BCD reaches $\sim$0.03~nm, yielding microampere-scale nonlinear Hall currents under experimentally accessible conditions. Our results therefore establish Floquet symmetry engineering as a route to activating the symmetry-forbidden nonlinear transport on TI surfaces without breaking the TRS.

Vortex solitons in star networks

Authors: Yaroslav V. Kartashov
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.13951v1

Summary. This work analyzes vortex solitons in complex star network waveguide arrays, demonstrating that the system's discrete rotational symmetry dictates the possible topological charges. The stability and existence of these solitons are critically dependent on how their associated linear modes interact with the extended state band of the structure. This reveals complex, symmetry-protected behavior distinct from simpler ring geometries.

Why it may be interesting. The study of topological defects (vortices) in structured nonlinear media, especially where the underlying linear spectrum has band gaps, is highly relevant to quantum optics and nonlinear wave physics, providing insights into robust information carriers.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the existence, properties, and stability of vortex solitons within two-dimensional star networks formed by coupled line waveguide arrays.

Main result. The star networks support rich families of vortex solitons whose stability and behavior are highly dependent on the system's discrete rotational symmetry and whether the linear mode lies above or below the extended state band.

Method. The analysis involves solving the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NSE) by first finding localized linear eigenmodes and then analyzing the stability of the resulting soliton solutions via direct evolution of perturbed fields.

Model / system. The system is modeled as a star network of coupled one-dimensional line waveguides, governed by a nonlinear wave equation. The structure's symmetry is controlled by the number of rays (N) and the central ring radius, tuned by a shift parameter (delta).

Key observables. Topological charge (m), soliton power (U), propagation constant (b), and stability (stable/unstable branches).

Important parameters / regimes. Number of rays (N), central ring radius (controlled by delta), and the relative position of the linear mode's propagation constant with respect to the extended state band.

Assumptions / limitations. The waveguides are assumed to be Gaussian, and the analysis focuses on the case of a focusing nonlinear medium.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the refractive index profiles for different N and delta, show linear mode eigenvalues vs. delta, and map out the stability boundaries (U-b diagrams) for various vortex soliton families.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the physical system and linear modes to deriving the soliton solutions, and finally dedicating significant analysis to mapping out the stability properties of these vortex solitons, contrasting them with simpler ring geometries.

Abstract

I consider vortex solitons in two-dimensional star networks created by several one-dimensional line waveguide arrays (or rays) brought in close proximity. First waveguide in each array belongs to the ring of certain radius and its modal field overlaps with modal fields of waveguides from other rays laying on this ring, as well as with modal fields of waveguides in this line array. This creates a system with discrete rotational symmetry that is capable of supporting various localized linear vortex states due to local coupling between waveguides on the central ring, where line arrays approach each other. Localized vortex states emerge from the continuum of the states extended along the rays of the structure upon variation of the radius of the central ring. I show how different vortex modes with progressively increasing topological charges emerge when the number of rays in structure and, therefore, its discrete rotational symmetry increases. Each such linear localized mode gives rise to a family of thresholdless vortex solitons in nonlinear optical medium, whose topological charges are limited by the discrete rotational symmetry of the system. Vortex solitons in star networks exhibit qualitatively different behavior with increasing power depending on whether the propagation constant of linear vortex that gives rise to a family of solitons is located above or below the band of extended states. Stability properties of vortex solitons nontrivially depend on their topological charge and discrete rotational symmetry of the system, showing very different picture from usual ring-like waveguide arrays.

Constraint-Traceable Liquid-Nitrogen Dewar Design for Low-Noise HTS-SQUID Magnetometry

Summary. This paper presents a comprehensive engineering solution for designing a compact Dewar for HTS-SQUID magnetometers, balancing cryogenic endurance against physical size and mass limits. By developing a constraint-traceable thermal model, the authors predict long operational times while simultaneously demonstrating that the system maintains high magnetic sensitivity. This work establishes a robust framework for deploying sensitive quantum sensors in field-deployable, long-duration setups.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on cryogenics and instrumentation, the rigorous application of coupled constraint optimization and thermal modeling to ensure long-term, stable operation is relevant to any quantum system requiring sustained, controlled environmental conditions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the engineering challenge of designing a compact liquid-nitrogen Dewar for HTS-SQUID magnetometry that maximizes operational endurance while adhering to strict physical constraints like mass and envelope size.

Main result. The authors established a reproducible, constraint-traceable design workflow that predicts a long hold time (up to 224 h) and successfully demonstrated the compatibility of the cryogenic system with achieving low-noise SQUID measurements (41.5 fT Hz^-1/2).

Method. The methodology combines thermal modeling (calculating heat loads from conduction and radiation) with geometric optimization to determine the longest feasible operating time under coupled mass and size constraints.

Model / system. The physical system is a liquid-nitrogen Dewar containing an HTS-SQUID magnetometer. The model uses heat transfer equations to calculate the rate of liquid nitrogen mass loss, which is then integrated over time to predict the hold duration.

Key observables. Hold time (hours), maximum voltage-modulation depth (36.2 $\mu$V), and median noise level (41.5 fT Hz$^{-1/2}$ from 100 to 1000 Hz).

Important parameters / regimes. Maximum model mass ($\le 3.85 ext{ kg}$) and maximum length ($\le 550 ext{ mm}$); liquid nitrogen heat capacity ($L_v$).

Assumptions / limitations. The model simplifies complex heat leak sources (like vapor cooling or joints) into effective parameters, and the G10 architecture is assumed to minimize system-level penalties.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the constrained design space for the Dewar geometry, validate the predicted liquid nitrogen mass depletion against experimental measurements, and characterize the SQUID's voltage-flux response and noise spectrum.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the coupled design problem, develops the thermal model and constraint-tracing workflow, validates the model against experimental mass loss data, and finally presents the SQUID characterization results to confirm system performance.

Abstract

Compact high-temperature superconducting quantum interference device (HTS-SQUID) magnetometers require a liquid-nitrogen Dewar that balances operating duration against instrument-envelope and mass constraints. Here we introduced a constraint-traceable design workflow in which body radius and neck-length fraction were scanned under fixed outer-envelope volume and geometry-based mass limits. The longest-duration feasible grid point lay adjacent to the 3.85 kg and 550 mm constraint intersection, predicting 224 h hold time from a body-only initial fill to a residual liquid depth of 10 mm. A reference Dewar was experimentally monitored for 137.3 h, consuming 1.22 L liquid-nitrogen of its initial inventory. A thermal model calibrated over the first 40 h reproduced the remaining 97.3 h. Device measurements at liquid-nitrogen temperature showed a superconducting transition and Fraunhofer-like junction response, a maximum voltage-modulation depth of 36.2 $μ$V, and a median noise level of 41.5 fT Hz$^{-1/2}$ from 100 to 1000 Hz. These results establish a reproducible method for selecting Dewar geometry under coupled constraints and demonstrate compatibility with long-endurance and low-noise HTS-SQUID magnetometer systems.

Deformation of sine-Gordon two-soliton solutions in $\varphi^4$ kink-antikink configurations

Summary. This work develops an analytical method to approximate kink-antikink configurations in the non-integrable $\varphi^4$ model by deforming known solutions from the integrable sine-Gordon system. By quantifying the error using integrated residuals, the authors demonstrate that their deformed solutions significantly outperform the standard linear superposition method, offering superior initial data for numerical simulations of soliton scattering.

Why it may be interesting. The techniques used—mapping exact solutions from integrable systems to non-integrable ones—are highly relevant for developing physically motivated initial conditions for complex non-linear dynamics simulations in quantum field theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper constructs and analyzes kink-antikink (K$ar{ ext{K}}$) configurations in the non-integrable $\varphi^4$ model by mapping exact solutions from the integrable sine-Gordon (sG) system.

Main result. The deformation method yields two classes of K$ar{ ext{K}}$ configurations whose integrated squared residuals are significantly smaller than the naive additive superposition Ansatz, providing better initial data for numerical studies.

Method. The core method involves a field deformation mapping from the integrable sG system to the non-integrable $\varphi^4$ model, followed by quantitative comparison using local and integrated squared residuals.

Model / system. The study focuses on the $\varphi^4$ scalar field model, governed by a non-linear equation of motion, and compares its solutions to those derived from the integrable sine-Gordon model.

Key observables. Local field equation residuals ($R_A$) and integrated squared residuals ($\mathcal{R}_A$), which quantify the deviation from the $\varphi^4$ equation of motion.

Important parameters / regimes. Initial half-separation ($d$) and velocity ($v$), analyzed particularly in the small separation limit ($d \lesssim 1.5$).

Assumptions / limitations. The resulting mapped fields are mathematically consistent approximations but are not exact solutions to the $\varphi^4$ equation of motion because the target theory is non-integrable.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison of field profiles ($\varphi_{ ext{sup}}$ vs. $\varphi_{ ext{I}}$) and the quantitative comparison of integrated squared residuals ($\mathcal{R}_A$) versus separation ($d$) for various velocities ($v$).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mapping from sG to $\varphi^4$, develops two classes of deformed solutions (Class I and II), and then rigorously compares these analytical approximations against the standard superposition Ansatz using residual analysis to quantify accuracy.

Abstract

In this work, we construct analytical kink-antikink $(K\bar{K})$ configurations in the non-integrable $\varphi^4$ model by mapping exact solutions of the integrable sine-Gordon system via a field deformation. This procedure yields two distinct classes of configurations, parametrized by the initial half-separation and velocity. We compare these profiles with the standard additive superposition Ansatz in terms of vacuum structure and equation-of-motion residuals, deriving explicit closed-form expressions for the corresponding integrated squared residuals. For small separations, the mapped soliton-antisoliton configurations exhibit appreciably smaller residuals than the naive superposition Ansatz, which in turn performs better than the mapped two-soliton configurations. Although the mapped fields are not exact solutions of the $\varphi^4$ equation of motion, they provide mathematically consistent, topologically sound, and physically motivated initial data for numerical studies of kink-antikink scattering and resonance phenomena.
