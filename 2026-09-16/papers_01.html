Highlighted author(s): Johannes Knolle
Authors: Peng Rao, Johannes Knolle
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14228v1

Summary. This work theoretically describes 'vestigial Altermagnetism,' a magnetic order that persists even after the individual magnetic and orbital orders responsible for it have melted due to temperature. By analyzing the phase diagram and calculating the electron spectral function, the authors predict unique, measurable signatures, such as spin- and orbital-dependent quasiparticle broadening, confirming the exotic nature of this emergent state.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed calculation of electronic spectral functions and the prediction of anisotropic quasiparticle damping rates provide concrete, measurable signatures that can be sought in angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) experiments on correlated materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the emergence and properties of 'vestigial Altermagnetism' (AM) in systems where multiple magnetic and orbital orders compete and melt upon heating.

Main result. A distinct vestigial AM phase with $\mathbf{Q}=0$ can arise from the thermal melting of coexisting Antiferromagnetic (AFM) and Orbital Orders (OO), and the transition into this phase can be first or second order.

Method. The analysis employs Landau theory, mean-field approximations, and one-loop calculations to determine phase boundaries and calculate electronic spectral functions.

Model / system. The study uses a minimal two-orbital model on a square lattice, extended in later sections to a three-orbital model, incorporating Hubbard and Hund's couplings.

Key observables. The vestigial AM order parameter ($\phi$), the electron spectral function $A_{\lambda s}(p)$, and the quasiparticle lifetime ($\gamma$).

Important parameters / regimes. Bare susceptibilities ($r(0)_{\text{AFM}}, r(0)_{\text{OO}}$), interaction coupling constants ($\tilde{u}_1, \tilde{u}_2$), and temperature ($T$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on mean-field decoupling of interactions and neglects higher-order fluctuation corrections (e.g., logarithmic terms).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phenomenological phase diagrams showing transition lines, and spectral function plots demonstrating spin- and orbital-dependent quasi-particle damping rates characteristic of the vestigial AM state.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the mean-field description of the vestigial AM phase and its transition order (using Landau theory) to calculating the unique experimental signatures via the electron spectral function using one-loop self-energy corrections.

Abstract

Interaction effects and fluctuations govern the phase diagram of systems with multiple competing phases. In particular, as individual phases are destroyed by increasing temperature, fluctuations can still produce a composite vestigial' order given by a product of individual order parameters. Here, we describe vestigial Altermagnetism (AM) from the thermal melting of antiferromagnetic (AFM) and orbital orders (OO) on the square lattice. The coexistence of AFM and OO can give rise to conventional AM with ordering wave vector $\mathbf{Q}=(π,π)$. We show that a distinctvestigial AM' phase with $\mathbf{Q}=0$ can arise from thermal melting of AFM and OO, and investigate its generic phase diagram. We find that the transition from the high-temperature disordered phase into the vestigial AM phase can be either first or second order, depending on the AFM and OO bare susceptibilities and their interactions. We then investigate the unique experimental signatures by calculating the electron spectral function for a minimal two-orbital model. Despite the fact that the vestigial AM order parameter does not couple to electrons directly, fluctuations dominate the self-energy corrections giving rise to a spin- and orbital-dependent quasi-particle lifetime with the AM symmetry.

Summary. This work establishes rhombohedral multilayer graphene as a tunable platform for realizing nonrelativistic p-wave magnetism using only orbital coupling to an external magnetic field. The authors show that this field-induced magnetism is robust and scales predictably with the number of graphene layers. The findings are further supported by predicting a distinct magnetic Edelstein effect as a measurable transport signature.

Why it may be interesting. The mechanism relies on orbital coupling to an external field to induce unconventional magnetism (p-wave) without needing intrinsic spin-orbit coupling, which is a key concept in designing quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To propose and demonstrate that rhombohedral multilayer graphene (RMG) in an applied in-plane magnetic field acts as a highly tunable platform for nonrelativistic collinear p-wave magnetism.

Main result. The orbital coupling to the in-plane field, combined with interaction-driven layer antiferromagnetism, generates a field-induced nonrelativistic p-wave magnet whose spin splitting scales linearly with the layer number.

Method. The study employs a minimal low-energy effective theory and later extends the analysis using a self-consistent Hartree-Fock solution of the Hubbard model.

Model / system. The physical system is rhombohedral multilayer graphene (RMG). The theoretical framework involves modeling the electronic structure using tight-binding Hamiltonians, projecting to low-energy effective models, and analyzing stability using the Hubbard model.

Key observables. p-wave spin splitting ($\Delta E$), magnetic Edelstein susceptibility ($\chi_{\mu u}$), and layer-resolved contributions ($\chi^{(l)}_{\mu u}$).

Important parameters / regimes. In-plane magnetic field strength ($\mathbf{B}$), layer number ($N_L$), and doping level ($\Delta n$).

Assumptions / limitations. Initial analyses omit the Zeeman coupling; the Hubbard model analysis uses the Hartree-Fock approximation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of RMG stacking, low-energy band dispersions showing layer-number dependence of spin splitting, and the layer-number/probe-depth dependence of the magnetic Edelstein effect.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the low-energy effective theory for the p-wave magnetism (using minimal models) to confirming its robustness using a more realistic Hubbard model, and finally detailing the transport signature via the layer-resolved magnetic Edelstein effect.

Abstract

We propose rhombohedral multilayer graphene in an applied in-plane magnetic field as a highly tunable platform for nonrelativistic collinear $p$-wave magnetism. The orbital coupling to the magnetic field breaks time-reversal symmetry and, together with interaction-driven layer antiferromagnetism, generates an odd-in-momentum spin splitting without relying on spin-orbit coupling. Using a minimal low-energy effective theory, we show that the resulting $p$-wave spin splitting is strongly enhanced with increasing layer number. This enhancement originates from the surface-localized nature of the low-energy states and their layer-dependent orbital coupling to the in-plane field. The Zeeman coupling only weakly perturbs this predominantly orbital-field-induced mechanism. We further estimate stability of the $p$-wave magnetism under hole-doping and distinct transport signature using a more realistic Hubbard model. Our results establish orbital coupling to an external magnetic field as a controllable route to odd-parity spin splitting in collinear magnets.

Summary. This paper presents a novel method to achieve strong, directed light-matter coupling in free space by engineering an atomic antenna from the wavefunction of multiple trapped fermions. By utilizing Pauli blocking, the authors suppress unwanted inelastic decay channels, enabling deterministic, highly directional spontaneous emission from a single atom. This work offers a pathway to realizing high-cooperativity quantum optics without relying on traditional optical cavities.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses fundamental open quantum system problems by using many-body quantum statistics (Pauli blocking) to engineer quantum optical phenomena (directional emission) in a highly controllable, solid-state-like environment (the trap).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving coherent and controlled light-matter interactions in free space, circumventing the need for engineered boundaries like optical cavities.

Main result. The authors propose using quantum statistics (Pauli blocking) on an array of fermions to engineer a single-atom antenna that achieves directed spontaneous emission and high cooperativity in free space.

Method. The analysis uses the Green's function approach and master equations, incorporating Pauli blocking effects derived from filling the lowest vibrational modes of a trap.

Model / system. The system involves an atom trapped in a potential, where the collective behavior of multiple fermions (a Fermi sea) is used to modify the decay channels. The interaction is modeled via the dipole coupling to the free electromagnetic field.

Key observables. Cooperativity (C), directed spontaneous emission rate, and modified dipole-dipole interactions.

Important parameters / regimes. Fermion number (N), Lamb-Dicke parameter ($\eta$), and the ratio of elastic to inelastic decay rates.

Assumptions / limitations. The approach relies on the ability of Pauli blocking to suppress inelastic decay channels, forcing emission into the desired elastic channel.

Figures summary. Figures compare point dipole scattering (isotropic) versus antenna/wavepacket scattering (directed), and illustrate the mechanism of suppressing inelastic scattering via Pauli blocking.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from the general need for free-space light-matter coupling, introduces the single-atom antenna concept, develops the theory using fermionic loading and Pauli blocking, and concludes by discussing extensions like arbitrary spatial emission.

Abstract

Coherent and efficient light-matter interfaces between an atom and a single mode of the electromagnetic field are essential for quantum technologies. Traditionally, these systems employ optical cavities or waveguides to isolate a specific mode of light, but a more recent approach is to engineer antennas from ordered configurations of trapped ultracold atoms that exhibit controllable and directed scattering. In this work, we propose a method to engineer an antenna from the center-of-mass wavefunction of a single atom, which can produce directed emission and thereby exhibit strong coupling to a target mode of light in free space. We predict that the resulting single-atom cooperativity can be comparable to the current state of the art in optical cavity and waveguide QED experiments. Building on this approach, we show that a wavepacket antenna becomes a single-atom mirror in the linear response regime. We study the modified dipole-dipole interactions and band structure of chains of such emitters, which can exhibit sub- and superradiance at spacings much larger than the wavelength of the light. Extending our approach to multi-level atoms, we propose a method to achieve arbitrary spatial scattering from a single atom, including uni-directional spontaneous emission. Finally, we elucidate how our approach can enhance cooperative scattering in conventional arrays of tightly trapped atoms.

Summary. This paper rigorously tests the single-atom model for four-wave mixing in cold atomic ensembles using polarization-resolved photon detection. The authors find excellent agreement for single-photon counts but observe systematic underestimation of coincidence rates for certain polarization channels. This discrepancy strongly suggests that collective atomic interactions, beyond individual level dynamics, are required for a complete theoretical description of the quantum light statistics.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it pushes the boundaries of open quantum systems modeling in AMO physics, specifically addressing when simple single-particle descriptions fail and when collective, many-body interactions (like dipole-dipole coupling) must be incorporated.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study experimentally tests the limits of the single-atom model used to describe photon-pair generation via four-wave mixing (FWM) in cold atomic ensembles.

Main result. While the single-atom model accurately predicts single-photon counts, it consistently underestimates the measured coincidence rates for certain polarization configurations, suggesting that collective phenomena are necessary for a full description.

Method. The authors developed a comprehensive, high-level single-atom model incorporating all relevant hyperfine and Zeeman sublevels, solving the dynamics using the Lindblad master equation, and comparing predictions to polarization-resolved experimental measurements.

Model / system. The system uses a cold-atom source (e.g., Rb in a MOT) undergoing FWM in a diamond configuration. The model is a detailed 32-level description solved via the Lindblad master equation, accounting for complex internal atomic structure.

Key observables. Single-photon counts, coincidence rates ($G^{(2)}$) for various polarization channels (e.g., $G^{(2)}_{HV}$), and population dynamics of Zeeman sublevels.

Important parameters / regimes. Pump laser powers, detunings ($\delta_1, \delta_2$), and the inclusion/exclusion of a re-pump laser field.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation/assumption tested is the validity of the single-atom approximation; the discrepancy points to the necessity of including collective effects.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the minimal diamond FWM configuration and the full 32-level atomic system for Rb, while other figures compare calculated photon counts and coincidence rates against experimental data across varying laser powers.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical problem (FWM in atoms), details the advanced 32-level theoretical model (Hamiltonian, Lindblad equation), presents the experimental measurements (counts and coincidences), and concludes by comparing theory to experiment to identify the breakdown point of the single-atom approximation.

Abstract

The limits of the well-established single-atom model for describing photon-pair generation via four-wave mixing in a diamond configuration in atomic ensembles are experimentally tested. Using a cold-atom source, biphotons are generated and detected through polarization analyzers that resolve the emitted light into horizontal and vertical components in the laboratory frame. The pump lasers driving the first and second excitation transitions are horizontally and vertically polarized, respectively. To predict single-photon counts and coincidence rates in all polarization channels, the first- and second-order correlation functions are calculated directly using a comprehensive model that accounts for all Zeeman sublevels of the relevant hyperfine states. Furthermore, the population dynamics with and without the re-pump laser of the magneto-optical trap are compared, revealing substantial differences in the populations of the Zeeman sublevels. This motivates the inclusion of two additional hyperfine levels and their corresponding Zeeman sublevels in the final model. The resulting density matrix is used to calculate expectation values of the far-field electric-field operators and compare them with experimental measurements over a range of pump-laser powers. Excellent agreement with the measured photon counts is obtained over most of this range. For the coincidence measurements, good agreement is found when the polarization of the photon generated by the first (second) decay is parallel to that of the first (second) pump beam. In contrast, the model consistently underestimates the experimental coincidence rates for the opposite polarization configuration. These findings indicate that effects beyond the internal level dynamics of individual atoms, most notably collective phenomena, are required to fully account for photon coincidences generated by four-wave mixing in atomic ensembles.

Summary. This work investigates how directional dissipation in a Mott insulator can be controlled by separating virtual charge motion from spin coherence. By analyzing the dynamics through a two-stage reduction, the authors show that random return processes induce random Liouvillian skin localization. This establishes a novel link between local return rates and the macroscopic control of quantum transport in constrained open systems.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a detailed theoretical framework for controlling transport in open quantum systems by engineering local return rates, which is highly relevant for understanding dissipation in quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to connect directional dissipation arising from virtual charge motion to controlled microscopic spin-exchange processes in correlated matter.

Main result. Reservoir-conditioned virtual returns generate asymmetric Mott exchange, leading to random Liouvillian skin localization even when the end-to-end bias vanishes.

Method. The study employs a two-stage reduction process on the Hubbard-Lindblad dynamics, separating charge defect elimination from Mott coherence suppression to derive effective population rate equations.

Model / system. The system is a half-filled Hubbard chain with reciprocal hopping, analyzed using a full Hubbard-Lindblad dynamics approach, which is then reduced via timescale separation.

Key observables. Random Liouvillian skin localization, asymmetric spin-exchange rates, finite-density hard-core stationary state, and one-particle activated relaxation scale.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis relies on a strict hierarchy of timescales ($t/\sqrt{D_0} \ll 1$, $\gamma_m/\kappa_0 \ll 1$, etc.) for the reduction to be valid.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation requires an explicit separation of timescales and assumes that the final rate ratios are robust even if source lifetimes are unequal.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates how balanced random returns cause sample-selected accumulation (random localization) compared to fixed-edge accumulation under uniform bias.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by defining the full Hubbard-Lindblad dynamics, applying a two-stage reduction (charge elimination then coherence suppression), deriving the effective rate equations, and finally analyzing the resulting localization properties and stationary states.

Abstract

Directional dissipation can concentrate relaxation modes in space, but connecting this accumulation to controlled microscopic processes in correlated matter requires separating virtual charge motion from spin coherence. We study this connection in a half-filled Hubbard chain with reciprocal hopping and number-conserving, direction-selective returns of virtual doublon--hole defects. Eliminating charge defects and then Mott coherences yields asymmetric spin-exchange rates under an explicit separation of timescales, with full short-chain dynamics supporting the reduction on finite exchange times. Balanced random returns concentrate the stationary population and several low-lying right modes at sample-selected positions rather than a predetermined edge, producing random Liouvillian skin localization despite zero end-to-end logarithmic rate bias. The accumulated rate imbalance determines an exact finite-density hard-core stationary state and a one-particle activated relaxation scale governed by random barriers. This connection between locally calibrated return rates, many-body stationary weights and slow relaxation offers a means of controlling transport in constrained open quantum matter without changing the reciprocal Hamiltonian.

Summary. This research develops a state-dependent diffusion model to describe the optical response of thermal atomic ensembles under intense laser driving. By analyzing the internal-state dynamics, the authors predict an anomalous optical-pumping mechanism that resists saturation. Main problem. The paper addresses deriving experimentally accessible continuum fields from the microscopic, state-dependent internal-state dynamics of a thermal ensemble of strongly driven atoms.

Main result. The model reveals an anomalous optical-pumping pathway, enhanced by hyperfine Paschen-Back splitting, which allows the atomic filter to maintain high transmission ($\approx 97\%$) even at intensities far exceeding saturation levels.

Abstract

We propose a state-dependent diffusion model for strongly driven thermal-atom spectra. Starting from the trajectory-dependent internal-state evolution of individual atoms, we derive a closed spatial equation for the local density-matrix field using a velocity-moment expansion. Measurements of an $^{85}$Rb atomic-filter transmission spectrum agree well with the model up to a maximum Gaussian peak intensity of $1.27\times10^{3}$ W/cm$^2$, approaching six orders of magnitude above the $^{85}$Rb D2-line saturation intensity. Counterintuitively, the model reveals an anomalous optical-pumping pathway in which intense light transfers atoms from nominally dark states into bright states. Hyperfine Paschen--Back splitting selectively enhances this anomalous pathway while suppressing conventional optical pumping, allowing the filter to maintain approximately 97$\%$ transmission at the highest intensity studied. This work provides a framework for controlling strongly driven atomic ensembles and designing saturation-resistant atomic optical devices.

Abstract

The circuit enables interferometry beyond the shot noise limit. Abstract

Abstract

Continuous weak measurements of quantum systems are of great relevance in quantum foundations and applications. They can be achieved by probing the quantum system of interest by repeatedly measuring an auxiliary system weakly coupled to it. Here we study two qubits coupled in different points to a common one-dimensional electromagnetic field and simultaneously monitored in the right- and left-propagating output channels by homodyne detection. Abstract

This text explores the fundamental divide between empirical laboratory data and continuous theoretical physics models, framing the "quantum measurement paradox" as an ontological error, specifically E.T. Jaynes' ``mind projection fallacy'' where abstract mathematical tools like wavefunctions are mistaken for physical reality. While quantum theory successfully predicts ensemble averages, it remains inherently silent on individual, event-by-event detection statistics. To resolve these conceptual mysteries without resorting to ad-hoc explanations like wavefunction collapse, the text introduces Event-By-Event Simulation (EBES). As a modular, discrete-event simulation framework utilizing causally-driven Deterministic Learning Machines (DLMs), EBES bypasses continuous time and pre-specified probability distributions. Instead, it generates discrete data from the bottom up in a strictly localized, cause-and-effect manner. Ultimately, EBES provides a computationally efficient methodology that mirrors actual laboratory observations, modeling phenomena that lie entirely beyond the reach of standard quantum theory.
