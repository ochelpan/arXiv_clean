Superconducting levitation and control of a high-reflectivity micromirror

Authors: Philipp Koller, Jannek J. Hansen, David Walcher, Tomáš Plichta, Rémi Claessen, Martin Žemlička, Philip Schmidt, Rhys G. Povey, Stefan Minniberger, Stefan Putz, Markus Aspelmeyer, Michael Trupke

Summary. This paper presents a method to suspend and control a high-reflectivity micromirror using superconducting magnetic levitation at cryogenic temperatures. By employing active feedback and interferometric readout, the system achieves high sensitivity and stability, overcoming mechanical clamping losses. This establishes a versatile, low-dissipation platform crucial for advanced cavity optomechanics and quantum sensing.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges mechanical engineering, superconducting physics, and quantum optics, providing a highly controllable, low-loss mechanical element ideal for testing quantum ground states or coupling to quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge addressed is the mechanical suspension of high-precision optical elements while minimizing environmental noise and dissipation.

Main result. The authors successfully demonstrated controlled superconducting magnetic levitation of a high-reflectivity micromirror, achieving high sensitivity and establishing a robust platform for cavity optomechanics.

Method. The system uses superconducting anti-Helmholtz traps for levitation, combined with active feedback control (camera and intensity-based) to stabilize all three translational degrees of freedom.

Model / system. The platform involves a microfabricated silicon mirror coated with a dielectric stack, levitated by superconducting microspheres within a magnetic quadrupole field at cryogenic temperatures. The motion is modeled by extending levitated sphere dynamics to a composite object.

Key observables. Displacement sensitivity ($\sim 0.1 ext{ nm}/\sqrt{ ext{Hz}}$), axial trap frequency ($\sim 167 ext{ Hz}$), and cavity finesse ($ ext{F} > 10^4$).

Important parameters / regimes. Micromirror mass ($ ext{30 } \mu ext{g}$), cryogenic temperature ($ ext{3.2 K}$), and the superconducting critical field dependence.

Assumptions / limitations. The measurements are limited by extrinsic heating (leading to quench) and the accuracy of the feedback control schemes.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate fabrication steps, the experimental setup (cryostat, magnetic trap, interferometer), and the dependence of levitation time on trap current and optical probe power.

Paper structure. The paper details the fabrication process, the experimental setup for magnetic levitation, the implementation of multi-stage feedback control for motion stabilization, and the characterization of mechanical resonances and cavity performance.

Abstract

We introduce a method to suspend an optical micromirror, with a total mass of \SI{30}{\micro\gram}, using superconducting magnetic levitation. The micromirror is formed on a silicon membrane, coated with a high-reflectivity dielectric stack and attached to superconducting microspheres. The object is stably levitated inside a magnetic quadrupole field at cryogenic temperatures. Magnetic feedback on the transverse motion is used to stabilize the position of the levitator within the trapping field, allowing to measure the axial displacement of the levitator using optical interferometry. The system reaches a sensitivity of order \SI{100}{\pico\metre/\sqrt{\hertz}} near the axial trap frequency of \SI{167}{Hz}. This approach enables free-standing mirrors with minimal dissipation and tunable oscillation frequencies, offering a platform for precision sensing and quantum cavity optomechanics in the microgram regime.

Entropy spectroscopy of a tunable two-site Hubbard molecule

Summary. This paper reports on an entropy spectroscopy technique applied to a double quantum dot acting as a tunable Hubbard molecule. By measuring the total entropy, the researchers quantitatively track how the thermal population of quantum states changes as interdot tunneling increases. This method successfully distinguishes the contributions of different molecular eigenstates, providing deep insight into the interplay between quantum coherence and thermal fluctuations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a direct, measurable thermodynamic probe (entropy) to characterize the competition between quantum tunneling and interactions in a minimal many-body system, offering insights into the nature of quantum phase transitions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central question is determining which microscopic states remain thermally active when tunneling and interactions compete in Hubbard physics.

Main result. Entropy spectroscopy reveals that as interdot tunneling increases, the total entropy decreases because the energy splitting suppresses the occupation of higher-energy states.

Method. The authors developed an entropy measurement protocol for a double quantum dot, utilizing a specific trajectory ($\delta$-axis) to circumvent measurement limitations with a single charge sensor.

Model / system. The system is a tunable two-site Hubbard molecule realized in a GaAs double quantum dot (DQD). The physics is governed by the competition between interdot tunneling ($t$) and interactions, analyzed via the total entropy ($S$).

Key observables. Total entropy ($S$), Charge Sensor (CS) conductance ($G$), and the number of thermally accessible states ($\Omega$).

Important parameters / regimes. Interdot tunnel coupling ($t$), thermal energy ($k_B T$), and the detuning ($\delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The protocol assumes the system remains in thermal equilibrium with the reservoirs, and the $\delta$-axis trajectory is used to ensure the Maxwell relation depends only on the total DQD occupation.

Figures summary. Figures show the tunability of tunnel coupling, the evolution of entropy across different tunneling regimes, and comparisons between entropy extracted via the proposed $\delta$-axis protocol versus other measurement paths.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the challenge of measuring DQD entropy, develops the $\delta$-axis measurement protocol based on Maxwell relations, and then presents experimental results showing entropy evolution as a function of interdot tunneling strength, confirming theoretical predictions for state occupation.

Abstract

Determining which microscopic states remain thermally active when tunneling and interactions compete is a central question in Hubbard physics. Here we develop an entropy measurement protocol for a double quantum dot, extending the charge-based approach established for a single dot, and measure the total entropy of a tunable two-site Hubbard molecule realized in a GaAs double dot. By shifting both dot levels together at fixed detuning, the entropy is correctly probed with only a single charge sensor. As interdot tunneling increases, the system evolves from two atomic-like dots through hybridized molecular states to a merged single dot. Across this evolution, the entropy decreases as tunnel-induced energy splitting exceeds the thermal energy and suppresses the occupation of higher-energy states. The measurements resolve the diminishing contribution of antibonding states and the changing thermal contributions of hybridized singlet and triplet states, in quantitative agreement with a two-site Hubbard model. By distinguishing states with the same charge configuration but different orbital and spin content, entropy reveals how tunneling and interactions determine the thermally active states of the minimal Hubbard system.

Summary. This paper proposes a novel 'sweep algorithm' to accurately estimate dynamical expectation values in quantum circuits by jointly optimizing approximations for both the quantum state and the observable. By deriving an analytical error decomposition, the method shows significant error reduction compared to existing techniques, making it a powerful tool for simulating quantum dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, numerically superior framework for calculating time-dependent expectation values, which is crucial for simulating quantum dynamics in complex systems where simple state or observable approximations fail.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Dynamically estimating expectation values in quantum systems is challenging because conventional approximation methods optimize the state or observable separately, failing to account for the combined error in the final expectation value.

Main result. The proposed joint state and observable approximation algorithm achieves errors two to three orders of magnitude smaller than variational state compression on 30-qubit random circuits.

Method. The authors introduce a 'sweep algorithm' utilizing coupled loss functions that jointly guide the approximation of the quantum state and the observable, derived from an analytical error decomposition.

Model / system. The work analyzes quantum systems undergoing dynamics, modeled by quantum circuits applied to an initial state, aiming to approximate $ ext{Tr}( ho_0 C^\dagger O C)$.

Key observables. The expectation value $\langle ilde{O} angle$, and error components $R^{(i)}_1$ and $R^{(i)}_2$ derived from error decomposition.

Important parameters / regimes. Bond dimensions ($\chi_ ho, \chi_O$), circuit depth ($L$), and the coupling parameters ($\gamma, \mu$) in the loss functions.

Assumptions / limitations. The optimal form of the joint guidance loss functions is heuristic, and the derived updates generally do not preserve unitarity.

Figures summary. Figure 1 compares error components ($|R^{(i)}_1|$ and $|R^{(i)}_2|$) across barren plateau circuits, showing suppression of one error component relative to another. Figure 3 demonstrates the superior performance of the proposed algorithm versus other methods for 30-qubit circuits.

Paper structure. The paper first identifies the error combination problem, develops the joint loss-guided sweep algorithm, provides an analytical error decomposition ($E^{-b}(i) = R(i)_1 + R(i)_2$), and validates the method using numerical tests on large random circuits.

Abstract

Computing dynamical expectation values typically relies on approximations optimized for the state or observable separately, without accounting for how their errors combine in the final expression.This work explores an alternative approach in which each approximation is guided by both the state and the observable, accounting for how they jointly determine the target expectation value. This idea is implemented through a sweep algorithm with coupled loss functions for forward state and backward observable updates. Exact error relations provide an analytical rationale for how the proposed losses can improve the accuracy. Numerical tests on 30-qubit random circuits show errors two to three orders of magnitude smaller than those of variational state compression at equal bond dimensions. These results motivate further exploration of joint state and observable approximation for dynamical expectation value estimation.

Geometry-Driven Suppression of Fermionic Pairing on a Spherical Surface

Authors: Lorenzo Frigato, Andrea Tononi, Luca Salasnich
Type: theory · Category: quantum gases · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18386v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5

Summary. This paper theoretically examines fermionic pairing in an attractive Fermi gas confined to a spherical surface. It demonstrates that the spherical geometry fundamentally alters the pairing physics, causing pronounced shell effects that suppress superfluidity at weak interactions. The findings highlight geometry as a powerful control knob for quantum matter in ultracold atomic experiments.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete theoretical framework for understanding how geometric confinement (curvature) acts as a tunable parameter to control fundamental quantum phases like superfluidity in ultracold atomic systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how confining a two-component attractive Fermi gas to a spherical surface modifies its ground-state pairing properties compared to the idealized planar limit.

Main result. The geometry introduces strong shell effects, causing the fermionic pairing to be significantly suppressed at weak interactions for specific 'magic' particle numbers, especially in the BCS regime.

Method. The analysis uses functional integration and self-consistent solutions to derive curvature-corrected equations for the pairing gap and chemical potential, utilizing spherical harmonic expansions.

Model / system. The system is a two-component attractive Fermi gas confined to a sphere of radius R. The physics is analyzed across the BCS-BEC crossover, governed by the interaction strength relative to the sphere's curvature.

Key observables. Pairing gap ($\Delta$), chemical potential ($\mu$), and the characteristic shell gap ($\delta_F$).

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio of scattering length to radius ($a/R$), which dictates the crossover regime, and the particle number $N$ relative to magic numbers.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on systematic expansions in $1/R$ and uses mean-field theory for the pairing gap, though it rigorously handles the discrete spectrum effects.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the quantitative convergence of results towards planar limits in the BEC regime and show the distinct, step-like suppression of the pairing gap for closed shells in the BCS regime.

Paper structure. The paper systematically derives the grand canonical potential using functional integration, derives curvature-corrected equations for $\mu$ and $\Delta$ via the Euler-Maclaurin formula, and then analyzes the resulting BCS-BEC crossover, highlighting shell effects.

Abstract

We investigate the ground-state properties of a two-component attractive Fermi gas confined to a spherical surface, demonstrating how curvature and finite-size effects lead to qualitatively different behavior compared to the planar limit. In the strongly attractive regime, the system forms a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) of tightly bound dimers, yielding a gap and chemical potential that approach flat-space results with leading-order geometric corrections. However, as the attraction decreases, the system crosses over into a Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) regime where the Cooper pair size becomes constrained by the sphere radius, producing pronounced deviations from flat-space behavior. We identify strong shell effects at magic particle numbers set by the single-particle spectrum, revealing the geometry-driven suppression of fermionic pairing at weak interactions. Our results highlight a fundamental interplay among geometry, quantum statistics, and interactions, motivating future experiments with shell-trapped ultracold fermions and inspiring geometry-controlled condensed matter devices.

Logarithmic-depth quantum simulation of boson sampling

Authors: Changhun Oh
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.18907v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: interference shaping light 3/5 · Full counting statistics 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This paper demonstrates that the complex process of boson sampling can be simulated using a quantum circuit with only logarithmic depth. The authors achieve this by mathematically decomposing the optical transformation into simpler 'shears' and then mapping these shears onto a qubit circuit using Clifford+T gates. This result places boson sampling within the class of shallow quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, resource-bounded quantum circuit implementation for a canonical problem in quantum optics, placing it firmly within the realm of shallow quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine the quantum resources, specifically the circuit depth, required to simulate the output distribution of boson sampling.

Main result. The boson sampling distribution can be simulated by a Clifford+T circuit with a depth of $O(\log(m/\epsilon))$ and polynomial width.

Method. The technique involves enlarging the optical system to decompose the transformation into six quadratic shears, redistributing modes over submodes, and finally mapping the structure to a qubit circuit using Clifford+T gates.

Model / system. The model is boson sampling, realized by a passive interferometer $U$ acting on $m$ optical modes with $n$ input photons. The simulation is mapped onto a qubit circuit.

Key observables. The Total Variation (TV) error $\lVert eP_U - P_U Vert_{TV} \le \epsilon$, and the final output photon count distribution.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of modes ($m$), the number of input photons ($n$), the desired accuracy ($\epsilon$), and the local occupation cutoff ($s$).

Assumptions / limitations. The simulation relies on truncating local quadratures at a fixed cutoff $s$ and distributing modes over $K$ submodes, introducing controlled approximation errors.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the entire construction pipeline: from the original $m$-mode problem to enlargement to $4m$ modes, distribution over $K$ submodes, and final local truncation/qubit mapping.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first enlarging the interferometer to decompose it into six quadratic shears. Next, it details the mode redistribution over submodes to enable a constant local cutoff. Finally, it maps this structure to a qubit circuit using Clifford+T gates, providing complexity bounds for each step.

Abstract

We show that boson sampling with an arbitrary $m$-mode interferometer and $n\le m$ single-photon inputs can be simulated to inverse-polynomial total-variation error by a logarithmic-depth qubit circuit with polynomially many qubits. The circuit uses Clifford+$T$ gates, arbitrary qubit connectivity, and a single final measurement, and its family is logspace uniform. The key idea is to enlarge the optical system, decompose the resulting transformation into six quadratic shears, and distribute each mode over many submodes. This redistribution permits a fixed local occupation cutoff, after which local basis changes and parallel phase gates give the qubit circuit. Consequently, our result places boson sampling within shallow quantum computation.

Summary. This paper introduces a novel, rigorous functional-theoretic framework for Quantum Electrodynamics in equilibrium systems, termed MaxQEDFT. It achieves this by grounding the theory in the Maxwell equations and coupling them directly to the structure of Density-Functional Theory. The resulting formulation is fully regularized, allowing for a non-perturbative description of light-matter interactions that correctly reproduces macroscopic electrodynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it rigorously merges the mathematical formalism of DFT with the fundamental field theory of electromagnetism (QED), providing a necessary theoretical tool for describing strongly coupled light-matter systems beyond standard approximations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a rigorous functional-theoretic framework for Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) applied to equilibrium systems, aiming to overcome representability issues in standard Density-Functional Theory (DFT). It seeks to provide a fully regularized description of light-matter interactions.

Main result. A Maxwell-regularized functional theory (MaxQEDFT) is proposed that successfully incorporates external sources and energy, allowing for a non-perturbative description where the ultraviolet cutoff can be removed.

Method. The approach reformulates QED by founding the theory on the Maxwell equations and linking them to DFT structure. It employs a Kohn-Sham-like construction within a functional analysis framework defined over coupled potential and field spaces.

Model / system. The system involves light-matter interactions in equilibrium, modeled by coupling the quantum system's internal observables (density, current) to an external classical environment governed by Maxwell's equations. Harmonic confinement is used mathematically to establish a reference state.

Key observables. Charge density ($\sigma$), current ($\mathbf{j}$), electrostatic potential ($\phi$), and vector potential ($\mathbf{a}$); the electromagnetic field energy.

Important parameters / regimes. Plasma frequency ($\omega_p$), confinement strength ($\gamma$), and the coupling constants ($\epsilon_0, \mu_0$).

Assumptions / limitations. The theory assumes the system can be modeled by coupling to a classical environment governed by Maxwell's equations, and it utilizes harmonic confinement for mathematical tractability.

Paper structure. The structure moves from identifying the foundational problem (QED in equilibrium) to establishing the mathematical framework using Hilbert spaces and duality mappings derived from Maxwell's equations. It then details the construction of the regularized functional theory, culminating in the physical interpretation that reproduces macroscopic Maxwell equations.

Abstract

A framework for quantum electrodynamics of equilibrium systems is proposed that takes the Maxwell equations as foundational and links them to the structure of density-functional theory for the quantum system. By switching from purely internal observables like the one-particle density to combined internal and external quantities, the external sources and their energy are taken into account. A fully regularized functional theory emerges that avoids the usual representability problems. With the Kohn-Sham construction that links to an auxiliary uncoupled system, the ultra-violet cutoff can be removed and one arrives at a fully renormalized and non-perturbative light-matter description. Regularized forms of density-functional theory with and without magnetic fields appear as boundary cases, while the emergent physical picture reproduces the macroscopic Maxwell equations.

QEMScore: How Much Does the Measurement Add to Learned Quantum Error Mitigation?

Authors: Yue Zhao, Huayue Gu, Yushun Dong, Xiyang Hu
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17896v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This paper introduces QEMScore, a diagnostic framework to quantify the specific contribution of noisy measurement outcomes to the performance of learned quantum error mitigation techniques. By comparing full models against controls that ignore measurement data, the authors show that while measurement matters, the mitigation gain can stem from other sources, necessitating more nuanced reporting standards in the field.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous diagnostic tools for assessing the true physical utility of complex machine learning models applied to quantum computation, helping researchers distinguish between genuine physical insight and mere correlation with measurement noise.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental question of how much of the performance gain in learned quantum error mitigation (QEM) techniques is attributable to the noisy measurement input versus the circuit structure itself.

Main result. The measurement-only control can account for a significant portion of the mitigator's gain under controlled conditions, and on actual hardware data, measurement inputs do carry predictive gains.

Method. A novel framework, QEMScore, is introduced to compare learned mitigators against capacity-matched controls that are explicitly prevented from reading the measurement outcome, allowing for the quantification of the measurement's contribution.

Model / system. The study uses simulated quantum circuits, including spin-chain families (e.g., transverse-field Ising), and evaluates observables $\langle O angle$ under various noise models and measurement protocols.

Key observables. Error reduction quantified by the measurement-associated gap ($D = C - F$), and the share ($S$) of error reduction attributed to the measurement input.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis is highly dependent on the specific simulated circuit regimes, the noise configuration, and the computational budget used for the learned models.

Assumptions / limitations. The conclusions are strictly conditional on the tested simulator regimes and the specific comparison controls implemented; the measurement contribution is not always the sole source of mitigation gain.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the comparison framework, showing how the full model's performance is decomposed into components related to circuit features and measurement readings. Other figures detail the comparison across various evaluation settings (S0-S6).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the scientific problem by noting the ambiguity in current error mitigation reporting. It then introduces the QEMScore framework, details controlled simulations on spin-chain families, presents quantitative results comparing different control models, and concludes with guidelines for reporting learned mitigator accuracy.

Abstract

How much does the noisy measurement add to learned quantum error mitigation? An accuracy table cannot say, because a model handed circuit structure can score well without reading the measurement at all. QEMScore adds the comparison that can. Each simulated circuit carries an exact ideal answer. The learned mitigator is scored beside a capacity-matched control, a model just as flexible that reads the same circuit description but never the measurement. Each method's measurement spend is accounted and not equalized. We run a controlled campaign on simulated circuits and reanalyze two published learned mitigators, Q-LEAR and QRAFT, from their released hardware data. Three findings stand out. First, under familiar within-family conditions (S0) evaluated across two spin-chain families and three seeds, continuous couplings identify the target, and the control that never reads the measurement matches 87.7 to 100.5 percent of the mitigator's gain over an affine fit to the circuit description. A plain polynomial in the coupling parameters, fitted after the campaign, beats the mitigator on all six evaluations, reflecting the selected learners' capacity. Second, for these selected learners, matching most of the gain is not matching the accuracy: on five of six evaluations the mitigator removes 19.5 to 74.5 percent of the error the capacity-matched control leaves, a learner-specific gap rather than a measurement requirement. Third, on released hardware data where descriptors only partially identify queries, the findings differ: flexible models of the descriptors show negligible mean gain over affine fits in Q-LEAR, and measurement inputs carry predictive gains in both Q-LEAR and QRAFT. These comparisons reflect representation- and protocol-specific behavior rather than an isolated cross-regime difference. A learned mitigator's accuracy should therefore be reported beside such controls.

Abstract

NV magnetometers typically rely on an external bias field to lift the degeneracy of the ground-state spin sublevels, enabling spectral discrimination of different NV orientations and spin transitions. At high accuracy, however, such a bias field systematically introduces errors through thermal, mechanical, and hysteretic drifts, thus hindering the accurate measurement of the magnetic field of interest. Several strategies have been proposed to address this limitation, including optical polarization-based orientation labeling, optical anisotropy, strong coupling to nearby nuclear spins, circular microwave polarization, and tailored pulse sequences. In this work, we introduce an optimization-based pulse-shaping control framework that enables selective and robust manipulation of NV ensembles without relying on a static bias field. Our method achieves both orientation-selective and subspace-selective control through optimal temporal modulation of the driving fields, providing a route to resolving spectral overlaps in degenerate NV systems.

Classical, quantum, and general probabilistic state-discrimination profiles

Authors: Mihály Weiner
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17920v1

Summary. This paper develops a mathematical framework to characterize the boundaries between classical, quantum, and general probabilistic theories using a 'discrimination profile' function. By analyzing specific cases like three-state discrimination, the authors establish strict inequalities that quantify quantum advantages over classical predictions. This work deepens the understanding of operational quantum correlations beyond standard Bell inequalities.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a rigorous mathematical framework for quantifying the nonclassicality of quantum correlations in state discrimination, which is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper characterizes the set of achievable 'discrimination profiles' $F$ for distinguishing a collection of quantum states, comparing classical, quantum, and general probabilistic theories.

Main result. It establishes a hierarchy of profile regions ($C_n \subseteq Q_n \subseteq G_n$) and derives specific bounds, such as $eta_C < eta_Q \le 2/3 < 1 = eta_{GPT}$ for $n=3$.

Method. The authors use polytope theory, linear programming, and geometric analysis (e.g., analyzing the trine profile) to derive necessary and sufficient conditions for a profile to belong to a specific physical theory.

Model / system. The model involves discriminating $n$ quantum states (e.g., qubits) using optimal measurements, formalized through the 'discrimination profile' $F(H)$ for any subcollection $H$.

Key observables. The discrimination profile $F(H)$, and the derived nonclassicality witness $eta = F(123) - F(12) - F(13) - F(23)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of states $n$, the dimension of the Hilbert space (e.g., qubits), and the prior probabilities associated with the states.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on characterizing the regions as polytopes and comparing the results derived from the general probabilistic theory (GPT) against quantum and classical limits.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes, but the analysis focuses on deriving inequalities and comparing the values of $eta$ across different physical regimes.

Paper structure. The paper systematically defines the profile regions, proves convexity, establishes the polytope structure of the classical region, and then derives specific bounds and separations (especially for $n=3$) by comparing the bounds derived from classical, quantum, and general theories.

Abstract

For a collection of states indexed by $[n]={1,\ldots,n}$, let $F(H)$ denote the optimal unnormalized minimum discrimination error for each nonempty subfamily $H\subseteq[n]$. We call $F$ the discrimination profile and characterize exactly which profiles can arise in a general probabilistic theory (GPT). For fixed $n$, the GPT region $\mathfrak{G}_n$ is a bounded rational polytope, giving a hierarchy $\mathfrak{C}_n\subseteq\mathfrak{Q}_n\subseteq\mathfrak{G}_n$ of classical, quantum, and GPT profiles, analogous to the local--quantum--no-signalling hierarchy in Bell theory. For $n=3$ we determine the classical and GPT polytopes explicitly. Within $\mathfrak{G}_3$, the classical region is characterized by $β:=F(123)-F(12)-F(13)-F(23)\le0$, while the GPT maximum is $1$. For qubit triples, the maximal value is attained by the trine, $β_{\mathrm{tr}}=3\sqrt3/2-2$, and we prove the dimension-independent quantum bound $β_{\mathrm Q}\le2/3$, with a slight further improvement. Hence $0=β_{\mathrm C}<3\sqrt3/2-2\leβ_{\mathrm Q}\le2/3<1=β_{\mathrm{GPT}}$. We conjecture $β_{\mathrm Q}=β_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and prove this for arbitrary pure-state triples, together with further supporting evidence. For arbitrary $n$, every classical facet admitting a GPT violation already admits a quantum violation in dimension at most $3$. A four-state qubit example shows that quantum success profiles need not be submodular, unlike for $n=3$. Finally, we exhibit a four-dimensional quantum triple whose discrete profile is classical for all subfamilies but becomes nonclassical when unequal priors are allowed.

Abstract

We present a general method for estimating entropy differences between arbitrary probability distributions using stochastic interpolants. The marginal distributions which bridge the base and target obey a continuity equation, which allows a straightforward calculation of the entropy difference in terms of an inner product of the probability flow velocity and score fields. This formulation has several advantages: (i) no computationally expensive divergence calculations of either field are required, (ii) the score field need not even be learned directly if model transferability is not required, and (iii) on-the-fly estimates are produced nearly for free during training. When tractable base distributions are chosen (e.g. Gaussian chain, ideal gas, etc.), statistical thermodynamic entropies are then immediately recovered. Notably, the analysis relies only on the definition of the Gibbs-Shannon entropy, rather than any particular statistical mechanical ensemble, so that generalized non-equilibrium entropies may be computed. The method is demonstrated on several systems of increasing complexity: (i) a 40-dimensional Gaussian mixture model, (ii) the classical XY model of N spins arranged in one dimension, (iii) the 13-atom Lennard-Jones cluster, and (iv) an active Brownian polymer.

Abstract

Time-dependent coherent control can enhance excitation transport in open quantum networks, but independently controlling every inter-site coupling creates a control space of high dimension and leads to difficult optimization problems. We introduce an edge-ranking strategy based on the control-induced change in the gradient component of the time-integrated quantum probability current, which is obtained via a graph Hodge decomposition. When our strategy is applied to the seven-site Fenna-Matthews-Olson (FMO) model, the six-edge set retains $99.83\%$ of the enhancement achieved by full control, and the four-edge set retains $97.60\%$ while reducing the pulse fluence---used here as a proxy for control effort---by $41.55\%$ relative to full control. Dephasing scans and comparisons with random edge sets and random networks provide numerical support for the relevance and potential broader utility of the ranking. These results show that edge selection guided by the quantum probability current can substantially reduce the control space while preserving high transport performance with lower control effort.
