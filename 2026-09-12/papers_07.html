Phase-Controlled Majorana Zero Modes in Altermagnetic Topological-Insulator Josephson Junctions

Authors: Hao Dong, Xun-Jiang Luo, Xiao-Hong Pan, Xin Liu
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11633v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical work demonstrates how the d-wave altermagnetic order in a topological insulator creates distinct electronic properties on different crystal facets. By coupling this material into a Josephson junction, the authors show that tuning the superconducting phase bias can induce a superconducting mass inversion at the crystal hinges, thereby realizing and spatially controlling Majorana zero modes.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to engineer topological superconductivity and control MZMs using geometric/structural properties (facets) and external biases ($\phi$) is highly relevant for realizing fault-tolerant quantum computing platforms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The research aims to exploit facet-dependent Andreev phase shifts to control topological superconductivity in a Josephson junction formed by an altermagnetic topological insulator.

Main result. The study establishes a mechanism where the altermagnetic order induces distinct phase responses on different crystal facets, leading to a superconducting mass inversion at the facet hinges that hosts Majorana zero modes (MZMs).

Method. The analysis involves deriving low-energy effective Hamiltonians by projecting the full lattice model onto the surface-state subspace and analyzing the resulting Andreev bound-state quantization conditions.

Model / system. The system is a Josephson junction (SC/AMTI/SC) where the AMTI is modeled using a Hamiltonian incorporating d-wave altermagnetic order. The physics is analyzed by examining the Andreev spectra and the resulting superconducting gap structure.

Key observables. Facet-dependent gap closings in Andreev spectra, spatial localization of MZMs, and the sign change of the effective superconducting mass across crystal facets.

Important parameters / regimes. Superconducting phase bias ($\phi$), altermagnetic coupling strength ($t_J$), and the chemical potential ($\mu$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on projecting the full Hamiltonian onto the low-energy surface-state subspace and retaining only the leading low-energy Dirac expansion for the final bound-state expressions.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of anisotropic AMTI surface states showing facet-dependent Dirac-cone shifts, and the Josephson junction setup with phase difference $\phi$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first modeling the AMTI surface states, showing how the altermagnetic term causes momentum shifts on lateral facets but not on top/bottom facets. It then applies this to the Josephson junction, deriving the phase-dependent Andreev spectra and demonstrating that the resulting mass inversion at the hinges hosts MZMs.

Abstract

We exploit facet-dependent Andreev phase shifts to control topological superconductivity with a phase bias in a three-dimensional altermagnetic topological-insulator Josephson junction. In the weak link between two conventional s-wave superconductors, the d-wave altermagnetic order produces facet-dependent momentum shifts of the surface Dirac cones. The resulting net momentum of the states involved in Andreev reflection generates additional propagation phases that differ between facets. Consequently, the facet-resolved Andreev spectra exhibit gap closings at distinct phase biases, giving rise to topological superconducting regimes that host Majorana zero modes(MZMs). We further show that tuning the phase bias controls the spatial localization of these modes. The associated topological superconducting transitions are only weakly affected by moderate variations in the chemical potential, obviating the need for fine-tuning to the Dirac point. Our results establish a platform for realizing and spatially controlling MZMs by tuning the superconducting phase bias in altermagnetic topological-insulator Josephson junctions.

Moiré Topology in Twisted Structures with Noncollinear Spin-Orbit Coupling

Authors: Xilong Xu, Liangtao Peng, Shaffique Adam, Li Yang

Summary. This work proposes a novel platform for realizing topological moiré minibands using noncollinear spin-orbit coupling in centrosymmetric bilayers, moving beyond traditional valley-based models. By modeling twisted $ ext{HgI}_2$ and optimizing the system using machine learning, the authors predict the formation of robust topological phases in $ ext{PbI}_2$. This provides a general, material-designable route for engineering correlated topological insulators.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on engineered topological phases driven by strong spin-orbit coupling and correlated electron behavior makes it highly relevant to condensed matter theory, particularly for understanding emergent quantum states in artificial lattices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To establish a general theoretical route to realizing topological moiré minibands that bypasses the reliance on valley degrees of freedom or hexagonal lattice symmetries.

Main result. The study demonstrates that noncollinear spin-orbit coupling in centrosymmetric bilayers can generate topological moiré minibands, which can be optimized using machine learning to support tunable quantum spin Hall and Chern insulating phases.

Method. The research combines theoretical modeling of moiré Hamiltonians with advanced computational techniques, including the use of a physics-informed machine-learning surrogate for topological classification.

Model / system. The system involves Moiré superlattices formed by twisting type-II SOC bilayers, initially modeled with $ ext{HgI}_2$ and optimized for $ ext{PbI}_2$. The physics is governed by the interplay between interlayer hybridization, noncollinear SOC, and the resulting moiré potential.

Key observables. Topological $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant, localized Berry curvature hotspots, topological flat bands, and the magnitude of the superconducting/insulating gap.

Important parameters / regimes. Strength of SOC ($\lambda_{ ext{SOC}}$), lattice mismatch/twist angle, and the gap threshold (1 meV).

Assumptions / limitations. The mechanism relies on interlayer hybridization opening pseudogaps, and the ML model assumes that SOC strength is the primary determinant of topology.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically summarizes the mechanism from type-II noncollinear-SOC bilayers to topological moiré minibands. Figure 2 illustrates the pseudo-gap opening and localized Berry curvature in the D-2 homobilayer.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the limitations of valley-based moiré systems, proposes the noncollinear SOC route using $ ext{HgI}_2$, develops the moiré Hamiltonian, characterizes the topology using Wilson loops, and finally employs a physics-informed ML surrogate to guide material optimization towards $ ext{PbI}_2$.

Abstract

Moiré superlattices provide a powerful platform for flat bands and correlated topological phases, yet most established examples rely on valley-contrasting Berry curvature in hexagonal lattices. Here, we propose a different route to achieve topological moiré minibands based on noncollinear spin orbit coupling in centrosymmetric type II SOC bilayers. Interlayer hybridization opens local pseudogaps and produces sharply localized Berry curvature, which twisting reconstructs into isolated topological minibands without requiring valley degrees of freedom or hexagonal symmetry. We demonstrate this mechanism in tetragonal Dresselhaus-SOC HgI2, where a Lieb-like moiré potential yields topological flat bands. To improve miniband isolation, we develop a physics-informed machine-learning surrogate that identifies stronger SOC as a key design principle and guides the replacement of Hg by Pb. The resulting PbI2 minibands are narrower and better isolated, supporting correlation-driven magnetism and tunable quantum spin Hall and Chern insulating phases, thereby providing an optimized material realization for experimental exploration.

Thermal Stability of Encapsulated Molecular Structures with Extended OH-Hydrogen-Bond Chains

Authors: Alexander V. Savin
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.10777v1

Summary. This work uses molecular dynamics simulations to study the thermal stability of molecular chains stabilized by hydrogen bonds, particularly those formed by molecules like paracetamol. By encapsulating these chains between layers of hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), the researchers demonstrate a massive enhancement in thermal stability. This suggests a viable pathway for designing next-generation, high-temperature anhydrous proton-exchange membranes.

Why it may be interesting. While the focus is on classical thermal stability, the use of molecular dynamics simulations to model phase transitions (chain dissociation) and high-temperature material properties touches upon non-equilibrium statistical mechanics and energy landscape exploration.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop materials with high proton conductivity at elevated temperatures and in the absence of water, suitable for Proton-Exchange Membranes (PEMs).

Main result. Encapsulation of molecular hydrogen-bond chains within h-BN significantly enhances thermal stability, allowing the creation of anhydrous PEMs operating up to 1140 K.

Method. The study employs coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations to model the thermal stability and dynamics of molecular chains.

Model / system. The system involves linear molecular chains (e.g., paracetamol) forming OH...OH...OH hydrogen-bond chains, adsorbed on a substrate and encapsulated by a hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) sheet. The dynamics are governed by a total Hamiltonian incorporating bond, angle, dihedral, and Lennard-Jones potentials.

Key observables. Dissociation temperatures ($T_1$ and $T_2$), and the specific number of hydrogen bonds ($ ho_{hb}$) as a function of temperature ($T$).

Important parameters / regimes. The simulation uses various force field parameters (e.g., LJ parameters, stiffness coefficients $K_0$) and compares stability regimes (open vs. encapsulated).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations and assumes the applicability of the modeled potentials to describe the physical interactions.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the linear structure of the molecular chains adsorbed and covered by h-BN. Temperature dependence plots (Fig. 14) show the retention of hydrogen bonds under different conditions, with encapsulation providing the highest stability.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the scientific problem (high-temp PEMs), details the physical model (molecular chains + h-BN encapsulation), presents the simulation methodology (coarse-grained MD), and concludes by quantifying the enhanced thermal stability of the encapsulated structures.

Abstract

Using a coarse-grained model, we performed numerical simulations of the dynamics of linear molecular chains adsorbed on a flat substrate (on the surface of an h-BN crystal). It is shown that molecules containing benzene rings and hydroxyl groups in their structure can form stable hydrogen-bond chains OH$\cdots$OH$\cdots$OH. Such chains can be formed by phenol C$_6$H$_5$OH, 4-phenylphenol C$_6$H$_5$--C$_6$H$_4$OH, paracetamol CH$_3$C(O)NHC$_6$H$_4$OH, and 4-hydroxybenzanilide C$_6$H$_5$C(O)NHC$_6$H$_4$OH molecules. The dissociation of these chains occurs at temperatures above $T_1=190$, 240, 300, and 400K, respectively. Coating such molecular systems with a hexagonal boron nitride sheet (their van der Waals encapsulation) significantly enhances their thermal stability. Such encapsulated molecular structures retain hydrogen-bond chains up to temperatures of $T_2=470$, 800, 880, and 1140K, respectively. The simulations allow us to conclude that h-BN-encapsulated chains of these molecules can be used to create anhydrous proton-exchange membranes capable of operating at high temperatures. The most promising are encapsulated chains of paracetamol and 4-hydroxybenzanilide molecules.

A Phase-Field Study of Desiccation Crack Pattern Maturation under Drying-Wetting Cycles

Authors: Hiroki Fukushima, Satoshi Yukawa
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11048v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops and applies a phase-field model to simulate how desiccation cracks mature during repeated drying-wetting cycles. By incorporating crack healing and a 'scar effect' from past damage, the model accurately predicts the relaxation of the crack intersection angle towards 120 degrees. The work demonstrates the power of advanced numerical techniques in modeling complex, history-dependent material failure.

Why it may be interesting. While the physics is classical mechanics, the rigorous application of phase-field modeling, coupled with complex iterative numerical solvers (FEM, Newton's method), provides a detailed case study in advanced computational physics techniques applicable to many non-equilibrium systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the relaxation of the characteristic intersection angle of desiccation cracks from near 90 degrees toward 120 degrees under repeated drying-wetting cycles.

Main result. The model successfully predicts that the angle distribution develops a peak near 120 degrees, and both the angle standard deviation and crack energy decay exponentially with the cycle number.

Method. A phase-field model is employed, solved using a finite element method (FEM) with an alternating iteration scheme to track crack evolution and material damage.

Model / system. The system is a two-dimensional isotropic elastic body (paste) undergoing desiccation. The model incorporates a phase-field damage variable, crack healing, and a 'scar effect' (memory field) that modifies local fracture toughness in subsequent cycles.

Key observables. Crack intersection angle distribution, standard deviation of the intersection angle, and crack energy.

Important parameters / regimes. Shrinkage strain ($\epsilon_{sh}$), degradation function $g(d)$, and parameters controlling the scar effect ($a_G, a_P$).

Assumptions / limitations. The paste is treated as a thin, two-dimensional elastic body, and the simulation relies on iterative numerical solvers (Newton's method) to solve coupled displacement and damage equations.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the analysis involves tracking spatial distributions of damage and calculating intersection angles from the damage field.

Paper structure. The paper details the formulation of the total energy functional, describes the desiccation process incorporating history/scar effects, and outlines the numerical solution procedure using alternating iterations for the displacement and damage fields.

Abstract

The characteristic intersection angle of the desiccation crack relaxes from near \ang{90} toward \ang{120} under repeated drying--wetting cycles. However, the theoretical understanding of this relaxation is insufficient, especially the modeling of the drying--wetting cycles. Here we introduce a phase-field model of desiccation fracture, extending the model proposed in previous studies by adding crack healing and a scar effect left by past cracks. By repeating drying--wetting cycles in a finite element simulation, we find that the angle distribution develops a growing peak near \ang{120} as the cycle number increases, consistent with experiments. The standard deviation of the intersection angle from \ang{120} relaxes exponentially with a characteristic time of about 2.85 cycles. These results are consistent with experiments, except that the characteristic time is slightly smaller than the experimental value. Crack energy dominates the total energy and also relaxes exponentially with nearly the same characteristic cycle as the angle relaxation. This decay is driven mainly by a shortening of the effective crack length rather than a change in effective fracture toughness.

Influence of magnetic fields on the performance of spin-orbit torque magnetic random-access memory

Authors: Jiaxin Liu, Yuqing Zhou, Guoyi Shi, Kaiming Cai
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11397v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This paper addresses performance limitations in SOT-MRAM caused by intrinsic stray magnetic bias fields. The authors propose a novel, non-invasive method using magnetic filling in interconnect vias to compensate for this bias field. This technique significantly reduces switching current asymmetry, making the memory more energy-efficient and scalable for future integration.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on spintronics, the concept of engineering local fields to control switching dynamics is relevant to understanding engineered quantum potentials or controlling interactions in artificial quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. SOT-MRAM suffers from an intrinsic bias field due to stray fields, causing asymmetric critical switching currents and increased energy consumption.

Main result. The authors propose engineering local stray magnetic fields via magnetic filling in VIA channels, reducing the write-current bias ratio from 21.6% to 1.3% and maintaining performance at nanoscale dimensions.

Method. The study uses micromagnetic simulations to model the effect of engineered local stray fields on the magnetization switching dynamics of SOT-MRAM.

Model / system. The physical system is Spin-Orbit Torque Magnetic Random-Access Memory (SOT-MRAM) devices, analyzed using the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation incorporating SOT terms for both PMA and IMA structures.

Key observables. Write-current asymmetry, write-current bias ratio, power consumption, and data-retention stability.

Important parameters / regimes. Bias magnetic field (H_s), critical current densities (J_AP->P, J_P->AP), and device size (down to 10 nm diameter).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that modifying VIA channels with ferromagnetic material can effectively modulate the stray field without altering the sensitive MTJ thin films.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the asymmetry in switching fields and currents due to bias fields, and show the modulation of the bias ratio and current density using the proposed magnetic filling structure.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the problem of bias-induced asymmetry in SOT-MRAM, then proposes the non-invasive BEOL-compatible field engineering technique, and finally quantifies the resulting improvements in switching symmetry and power consumption.

Abstract

Spin-orbit torque magnetic random-access memory (SOT-MRAM) offers high speed, ultrahigh endurance, and compatibility with advanced semiconductor processes, making it a promising candidate for next-generation nonvolatile memory. However, intrinsic bias fields in magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs), originating from reference-layer stray fields and interlayer coupling, cause asymmetric critical switching currents and increased energy consumption. Existing compensation approaches usually introduce additional magnetic layers into the MTJ stack, which increases fabrication complexity and limits wafer-scale integration. Here, we propose a bias-compensation strategy without modifying the MTJ stack by engineering local stray magnetic fields through magnetic filling materials in vertical interconnect access (VIA) channels during the back-end-of-line process. Micromagnetic simulations show that the proposed magnetic filling layer can provide the required auxiliary field for deterministic switching and significantly suppress write-current asymmetry. By optimizing the MTJ position relative to the magnetic filling structure, the write-current bias ratio is reduced from 21.6% in the conventional design to 1.3%. The approach is also applicable to in-plane magnetic anisotropy SOT-MTJs, reducing the bias ratio from 19.8% to -0.2%. Scaling analysis further demonstrates that the compensation effect remains effective when the device size is reduced to 20% of the original dimension (MTJ diameter approximately 10 nm), indicating its potential for high-density SOT-MRAM integration.

Interplay of Kekulé order, flat bands and electron-phonon coupling in graphene

Authors: Dominik Szczȩśniak
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11711v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This work theoretically investigates how Kekulé lattice distortions in graphene simultaneously induce flat electronic bands and enhance electron-phonon coupling. The findings show that the density of states at the Van Hove singularity drives this coupling enhancement, suggesting that the system may enter a nonadiabatic regime where standard approximations fail.

Why it may be interesting. The strong coupling between lattice structure (Kekulé order) and electronic correlations (flat bands, enhanced coupling) provides a concrete platform for studying emergent correlated quantum phases, which is central to many-body physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To provide a unified, microscopic description linking Kekulé ordering, flat band formation, and the resulting enhancement of electron-phonon coupling in graphene.

Main result. The enhancement of electron-phonon coupling is predominantly governed by the increasing density of states at the Van Hove singularity, suggesting potential nonadiabatic effects beyond the conventional Migdal approximation.

Method. Theoretical calculation involving the analysis of the electronic band structure and the application of an effective Hopfield-McMillan formula for electron-phonon coupling.

Model / system. The system is graphene subjected to Kekulé order, modeled by a spinless Hamiltonian incorporating hopping up to third-nearest neighbors, leading to a $\sqrt{3} imes \sqrt{3}$ superlattice distortion.

Key observables. Density of States $N(E)$ at the VHS, global width of the $\pi^*$ band ($W$), and the dimensionless electron-phonon coupling ($\lambda$).

Important parameters / regimes. Kekulé distortion magnitude ($\Delta$), filling fraction ($1/4$), and the ratio governing adiabaticity ($\lambda\Omega_0/W$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis uses an effective Hopfield-McMillan formula and assumes the deformation potential can be approximated by $\alpha$, while also noting the potential breakdown of the adiabatic approximation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the $\sqrt{3} imes \sqrt{3}$ supercell and band folding, showing band renormalization and the evolution of $N(E)$ and $\lambda$ as the distortion parameter $\Delta$ increases.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical problem, models the system using a modified Hamiltonian, calculates key observables like $N(E)$ and $\lambda$ as functions of the distortion, and concludes by discussing the implications for nonadiabatic physics.

Abstract

The Kekulé order in graphene is known to give rise to extended flat bands near the van Hove singularity (VHS) and enhanced electron-phonon coupling. However, the microscopic mechanism underlying the coexistence of these effects remains unclear. Here, it is shown that they jointly emerge as a consequence of the lattice distortion manifested through the differentiation of carbon bonds and associated electronic hoppings. In this picture, the conduction band around the $M$ point decreases almost linearly in width with increasing Kekulé order, while the VHS and dimensionless electron-phonon coupling exhibit approximately quadratic upward responses. The resulting enhancement of the electron-phonon coupling is governed predominantly by the increasing density of states at the VHS, with the effective deformation potential remaining weakly affected by the distortion. As such, the obtained results are consistent with previous related theoretical and experimental studies, while also pointing toward a potential shallowing of the conduction band under the characteristic $1/4$ filling and an increasing relevance of nonadiabatic electron-phonon effects beyond the conventional Migdal approximation. Altogether, these findings highlight the potential of controlled Kekulé ordering for engineering emergent correlated quantum phases in graphene.

Long interfacial internal waves with currents: current profiles with quadratic depth-dependence

Authors: Conor T. Curtin, Rossen I. Ivanov

Summary. This paper develops a theoretical model for internal waves propagating in stratified ocean layers affected by a background shear current, specifically assuming a quadratic current profile. Using advanced fluid dynamics techniques, the authors derive nonlinear evolution equations, such as the Gardner's equation, governing the wave dynamics. The key finding is that the resulting wave behavior is highly sensitive to the parameters of the background current.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on geophysical fluid dynamics, the derivation of integrable nonlinear equations (like Gardner's) from physical constraints and the analysis of their soliton solutions share mathematical techniques with those used in nonlinear wave physics relevant to quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study models the propagation of long interfacial internal waves in a two-layer fluid system that is subjected to a background shear current.

Main result. The analysis derives nonlinear evolution equations, specifically leading to the Gardner's equation, whose solutions (solitons) are shown to be highly sensitive to the parameters defining the background current profile.

Method. The authors employ fluid dynamics principles, utilizing stream functions, complex potentials, and Dirichlet-Neumann Operators (DNO) to derive the governing equations in the long wave asymptotic limit.

Model / system. The system is a two-layer fluid (upper and lower) separated by an interface (thermocline), where the background current profile is assumed to be a quadratic function of depth, mimicking the Equatorial Undercurrent (EUC).

Key observables. Thermocline elevation $\eta(x, t)$, and the associated nonlinear wave solutions (solitons).

Important parameters / regimes. The quadratic coefficients ($\kappa, \gamma, eta$) defining the background current profile, and scaling parameters like amplitude/depth ($\epsilon$) and depth/wavelength ($\delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The current profile is assumed to be quadratic in depth, and the analysis relies on long-wave asymptotic expansions (Boussinesq/KdV scaling).

Figures summary. Figures schematically illustrate the two-dimensional internal wave model, the quadratic flow profile of the EUC, and the general concept of internal waves interacting with a shear current.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the physical model and the quadratic current assumption, through the rigorous mathematical derivation using potential theory and boundary operators, culminating in the derivation and analysis of the resulting nonlinear evolution equations.

Abstract

Internal waves often propagate in the presence of currents. Internal waves and currents are widespread in the world's oceans. The equatorial internal waves and the equatorial undercurrent (EUC) are perhaps the most representative illustration of this phenomenon. The current profile in this study is a quadratic function of depth, which is in agreement with the known results of the EUC. With this assumption the integrable nonlinear Gardner's equation is derived as a model for the wave motion of the thermocline, separating the two layers of slightly different densities. In addition, we derive the generalised Gardner's equation in the case of variable bottom. We demonstrate that the coefficients of the equation are very sensitive with respect to the parameters of the EUC.

Néel-Vector-Dependent Altermagnetic Spin Splitting in the One-Dimensional Limit

Authors: Xin Chen, Duo Wang
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11547v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This work explores how altermagnetism, typically identified by spin-split bands, can hide in one-dimensional magnetic nanoribbons due to geometric confinement. By incorporating Spin-Orbit Coupling (SOC), the authors demonstrate that a robust, SOC-driven spin splitting can reappear, establishing a mechanism for 1D altermagnetism hidden within seemingly conventional antiferromagnetic materials.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter magnetism, the concept of symmetry-protected topological phases and the interplay between lattice confinement and relativistic quantum effects (SOC) is relevant to understanding engineered quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the diagnostic signature of altermagnetism (spin-split bands in nonrelativistic spectra) can disappear under one-dimensional (1D) confinement, and how relativistic spin splitting can reappear despite this nonrelativistic degeneracy.

Main result. The study shows that 1D fully compensated altermagnetic functionality can exist, characterized by spin-degenerate nonrelativistic bands but sizable SOC-induced spin splitting when the Néel vector is oriented along specific crystal directions.

Method. The research uses first-principles Wannier Hamiltonians with spin matrices to model the electronic structure and analyze the effect of symmetry operations on spin splitting.

Model / system. The system studied is fully compensated [110] Ta2TeSeO nanoribbons, modeled using a Lieb-based construction, which is analyzed in the 1D limit.

Key observables. Spin expectation value along the Néel vector ($\sigma \cdot \hat{N}$), SOC-induced spin splitting, and right-moving-mode spin polarization ($P_{ ext{mode},q}$).

Important parameters / regimes. One-dimensional confinement, Néel-vector orientation ($\mathbf{N}$), and the presence/absence of Spin-Orbit Coupling (SOC).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the ribbon is wide enough to retain the parent easy-axis magnetic order, and transport calculations rely on ideal Landauer conductance models.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dimensional reduction process, the spin-degenerate non-SOC bands, and the emergence of spin-split relativistic spectra for various Néel vector orientations, alongside mode polarization plots.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical problem of 1D confinement suppressing the altermagnetic signature, then uses a Lieb-based model to calculate the electronic structure, demonstrating the SOC-induced splitting, and finally analyzes the resulting spin polarization in transport measurements.

Abstract

Altermagnetism is normally identified by nonrelativistic spin-split bands in a compensated collinear magnet. Under one-dimensional confinement, however, this diagnostic can disappear: a boundary-compatible sublattice-exchange operation may leave the only Bloch momentum unchanged, forcing the nonrelativistic spin-up and spin-down spectra to coincide. We show that nonrelativistic spin degeneracy can coexist with relativistic spin splitting in a compensated one-dimensional magnet. In a Lieb-based construction, confinement along the diagonal cancels the projected $d$-wave spin splitting while preserving the real-space sublattice-exchange motif. Spin-orbit coupling (SOC) then locks spin to the lattice, so the Néel-vector orientation selects a magnetic line group that can reveal spin-split bands. Using fully compensated $[110]$ Ta$_2$TeSeO nanoribbons as a prototype, we find spin-degenerate nonrelativistic bands and sizable SOC splitting in the inherited easy-axis domain, $\mathbf N\parallel[100]$ or $[010]$. For sufficiently wide ribbons that retain the parent easy-axis order, this splitting is an equilibrium property. The easy-axis ribbon also supports a right-moving-mode spin polarization of about $35\%$ at finite ideal conductance. Our results establish one-dimensional fully compensated altermagnetic functionality hidden inside apparently conventional antiferromagnetic bands.

Quantum interference between vortex- and impurity-bound states boosts thermoelectricity

Authors: Erik Haatuft, Jacob Linder
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.10687v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

Summary. This theoretical work explores how quantum interference between localized electronic states—specifically those bound by vortices or impurities—can dramatically boost the thermoelectric response in superconductors. Using the BdG framework, the authors demonstrate that the overlap of these defect states breaks particle-hole symmetry, offering a pathway to engineer local thermoelectric energy conversion in superconducting devices.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on local transport properties (Seebeck coefficient) driven by quantum interference in superconducting defects provides a concrete, measurable physical mechanism for controlling electronic properties in superconducting materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how quantum interference between localized bound states induced by vortices or nonmagnetic impurities modifies the local thermoelectric response in superconductors.

Main result. The thermoelectric response is strongly enhanced when defect-induced states overlap and interfere, particularly in the vortex case, suggesting that spatial structure controls local thermoelectricity.

Method. The authors employed a lattice Bogoliubov-de Gennes approach combined with linear-response tunneling theory to calculate the spatially resolved density of states and Seebeck coefficient.

Model / system. The system models $s$- and $d$-wave superconductors on a lattice, incorporating superconducting pairing, hopping terms, and perturbations from vortices (via Peierls substitution) or impurities (via site-dependent chemical potential).

Key observables. Spatially resolved density of states ($ ho(\mathbf{r}, E)$) and the Seebeck coefficient ($S$).

Important parameters / regimes. Vortex/impurity separation distance ($d$), superconducting pairing symmetry ($s$-wave vs. $d$-wave), and the coherence length ($\xi$).

Assumptions / limitations. The calculations assume the linear-response regime and utilize a mean-field approximation for the superconducting state.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the enhancement of the Seebeck coefficient as vortex separation decreases, showing spectral reconstruction and LDOS changes; comparisons are made between vortex and impurity separation effects.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the general suppression of thermoelectricity due to particle-hole symmetry, then analyzes the enhancement mechanism separately for overlapping vortex-bound states and for impurity-induced states, comparing the magnitude and spectral signatures of both effects.

Abstract

Thermoelectric effects in superconductors are generally suppressed by the approximate particle-hole symmetry of the quasiparticle spectrum, but can become pronounced near defects that host particle-hole asymmetric bound states. Here, we investigate how the local thermoelectric response is modified when multiple vortices or nonmagnetic impurities are brought into proximity. Using a lattice Bogolioubov-de Gennes approach combined with linear-response tunneling theory, we calculate the spatially resolved density of states and Seebeck coefficient in $s$- and $d$-wave superconductors. We find that the thermoelectric response can be strongly enhanced when spatially extended defect-induced states overlap. For vortices, this enhancement persists beyond the immediate core regions and originates from interference between vortex-bound states. For impurities, the thermoelectric response exhibits a comparable dependence on impurity separation in the $s$- and $d$-wave cases, although the associated spectral reconstruction is considerably more localized in the $s$-wave superconductor. Our results show that the spatial extent and interference of defect-induced quasiparticle states provide a means of controlling local thermoelectricity in inhomogeneous superconductors, with potential relevance for cryogenic thermoelectric sensing and energy conversion.

Abstract

Copper(I) iodide (CuI) is a wide-bandgap semiconductor crystallizing in the 3D $γ$-phase under ambient conditions; its layered van der Waals bulk phase $β$-CuI is stable only between 643 and 673 K. The two-dimensional (2D) h-CuI form has been obtained via liquid-phase exfoliation of mechanochemically prepared precursors and via encapsulation between graphene sheets, whereas bottom-up growth of 2D h-CuI on open surfaces has not yet been demonstrated. Here, we report a vapor-phase synthesis of h-CuI directly on low-defect, large-area monolayer and within bilayer reduced oxo-graphene (r-oxo-G) at low temperatures. Abstract

The k$\cdot$p method can be applied directly to first-principles energies and momentum matrix elements, but the resulting models converge slowly with the number of bands and are inexact wherever the underlying Hamiltonian is nonlocal. We show that both limitations can be largely removed by renormalizing the eigenvalue spectra of the Hermitian momentum matrices. These spectra are gauge invariant, and are identical for symmetry-related Cartesian components, hence scaling the recurring magnitudes provides a modest set of parameters that preserves degeneracies and crystal symmetry without needing to construct a symmetry-adapted basis. We choose to renormalize the models against reference eigenvalues and band velocities on rays out of a high-symmetry point. For GaP, a 15-band model can reproduce the band structure in the near-gap reference regions within a few meV. For zincblende and wurtzite AlN, similar accuracy requires 30- or 66-band models, which in the case of zincblende is traced to the strong warping in the [110] direction. The scheme readily generalizes to rocksalt PbTe, which includes spin-orbit coupling, and has L-centered valence and conduction band extrema. Further, we demonstrate, for GaP, how compact four-band Hamiltonians can be obtained by downfolding within the same renormalization scheme. Moreover, by changing the fitting regime to encompass the entire Brillouin zone, the scheme can be used to generate full-zone models that reproduce the density of states. The practical utility is illustrated with a 59-band k.p model for GaP, which can be evaluated on meshes far denser than the reference calculation, that in turn can be used both to resolve fine features in the density of states and to compute the hole conductivity at low temperature.

Abstract
