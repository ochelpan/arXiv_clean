Summary. This paper introduces a novel, low-complexity protocol to extract macroscopic work from quantum batteries modeled by nonintegrable many-body systems. It achieves this by using a combination of engineered dissipation and continuous measurement to force the system into a rare, stable 'scar' state. This method bypasses the limitations imposed by the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis, offering a pathway for reliable quantum energy storage.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses non-equilibrium thermodynamics and energy harvesting in complex quantum matter, providing a concrete, hardware-implementable mechanism for energy storage in interacting quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is developing a reliable, local method to extract macroscopic amounts of work from large, strongly interacting quantum systems (quantum batteries) whose bulk eigenstates are useless due to thermalization.

Main result. The authors propose a combined protocol using engineered dissipation and continuous indirect measurement to steer the system into a rare, non-thermal 'scar' state, enabling sustained, local work extraction.

Method. The protocol is derived using quantum stochastic differential equations, combining engineered Lindblad dissipation with measurement backaction to drive the system to a specific, stable steady state.

Model / system. The study focuses on generic nonintegrable many-body systems, exemplified by the spin-1 XY chain Hamiltonian. The key resource is the 'scar' state, which is a specific, non-thermal eigenstate.

Key observables. Local ergotropy (extractable work), average charging time, and the time-integrated measurement current used for scar certification.

Important parameters / regimes. The protocol's performance is analyzed across various coupling regimes and depends on the structure of the underlying algebra (e.g., su(2)).

Assumptions / limitations. The protocol is assumed to work directly on hardware, requiring no simulation or suppression of interactions between subsystems.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the four stages of the process (discharged to work extraction), compare the charging time of the proposed protocol against alternatives, and show the time-integrated measurement current for state certification.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical limitation (ETH bulk vs. local work). It then details the combined protocol (dissipation + measurement) to reach a scar state. Finally, it optimizes the local unitaries to calculate the maximum extractable work from the resulting scar state.

Abstract

Realistic work extraction from many-body quantum batteries must be local. However, only a small fraction of energy eigenstates of a generic many-body system, called scars, can support local extraction. The remaining bulk is useless for the task due to the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis. Here we devise a universal low-complexity protocol that steers any initial state towards exactly one scar---representing a charged state of the battery---from which a macroscopic amount of work can be extracted using local unitary operations. This is achieved by leveraging the nontrivial interplay of engineered dissipation and continuous indirect measurement that, in addition, leads to enhanced stability and charging speed compared to any other strategy using these processes independently. Moreover, the protocol works directly on the hardware level, in that it requires no simulation or suppression of interactions between subsystems. Our construction thereby enables macroscopic charge storage in steady states of generic nonintegrable many-body systems indefinitely, from which a reliable stream of work can be extracted via purely local means.

Summary. This work resolves a major theoretical conflict regarding orbital angular momentum (OAM) relaxation in 2D materials by distinguishing between atom-centered (ACA) and itinerant contributions. Using advanced density-matrix simulations, the authors show that the itinerant component, which accounts for momentum-space texture, dictates the true, much longer orbital lifetimes, proving that ACA models are insufficient for accurate predictions.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit separation and comparison of local (ACA) versus delocalized (itinerant) degrees of freedom in a quantum system is highly relevant to understanding open quantum dynamics and decoherence mechanisms in solid-state devices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the discrepancy between predicted and experimentally observed orbital angular momentum (OAM) relaxation lengths, noting that atom-centered approximations (ACA) severely underestimate the true relaxation dynamics.

Main result. The itinerant contribution to OAM relaxation, which captures momentum-space texture, is crucial and leads to orbital lifetimes significantly longer (by an order of magnitude) than those predicted by ACA models.

Method. The study employs first-principles Lindbladian density-matrix dynamics and a parameterized tight-binding approach to explicitly separate and calculate the contributions of ACA and itinerant OAM components.

Model / system. The research focuses on 2D materials like $ ext{MoS}_2$ (strong SOC) and silicene (weak SOC), analyzing orbital dynamics under electron-phonon scattering and external electric fields.

Key observables. Orbital lifetimes ($ au_{Lz}$), OAM relaxation dynamics, spin-orbital coupling effects, and the ratio of itinerant to ACA relaxation rates.

Important parameters / regimes. Spin-orbit coupling strength (SOC), electric field strength, and the distinction between intravalley and intervalley scattering channels.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the full dynamics must be described by including the itinerant OAM component, moving beyond simplified atom-centered models.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the two distinct timescales of orbital relaxation in $ ext{MoS}_2$, contrast spin/orbital textures for ACA vs. itinerant OAM in silicene, and show the scaling of relaxation rates with momentum scattering rates.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical discrepancy, details the advanced computational methods (FPDMD, TB), applies these methods to $ ext{MoS}_2$ and silicene to characterize the two OAM components, and concludes that the itinerant component governs the long-time dynamics.

Abstract

Orbital angular momentum (OAM) is a promising degree of freedom for low-dissipation transport and magnetization control, yet its relaxation mechanisms remain controversial, with atomcentered approximations (ACA) predicting much shorter OAM diffusion lengths than experiments. We address this discrepancy using first-principles Lindbladian density-matrix dynamics, capturing electron-phonon scattering and itinerant OAM contributions, together with a first-principles parameterized tight-binding approach that separates the ACA and itinerant components. In MoS2,a strong-spin-orbit-coupling (SOC) system, orbital relaxation is multi-timescale, with fast intervalley redistribution followed by slower decay coupled to the spin. In weak-SOC silicene, spin and orbital dynamics decouple; an electric field tunes spin relaxation while leaving orbital lifetimes unchanged. In both materials, ACA orbital lifetimes are at least one order of magnitude shorter than itinerant ones, due to ultrafast precession driven by crystal-field splitting, absent from the itinerant component. These results demonstrate that going beyond atom-centered models is essential for describing orbital relaxation and diffusion.

Summary. This paper proposes a novel technique to autonomously stabilize complex many-body entangled states, such as spin-squeezed and Schrödinger-cat states, in quantum systems. The technique combines periodic Hamiltonian engineering (Floquet drives) with weak measurement to create a programmable dissipative environment. This approach offers a powerful pathway for practical quantum computation by actively counteracting environmental decoherence.

Main problem. Safeguarding many-body entanglement in large quantum systems against decoherence caused by dissipation.

Main result. The authors successfully demonstrated an autonomous protocol to stabilize both spin-squeezed and collective Schrödinger-cat states, even against realistic noise levels.

Method. The method interleaves Floquet Hamiltonian engineering to create non-native coherent interactions with weak measurement to impose a tunable dissipative channel.

Model / system. The system is modeled using a central-spin system in a semiconductor quantum dot, coupling an electron spin ($\vec{S}$) to a nuclear spin ensemble ($\vec{I}$). The dynamics are governed by a stroboscopic evolution mimicking a Lindblad master equation.

Key observables. Squeezing parameter ($\xi_2^R$), Cat infidelity ($1-F$), and the steady-state density matrix ($ ho_{ss}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Weak measurement strength ($\epsilon$), Liouvillian gap ($\Delta$), and the ratio of coupling strengths ($\Omega_+/\Omega_-$).

Assumptions / limitations. The process assumes the ability to separate coherent and dissipative steps in time, and that the auxiliary system decay is fast enough for adiabatic elimination.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dissipative engineering cycle, show Wigner snapshots of stabilized states (squeezed/cat), and plot time evolution of the squeezing parameter and cat infidelity, demonstrating stabilization below the Standard Quantum Limit (SQL).

Paper structure. The paper details the theoretical framework by combining Floquet engineering and weak measurement into a stroboscopic cycle. It then applies this to the quantum dot model to derive effective master equations for specific entangled states, analyzing robustness against various noise sources.

Abstract

Reaching a technological advantage with large quantum systems requires safeguarding their many-body entanglement. Dissipation typically acts to decohere a quantum system via random projective noise but, when judiciously engineered together with coherent interactions, it can funnel the system towards a target entangled state. The native interactions and dissipative channels available to most systems are, however, difficult to combine effectively. Here we propose interleaving Floquet Hamiltonian engineering, which allows the construction of non-native coherent interactions, with weak measurement, which enables a tuneable dissipative channel, to enable programmable and autonomous stabilization of many-body entanglement. We show this analytically and numerically for the central-spin system of a semiconductor quantum dot, for which we construct spin-squeezed and Schrödinger-cat states that are stabilized against realistic levels of dephasing. Our approach is applicable to any system compatible with periodic drives and tuneable measurement strength and could enable novel approaches to practical error correction.

Summary. This work tests the hypothesis that quantum systems near a critical point exhibit scaling laws analogous to those seen in LLMs. By training a transformer on Rydberg atom measurements, the authors found that stable power-law scaling occurs only near criticality. Furthermore, the statistical dependencies of these near-critical quantum measurements closely match those found in natural language, suggesting multi-scale structure is key to robust scaling.

Why it may be interesting. It bridges the gap between machine learning scaling laws and fundamental physics, suggesting that critical phenomena might manifest as universal scaling behaviors in information-theoretic measures, which is highly relevant for understanding complex quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To investigate whether the scaling laws observed in Large Language Models (LLMs) are replicated when training transformers on quantum measurement data from interacting Rydberg atoms.

Main result. The transformer loss exhibits stable power-law scaling near the quantum critical point, and the statistical structure of these near-critical measurements most closely resembles that of natural language corpora.

Method. The study uses autoregressive transformers (RydbergGPT) to model qubit projective measurements and compares the resulting statistical dependencies using entropy-normalized mutual information 'two-point' functions.

Model / system. The physical system is an interacting Rydberg atom array modeled by a Hamiltonian involving interaction terms and controlled by a laser detuning parameter ($\delta$). The model is trained on the resulting qubit projective measurement data.

Key observables. Transformer loss vs. dataset size, and the entropy-normalized, finite sample corrected mutual information 'two-point' function $ ext{b}I(r)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The laser detuning parameter ($\delta$), especially near the critical point ($\delta/\Omega \approx 1.1$), and the effective dataset size ($D_{ ext{eff}}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The scaling behavior is hypothesized to be a property of the model-data pair, and the analysis relies on fitting empirical power-law ansatze to both loss and mutual information.

Figures summary. Figures show representative occupation-basis measurements, data-scaling curves for the loss near and far from criticality, and mutual information profiles comparing Rydberg measurements to natural language corpora.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the quantum model and its critical behavior. It then analyzes the loss scaling laws, showing power-law behavior near criticality. Finally, it compares the statistical structure via mutual information, finding the strongest analogy to language near the critical point.

Abstract

In this work, we study the neural scaling laws of RydbergGPT, an autoregressive transformer model trained on qubit projective measurement data gathered from interacting Rydberg atom arrays. The quantum system is known to exhibit a finite-size remnant of a critical point as the laser detuning parameter is varied. We find that near the critical point the transformer loss as a function of training dataset size is well described by a power-law with a loss floor correction. However, away from criticality the quality of the power-law description is substantially reduced. We then compare the statistical structure of both Rydberg measurements and natural-language corpora using an entropy-normalised, finite sample corrected mutual information "two-point" function. We find that near-critical statistics of the two point functions are closest to those observed in natural-language, whilst other qubit configurations far from the critical point have two-point functions that decay more rapidly. This supports the hypothesis that multi-scale dependence contributes to stable neural scaling, and that scaling behaviour should be viewed as a property of the model-data pair.

Summary. This paper experimentally measures and theoretically analyzes the energy relaxation rates in interacting two-qubit systems on a D-Wave quantum annealer. By relating these rates to entanglement measures like concurrence, the authors demonstrate that single-qubit measurements can provide a practical and quantitatively accurate estimate for relaxation in small interacting quantum circuits.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses open quantum system dynamics in a programmable quantum hardware platform, providing crucial experimental validation for theoretical models of decoherence in superconducting qubits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to bridge the gap between theoretical predictions and experimental measurements of energy relaxation rates in interacting multi-qubit systems implemented on quantum annealers.

Main result. The predicted relaxation rates, derived by calibrating single-qubit measurements, remain within a factor of four of the experimentally measured rates, suggesting single-qubit measurements can practically estimate relaxation in small interacting systems.

Method. The analysis combines experimental measurements of energy relaxation rates with theoretical modeling using the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad master equation.

Model / system. The system is an interacting two-qubit model realized on a D-Wave quantum annealer using rf-SQUID flux qubits. The theory employs the GKSL master equation with independent local sigma_z noise channels.

Key observables. Energy relaxation rate ($\Gamma$), Concurrence ($C$), and the squared transition matrix element ($\mu^{(on)}_k$).

Important parameters / regimes. The energy gap ($\Delta E \approx 1.5 ext{ GHz}$), and the noise model assumes independent local $\sigma_z$-type noise channels.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumptions include the use of the Born-Markov and rotating wave approximations, and the assumption of independent noise channels between qubits.

Figures summary. Figures compare theoretical calculations (e.g., concurrence vs. field strength) with experimental measurements of relaxation rates, often showing power-law scaling relationships between these quantities.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the theoretical framework using the GKSL equation, to developing a prediction method by calibrating single-qubit rates, and finally correlating the measured relaxation rates with entanglement measures like concurrence.

Abstract

In D-Wave quantum annealers, various properties of the ground state have been clarified, whereas the correspondence between theory and experiment for energy relaxation rates in multiqubit excited states remains insufficiently understood. Here, we measured the energy relaxation rates of the first excited states in single-qubit systems and interacting two-qubit systems using a D-Wave quantum annealer. We analyzed the measured relaxation rates using a Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad master equation with independent local $σ^{\,\,z}$-type noise channels. The calculated relaxation rates reproduced the overall trends observed experimentally, supporting the model at a qualitative level. We then used the relaxation rates measured for the uncoupled single-qubit systems to calibrate the local relaxation parameters and predict the relaxation rates of the interacting two-qubit systems. The predicted rates remained within a factor of approximately four of the measured rates. These results show that the relaxation measurements on individual qubits can provide a practical estimate of relaxation in small interacting quantum systems and may help clarify relaxation mechanisms in programmable quantum annealers.

Abstract

Gradiometry provides a versatile alternative to passive environmental shielding in quasi-static magnetometry, effectively suppressing background noise through differential signal extraction. Nevertheless, traditional implementations rely on multi-sensor architectures restricted to spatial gradients, where subtracting signals from independent detectors involves imperfect suppression of common-mode noise and artifacts, limiting their sensitivity. To overcome these limitations, we introduce a quantum control sequence that enables intrinsic temporal and spatial vectorial gradiometry of magnetic fields using a single quantum sensor. Our method provides direct access to first and higher-order derivatives of the magnetic field and extended applicability via auxiliary nuclear spin memory. We showcase this protocol on an ensemble of nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond and combine it with mechanical control to realize high-precision differential sensing. Through detailed numerical simulations, we demonstrate the performance of our scheme in two critical DC magnetometry applications: (i) vector magnetic anomaly detection and (ii) non-invasive gradiometry of neuronal action potentials.

Ultimate Information Rate for Quantum Sensing under Multilevel Relaxation

Summary. This paper establishes the theoretical limit for quantum sensing in multilevel systems subject to relaxation, proving that the ultimate information rate equals the bright state's mean population lifetime. They construct an explicit, simple monitoring protocol that achieves this rate asymptotically, demonstrating that entanglement cannot improve the rate beyond the sum of independent local rates.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a fundamental benchmark for quantum sensing in realistic, dissipative environments, showing how the intrinsic relaxation dynamics dictate the ultimate measurement precision, even with advanced adaptive control.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the ultimate rate at which information can be extracted from a quantum sensor undergoing multilevel relaxation, especially when using unrestricted adaptive control.

Main result. The ultimate information rate is found to be exactly equal to the mean population lifetime of the bright state coupled by the signal. This rate can be achieved asymptotically using a simple rank-one monitoring and feedback protocol.

Method. The authors derive the ultimate bound using the Channel-Extension (CE) bound and then construct an explicit, rank-one monitoring protocol that saturates this bound asymptotically.

Model / system. The system is a multilevel quantum sensor with a ground state and an excited manifold. Sensing involves a weak field coupling the ground state to the excited manifold, while relaxation is governed by a master equation involving multiple decay modes.

Key observables. Ultimate Information Rate ($R$), Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), and the mean population lifetime ($ au_b$).

Important parameters / regimes. The relaxation geometry, characterized by the positive operator $G$, and the coupling strength of the weak signal.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes independent local relaxation for multiple sensors and relies on the existence of an optimal rank-one monitoring strategy.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the physical model, the optimal control protocol involving weak monitoring and feedback, and comparisons between the ultimate bound, free evolution, and the achieved rate for multiple sensors.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the theoretical upper bound (CE bound) to deriving the rate-optimal response, and finally constructing a physical, rank-one monitoring protocol that achieves this bound asymptotically.

Abstract

We determine the ultimate information rate of a relaxing multilevel quantum sensor under unrestricted adaptive control and construct an explicit strategy that attains it. We consider sensing a weak field that couples the ground state to several excited states that decay back to the ground state, a setting that reduces to amplitude-damping sensing for a single excited state. We show that the ultimate information rate is exactly the mean population lifetime of the bright state coupled to the ground state by the signal. Although the sensing dynamics generally involve several decay modes, an explicit rank-one protocol that monitors the ground--bright coherence using fresh meters and classical feedback attains this rate while keeping its finite-time information deficit bounded by a constant. For independently relaxing identical sensors, independent local monitoring attains the sum of their individual optimal rates, so intersensor entanglement and joint quantum error correction cannot increase the asymptotic rate. Consequently, the free population-decay curve provides an operational measure of the optimal sensing performance under arbitrary adaptive control.

Summary. This paper presents a quantum algorithm for simulating the linear advection operator, a fundamental tool in fluid dynamics. It overcomes the non-unitary nature of the required numerical scheme by using Linear Combinations of Unitaries (LCUs) and introduces a novel failure recovery protocol based on the Quantum Fourier Transform. The result demonstrates an exponential speedup per time step while maintaining stability and providing rigorous analysis of the failure probability.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the simulation of non-unitary, dissipative physical processes (like advection) on quantum computers, which is a major challenge beyond simple Hamiltonian evolution. The integration of failure recovery mechanisms makes it highly relevant for near-term quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a quantum algorithm to simulate the linear advection operator, which is crucial for fluid and plasma physics, while ensuring stability near sharp gradients.

Main result. The quantum algorithm achieves an exponential speedup per time step over classical methods by implementing the non-unitary upwind scheme via Linear Combinations of Unitaries (LCUs) and incorporating a QFT-based failure recovery mechanism.

Method. The core methodology involves embedding the non-unitary advection operator into a unitary framework using LCUs, and developing a probabilistic failure recovery scheme based on quantum indefinite integration using the Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT).

Model / system. The model is the one-dimensional linear advection equation ($\partial_t A + c \cdot abla A = 0$) solved on a uniform spatial grid with periodic boundary conditions. The simulation is implemented on quantum hardware, demonstrated on trapped-ion qubits.

Key observables. Waveform propagation (sine/square), failure probability ($P_{ ext{fail}}$), and computational gate complexity ($O(n^2)$ per step).

Important parameters / regimes. CFL number ($f = |c|\Delta t / \Delta x$), system size ($N=2^n$), and the decay exponent $\alpha$ governing convergence.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumptions include the applicability of LCUs to approximate the non-unitary operator and the assumption that the failure state is proportional to the derivative of the true state, enabling recovery.

Figures summary. Figures demonstrate the quantum algorithm matching classical upwind results for both sine and square waveforms under advection, illustrating the stability and accuracy of the scheme.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the physical problem and the need for a quantum approach. It details the use of LCUs to implement the non-unitary upwind scheme, followed by the development and analysis of a probabilistic failure recovery algorithm using QFT. Finally, it analyzes the convergence of the failure probability in the large $N$ limit.

Abstract

The linear advection operator is an ubiquitous building block in fluid and plasma problems. We develop a quantum algorithm for enacting the operator. When the advection velocity is constant in space, our algorithm is exponentially more efficient per time step than classical and avoids spurious oscillations near steep gradients. The algorithm is most cleanly illustrated using the one-dimensional advection equation on a uniform spatial grid with periodic boundary conditions, which can be extended to higher dimensions. The algorithm uses a first-order upwind scheme, which captures discontinuities in the wave envelope but is not unitary. We embed the non-unitary upwind scheme using Linear Combinations of Unitaries (LCUs), and develop an efficient quantum gate decomposition of the upwind unitary, which performs one step of advection using $O(n^2)$ two-qubit gates, where $N=2^n$ is the number of spatial grid points, as opposed to a classical computer which costs $O(N)$. Although LCUs introduces a small bounded probability of failure per time step, we show that the accumulation of failures does not lead to exponential-in-time complexity as one would naively expect. Moreover, when LCUs fails, we develop a probabilistic scheme to recover from the failure state, which avoids a full restart of the simulation. The failure recovery scheme uses quantum Fourier transform (QFT) and effectively achieves quantum indefinite integration of an unknown quantum state. The recovery, which can itself fail, is more efficient than a full restart if the wave envelop is well-resolved to include only low Fourier modes. We emulate our scheme classically and demonstrate small problems on Quantinuum's trapped-ion qubits. Our quantum algorithm provides a subroutine for physics simulations that involve linear advection.

Abstract

The resulting space-time profile separates into frozen, liquid, and gas phases divided by sharp boundaries known as arctic curves. Abstract

We investigate the nonequilibrium evolution of density correlations in a thermal one-dimensional Bose gas following a trap quench. We focus on the Tonks--Girardeau limit and develop an analytical description based on a finite-temperature extension of Quantum Generalized Hydrodynamics. We benchmark our predictions against exact numerics for a quench from a double well to a harmonic trap and for a trap release, and assess their temperature range of validity. Our results capture the dynamical enhancement of correlations during the expansion stages, namely the crossover from thermally suppressed to power-law bulk correlations. For the trap release, the conformal distance associated with fixed bulk points decreases as $1/t$, so the correlations progressively probe a universal regime~$\sim1/x^2$ that is independent of temperature. We thus show that, in the hydrodynamic long-time regime, the bulk density correlations approach their zero-temperature equilibrium form independently of the initial temperature.

Abstract

Quantum sensing uses nonclassical states to improve measurement sensitivity, but the same states that provide metrological gain also decohere more rapidly. This limits the usable interrogation time and, in practice, often precludes improvement in ultimate sensitivity - realized useful quantum advantage has consequently remained rare. Here, we restore persistent quantum advantage by embedding Fock-state enhancement within a quantum heterodyne (Qdyne) protocol, decoupling sensitivity from the decoherence-limited interrogation time τ. Measurements are acquired at short τ where the quantum-enhanced gain is optimal, while precision accumulates with the total measurement time. Demonstrated on the motional mode of a trapped 40Ca+ ion, we observe quantum-enhanced precision that persists to measurement times seven orders of magnitude beyond the dephasing limit, scaling as T^{-3/2} with no indication of saturation. Using the n=3 Fock state, we reach a frequency precision of 0.5uHz relative to an 86MHz carrier, achieving a fractional precision ~6x10^{-15}. This represents a quantum-enhanced gain of 7.1(10) dB over the n=0 state, in agreement with Fisher information predictions. This is the first demonstration of Qdyne beyond solid-state spin-defects. Further, by using the quantum harmonic oscillator to perform frequency mixing, we extend operation beyond 1GHz, two orders of magnitude above the ceiling of pulsed dynamical-decoupling implementations. These results recover quantum advantage at the long timescales required to improve ultimate sensitivity, with direct implications for nanoscale NMR and quantum logic spectroscopy.
