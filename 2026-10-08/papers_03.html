Quantum dynamics of spin systems using quantum-like resources

Authors: Debadrita Saha, Ethan Dickey, William Horvat, Sabre Kais, Gregory D. Scholes
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07308v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper proposes simulating quantum spin dynamics, specifically using the TFI model, by employing 'quantum-like' (QL) resources derived from complex synchronized networks. The authors show how QL-circuits can mimic quantum time evolution, and they introduce graph-level metrics to guide the engineering of physical hardware required for such simulations. This offers a pathway to harness classical network dynamics for quantum simulation.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a novel, potentially resource-efficient, theoretical framework for quantum simulation that bypasses direct quantum hardware requirements by mapping quantum dynamics onto classical network evolution rules.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses simulating quantum dynamics in spin systems by utilizing quantum-like (QL) resources derived from complex synchronized networks.

Main result. It demonstrates that classical resources, structured as QL-circuits, can simulate quantum time evolution for models like the transverse field Ising model, while also providing graph-level metrics to guide physical implementation.

Method. The simulation employs QL-bits, constructed from graphs, and models time evolution using QL-circuits built from QL-gate analogues, often involving Trotterization.

Model / system. The primary model is the transverse field Ising (TFI) model applied to a spin chain. The computational resource is the QL-bit, which is represented by a graph structure derived from synchronized network dynamics.

Key observables. Magnetization, microscopic coupling, phase coherence, and effective inter-block coupling (graph-level quantities).

Important parameters / regimes. The quench parameter $h$ in the TFI Hamiltonian, and the number of QL-bits ($N_{QL}$) which dictates resource scaling.

Assumptions / limitations. The approach assumes that the dynamics can be mapped onto QL-circuits, and it uses approximations like Trotterization for time evolution. It explicitly notes the exponential cost of the Cartesian product construction.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the mapping from a physical spin lattice to a QL-bit network, through time evolution via a QL-circuit, to the final evolved state representation.

Paper structure. The work builds from the concept of QL-bits derived from synchronized networks, applies this framework to simulate the TFI model dynamics, and then analyzes the resulting graph structure using specialized metrics to guide hardware design.

Abstract

Recent publications have introduced the concept of quantum-like (QL) bits, along with their associated QL-states and QL-gate operations, emerging from the dynamics of complex synchronized networks. The present work builds on this framework to present a simulation of quantum dynamics in spin systems. We focus on the transverse field Ising model, a widely studied Hamiltonian for interacting spin systems. We show how circuits, built using the QL analog of quantum gates, can be used to prepare an arbitrary initial wavepacket and simulate the time evolution of the resulting state for extracting physical observables such as magnetization. Beyond the statevector dynamics, we introduce a set of metrics that characterize how QL-circuit operations transform the underlying graph connections. These metrics quantify the microscopic coupling, phase coherence, and effective inter-block coupling generated by the circuit. These graph-level quantities provide constraints and design targets for potential physical implementations, indicating where interconnection resources, phase control, and effective coupling strength must be engineered in a hardware realization. Overall, this study demonstrates how classical resources can be harnessed to simulate quantum systems and we discuss the advantages and limitations.

Summary. This paper introduces QEMFN, a hybrid quantum-classical network that uses parameterized quantum entanglement to fuse vision and language embeddings. It demonstrates that this quantum structure improves retrieval performance over classical methods on standard benchmarks. The work provides a detailed, resource-aware evaluation, confirming the framework's utility without claiming quantum advantage.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous testing of entanglement dynamics (entropy correlation, intervention studies) and the direct comparison of performance across ideal, simulated, and real superconducting hardware provide a detailed resource characterization of quantum machine learning circuits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of multimodal vision-language fusion, proposing to replace classical fusion operators with a structured, trainable quantum entanglement mechanism.

Main result. The proposed QEMFN outperforms classical fusion baselines on image-text retrieval benchmarks (COCO-5k, Flickr30k) under parameter-matched conditions, demonstrating the utility of trainable entanglement as a fusion bias.

Method. It employs a hybrid quantum-classical framework where embeddings are encoded into quantum states, processed through parameterized entangling circuits, and measured to yield a fused representation.

Model / system. The model uses a quantum circuit structure involving visual and textual qubits, connected by trainable single-qubit rotations and paired cross-modal entangling gates. The computation is evaluated across ideal simulation, shot-based estimation, and real superconducting hardware.

Key observables. Image-text retrieval performance (Recall@1, MRR), von Neumann entropy ($S_{norm}$), Meyer-Wallach entangling capability, and gradient variance.

Important parameters / regimes. Matched parameter budgets, number of qubits ($n_q=6$), circuit depth ($L=4$), and noise levels ($p$ for depolarizing channel).

Assumptions / limitations. The work explicitly does not claim quantum computational advantage, as the circuit remains classically simulable at the studied scales.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the QEMFN quantum fusion circuit. Tables compare performance across various classical and quantum fusion methods, and Table XIII details hardware performance across different noise models.

Paper structure. The paper introduces QEMFN, details the quantum encoding and entangling evolution, presents ablation studies isolating the quantum module's contribution, analyzes the correlation between entanglement entropy and performance, and concludes with hardware benchmarking.

Abstract

Multimodal vision-language systems typically fuse image and text embeddings through classical operators such as concatenation, attention, bilinear pooling, or tensor interactions. We propose Quantum Entangled Multimodal Fusion Networks (QEMFN), a hybrid quantum-classical framework that introduces parameterized entanglement as a structured inductive bias for multimodal fusion. Pretrained visual and textual features are projected into compact latent spaces, encoded as angle-parameterized quantum states, processed through intra-modal and paired cross-modal entangling circuits, and measured to produce fused representations for retrieval. Under matched parameter budgets and identical frozen CLIP backbones, QEMFN outperforms classical fusion baselines on COCO-5k and Flickr30k, including multilayer perceptron, tensor fusion, FiLM, cross-attention, compact transformer, and a dequantized paired-topology analogue. An ablation suite isolates the quantum module's contribution from the surrounding classical projections, and quantum-centric analyses report Meyer-Wallach entangling capability, expressibility, gradient variance against barren-plateau bounds, and entropy-performance correlation under controls for training progress alongside an intervention study on the entangling component. QEMFN is executed under shot-based estimation, a noise-modeled fake backend, and a real superconducting device with zero-noise extrapolation. This work does not claim quantum computational advantage; the contribution is the framework together with a controlled empirical and quantum-centric evaluation that positions trainable entanglement as an interpretable, hardware-executable fusion mechanism at scales accessible on contemporary devices.

Summary. This paper applies Quantum Machine Learning (QML) to detect rare identity impersonation fraud in banking data using a one-class classification approach. The authors demonstrate that their quantum model, AQFM, significantly outperforms classical methods in terms of reducing false positives while maintaining high fraud detection recall. The work validates the approach on real hardware (NMR) and shows promise for generalizing to future data cohorts.

Why it may be interesting. The direct application of quantum algorithms (AQFM) to a real-world, highly imbalanced, and time-sensitive industrial problem (fraud detection) provides an empirical test case for quantum advantage in machine learning.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of detecting rare, post-onboarding identity impersonation fraud in digital banking accounts, which presents an extreme few-shot, one-class learning problem.

Main result. The Quantum Machine Learning classifier (AQFM) demonstrated superior performance over classical baselines, achieving high recall and significantly fewer false positives, even when tested on an independent future dataset.

Method. The study evaluates quantum one-class classifiers using Automatic Quantum Feature Mapping (AQFM) against classical methods like Isolation Forest, focusing on performance metrics like recall and precision.

Model / system. The quantum model uses a quantum feature map implemented on a 3-qubit Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) device (SpinQ Triangulum II) for hardware validation, alongside GPU-accelerated simulation.

Key observables. Recall, Precision, F1-score, and the anomaly score $s(\mathbf{x})$ derived from the expectation value of the quantum circuit output.

Important parameters / regimes. The best performance was achieved using a small 2-qubit circuit configuration, suggesting performance is not monotonically related to qubit count.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the legitimate data manifold can be effectively separated from anomalies using quantum feature mapping, and that the one-class paradigm is appropriate for this fraud detection task.

Figures summary. The notes reference comparisons of performance metrics (Recall, Precision, F1) across different qubit counts and between simulation and hardware results.

Paper structure. Abstract

Identity impersonation fraud may emerge only after digital account opening, requiring an early post-onboarding approach based on behavioral and transactional signals observed shortly after activation. Confirmed cases remain rare, creating extreme class imbalance and material operational risk for digital banks. We evaluate quantum machine learning (QML) against classical one-class methods in this setting using a real 2025 cohort of 3,419 digital account openings from Banco de Creditos e Inversiones (BCI), Chile, including 8 confirmed impersonation-fraud cases (0.23% prevalence). The data were provided through an industry-research collaboration between BCI and CoreDevX LABTAV under operational constraints. The best-performing configuration used only 2 qubits and was executed on CoreDevX's SpinQ Triangulum II, a 3-qubit nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) device, enabling hardware validation beyond classical simulation. The quantum one-class classifier based on Automatic Quantum Feature Mapping (AQFM) achieves 88% recall (7 of 8 frauds detected) and 9.21% precision, corresponding to approximately 70% fewer false positives than the best-performing classical baseline at comparable recall. When its 2025-calibrated decision threshold is applied unchanged to the independent 2026 cohort, AQFM detects 20 of 31 frauds (64.5% recall) with 8.81% precision and remains the best-performing model among those evaluated.

Quantum twisting microscopy as a momentum-resolved probe of quantum geometry and sublattice symmetries

Summary. This work introduces the Quantum Twisting Microscope (QTM) as a novel tool to probe the quantum geometry and sublattice symmetries in 2D flat-band materials like twisted bilayer graphene. By using a sublattice-polarized tip, the technique allows for the direct measurement of Pauli matrix expectation values. This capability enables researchers to reconstruct the full quantum geometric tensor, providing deep insights into correlated electronic phases.

Why it may be interesting. The technique provides unprecedented, symmetry-sensitive access to the underlying quantum geometry of correlated phases in moiré materials, which is crucial for understanding correlated electron physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is developing a technique to measure the momentum dependence of the quantum geometric tensor and sublattice symmetries in 2D flat-band systems.

Main result. The authors propose and demonstrate that a Quantum Twisting Microscope (QTM) with a sublattice-polarized tip can directly measure sublattice Pauli matrix expectation values, allowing for the reconstruction of the full quantum geometric tensor.

Method. The method relies on calculating the tunneling current ($I$) using Fermi's golden rule, which is then analyzed by measuring linear combinations of tunneling terms derived from the sublattice-polarized tip Hamiltonian.

Model / system. The study focuses on 2D flat-band systems, particularly graphene-based moiré superlattices like twisted bilayer graphene (TBG). The physics is modeled using Bloch states ($\xi_S(\mathbf{k})$) and analyzed via tunneling between the sample and the tip.

Key observables. Sublattice Pauli matrix expectation values ($\langle \sigma_i angle$), components of the quantum geometric tensor ($Q_{ij}(k)$), and the layer weight ($p_S(k)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Twist angle ($ heta$), bias voltage ($eV$), and the gap induced by the tip polarization ($\Delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the sample and tip have graphene-like symmetries, and approximations are made regarding the integration of momentum sums into integrals.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the QTM setup, the energetic conditions for momentum resolution, and show the near-constant weight of the top layer component ($p_S(k)$) along the scanned path for TBG.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the measurement challenge in 2D flat-band systems to detailing the QTM setup, deriving the current measurement formula, and finally showing how linear combinations of measurements allow for the extraction of sublattice symmetries and the full quantum metric tensor.

Abstract

In two-dimensional (2D) flat-band systems, as engineered, for instance, by stacking and twisting van der Waals materials, the momentum dependence of the Bloch states is a key factor in determining the interaction-induced emergence of different phases. Hence, methods of probing this form of ''quantum geometry'' are crucial to understanding these systems. Yet, extracting the momentum dependence of the underlying quantum geometric tensor from measurements is challenging in 2D. Here, we propose employing a quantum twisting microscope (QTM) with a sublattice-polarized tip to fill this gap. We show how expectation values of sublattice Pauli matrices can be probed across the scanned momentum path. This allows for the direct measurement of crucial symmetries characterizing the different candidate instabilities in graphene-based moiré systems. We furthermore show that the quantum metric component along the scan direction can also be extracted; the full quantum geometric tensor is also accessible, given that more than one sublattice polarization can be used. Our work illustrates the potential and extends the versatility of the QTM approach. If implemented in future experiments, this would provide unprecedented access to the symmetry signatures of the correlated phases and to the underlying quantum geometry in twisted bilayer graphene and related systems.

Analytic construction of dimension-dependent steering witnesses from generalized equiangular measurements

Authors: Adam Rutkowski, Katarzyna Siudzińska
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07314v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper provides a detailed theoretical construction of a steering witness for quantum steering in high-dimensional quantum systems. By analyzing Generalized Equiangular Measurements (GEAMs) and solving complex spectral problems related to operator positivity, the authors derive an explicit inequality that guarantees quantum violation for all prime dimensions $d \geq 5$. This advances the understanding of non-local correlations in quantum information theory.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous mathematical construction of quantum witnesses for steering, particularly for high-dimensional systems, provides fundamental tools for certifying non-local correlations beyond simple entanglement measures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to construct an explicit, dimension-dependent steering witness for quantum steering in every prime dimension $d \geq 5$. This is achieved by analyzing the structure of Generalized Equiangular Measurements (GEAMs).

Main result. The authors successfully construct an explicit steering inequality and a sufficient criterion guaranteeing quantum violation for all prime dimensions $d \geq 5$.

Method. The methodology involves complex spectral analysis of operators associated with the measurements, utilizing the Gershgorin theorem to derive analytic bounds on the measurement parameters. These bounds are then combined to form a valid Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM).

Model / system. The physical scenario is bipartite quantum steering ($ ho_{AB}$), analyzed using Generalized Equiangular Measurements (GEAMs) composed of $d-1$ Mutually Unbiased Measurements (MUMs) and a parameter-dependent family of Hermitian operators $\{E_j( au)\}$.

Key observables. The steering witness violation criterion, $Z(d, t, au) > 1$, and the quantum value $Q(d, t, au)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The prime dimension $d \geq 5$ and the continuous parameter $ au$, which must lie within a derived asymmetric interval to ensure POVM positivity.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation relies on the assumption that the non-central operators reduce to three-dimensional invariant blocks and that the spectral bounds derived from two different basis representations are complementary and accurate.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first defining the problem (steering witness construction), then tackling the technical hurdle of ensuring the measurement operators form a valid POVM via spectral analysis (using Gershgorin bounds), and finally combining these results to yield the explicit, dimension-dependent steering inequality.

Abstract

We construct a dimension-dependent family of generalized equiangular measurements that gives explicit steering witnesses in every prime dimension $d\geq5$. The construction consists of $d-1$ mutually unbiased measurements and an additional normalized family of $2d-1$ Hermitian operators depending on a parameter $τ$. Positivity of the latter family is the main technical problem. We show that the noncentral operators reduce to three-dimensional invariant blocks, while the remaining central operator is exactly solvable in a two-dimensional subspace. Two unitarily equivalent $3\times3$ matrix representations describe the same active spectrum, but yield different Gershgorin enclosures because Gershgorin bounds are basis dependent. Combining the stronger endpoint from each representation gives a fully analytic asymmetric interval for $τ$ that guarantees a valid POVM. Combining this POVM with the $d-1$ MUMs yields an explicit GEAM. Finally, using a maximally entangled realization and the local-hidden-state bound previously derived for generalized equiangular measurements, we obtain an explicit steering inequality and a sufficient criterion guaranteeing quantum violation in every prime dimension $d\geq5$.

Complexity of self-consistent entanglement certification

Authors: Yujie Zhang
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.08757v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical work determines the resource scaling required to certify entanglement in finite quantum experiments. It establishes that the necessary number of local measurements scales polynomially with the inverse of the entanglement resolution, $\gamma^{-(d-1)}$. Furthermore, it quantifies the sample complexity needed, showing a dependence on $\gamma^{-2}$, highlighting the resource cost of verifying quantum correlations.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous, quantitative bounds on the experimental resources needed to verify fundamental quantum properties like entanglement, which is crucial for quantum device characterization and certification protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper quantifies the complexity (in terms of required local measurements or sample size) needed to certify that an experimentally prepared bipartite quantum state is genuinely entangled.

Main result. The optimal effect complexity scales as $\Theta_d(\gamma^{-(d-1)})$, while the required sample complexity for certification with error $\delta$ scales as $\Theta_d(\gamma^{-2} \log(1/\delta))$ when operational identities are known.

Method. The analysis uses polytope theory, relating entanglement certification to the distinguishability between the set of separable states and the set of states consistent with local measurements (NCOM-realizable statistics).

Model / system. The study focuses on bipartite quantum states $ ho_{AB}$ in a Bell circuit setting, analyzed using local multi-measurements performed by Alice and Bob.

Key observables. Entanglement resolution ($\gamma$), effect complexity (number of local effects), and trace-distance entanglement ($E_{tr}( ho_{AB})$).

Important parameters / regimes. Local dimension $d$, entanglement resolution $\gamma$, and error probability $\delta$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis distinguishes between the case where operational identities are known versus the fully self-consistent case where they must be inferred from the same data.

Figures summary. Not specified.

Paper structure. The paper develops by establishing bounds on the entanglement resolution ($\gamma$) using covering arguments, relating it to the covering infidelity ($\epsilon$), and then deriving the scaling laws for both the required number of measurements (effect complexity) and the number of samples (sample complexity).

Abstract

In recent work Phys. Rev. X 16, 031057 (2026), we proposed a self-consistent approach to entanglement certification based on generalized noncontextuality. It requires no prior characterization of the measurement devices and, given access to all local measurements, can certify every entangled state in a Bell circuit. Here, we study the complexity of this protocol in a finite experiment. At fixed local dimension $d$, we show that the optimal number of distinct local effects needed to reach a trace-distance \textit{entanglement resolution} $γ$ is $Θ_d(γ^{-(d-1)})$. This scaling is necessary even when the measurements are tailored to the target state. Independent Haar-random projective measurements instead require $Θ_d(γ^{-(d-1)}\log(1/γ))$ effects. Finally, we show that, once the exact operational identities are known, $Θ_d(γ^{-2}\log(1/δ))$ copies of the target state are necessary and sufficient for certification with error probability at most $δ$. Determining the optimal sample complexity when those identities must instead be inferred from the same finite target-state data remains open.

Connectivity Controls Variational Accessibility in Symmetry-Preserving Quantum Circuits

Authors: Sahinur Reja
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07173v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work investigates how the connectivity of quantum circuit generators limits the variational space available to VQE algorithms for models like the Hubbard and XXZ Hamiltonians. The authors prove that simply increasing circuit depth is insufficient; instead, adding specific, minimal long-range or correlated gates is necessary to reconstruct the full symmetry-preserving Lie algebra. This establishes generator connectivity as the key determinant of variational accessibility in quantum simulation.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, actionable principle for designing quantum circuits for condensed matter simulations, suggesting that algebraic structure (connectivity) is a more fundamental resource than simple circuit depth.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of designing expressive quantum circuits for the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) when simulating interacting many-body systems.

Main result. The variational accessibility of the circuit is fundamentally controlled by the generator connectivity, not just the circuit depth; minimal additions of specific symmetry-preserving generators can reconstruct the full dynamical Lie algebra, leading to high VQE fidelity.

Method. The authors analyze the Dynamical Lie Algebra (DLA) generated by ansatz operators and correlate its dimension and the resulting tangent-space rank ($r_T$) with VQE performance metrics like ground-state fidelity and energy error.

Model / system. The study focuses on spin-1/2 XXZ and Hubbard models, which are canonical models for strongly correlated electrons, studied within fixed symmetry sectors.

Key observables. Variational accessibility (measured by $r_T$ and Hamiltonian alignment $A_H$), ground-state fidelity ($F$), and energy error ($\Delta E$).

Important parameters / regimes. Interaction strengths ($J_z$ for XXZ, $U/t$ for Hubbard), and the type of generator connectivity (Nearest-Neighbor vs. Next-to-Nearest-Neighbor or correlated double-excitation).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is performed within fixed, conserved symmetry sectors of the Hamiltonians.

Figures summary. Figures compare VQE performance metrics ($r_T$, $A_H$, $\Delta E$, $F$) as a function of circuit depth and system parameters, demonstrating the dramatic improvement upon adding specific long-range or correlated gates.

Paper structure. The paper establishes a link between connectivity and accessibility by comparing NN-only circuits to minimally augmented circuits (NN+1 or NN+d) for both the XXZ and Hubbard models, showing algebraic reconstruction leads to physical performance gains.

Abstract

Designing expressive quantum circuits for the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) is a central challenge in simulating interacting many-body systems. Even symmetry-preserving (SP) circuits need not access the full many-body symmetry sector. We demonstrate this explicitly for the spin-$1/2$ XXZ and Hubbard models, where Hamiltonian-inspired nearest-neighbor (NN) SP circuits generate restricted dynamical Lie algebras (DLAs). While these restricted manifolds can accurately represent the ground state near the free-fermion limits, $J_z=0$ for XXZ and $U=0$ for Hubbard, interactions expose inaccessible many-body directions and severely limit VQE performance even at large circuit depth. Remarkably, a minimal modification of the generator connectivity can reconstruct the full orthogonal DLA within all relevant symmetry sectors studied in this work. For XXZ model, the addition of {\it only} one SP next-to-NN single-excitation connection triggers this dramatic reconstruction, whereas the Hubbard case requires a SP correlated double-excitation;--showing that the relevant connectivity can extend from the spatial lattice to many-body configuration space. This reconstruction enlarges the tangent space and accessibility of the energy-lowering Hamiltonian direction, leading to near-unit ground-state fidelity in VQE simulations. Our results establish a direct link between generator connectivity and variational accessibility and suggest a broader circuit-design principle: identify minimal symmetry-preserving generators that enlarge the accessible many-body space rather than increasing circuit depth alone. This perspective may have broader relevance for quantum many-body simulation at the interface of condensed-matter physics and quantum computing.

Emergent Dimensionality in Diluted Power-Law Quantum Walks

Summary. This work analyzes quantum walks on disordered lattices with long-range hopping to understand the dimensional crossover from 1D to 2D. The key finding is that this crossover is not arbitrary but is dictated by an intrinsic transverse eigenstate scale ($\ell_e$). This establishes a fundamental link between the transport dynamics and the underlying quantum structure of the disordered system.

Why it may be interesting. The direct connection found between the transport-derived scale ($\ell_e$) and the intrinsic eigenstate structure ($ar{\xi}$) provides a powerful, non-perturbative understanding of how dimensionality emerges in complex, disordered quantum systems, which is highly relevant to quantum simulators.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the dimensionality of quantum transport emerges in systems with long-range, power-law hopping interactions when disorder is present.

Main result. The crossover from 1D to effective 2D behavior is governed by a characteristic transverse scale ($\ell_e$), which is microscopically determined by the transverse extent of the system's eigenstates.

Method. The study uses numerical analysis of the finite-size localization crossover filling ($ ho_c$) via exponential fitting, complemented by independent spectral analysis of eigenstate spatial extents.

Model / system. The model consists of single-excitation quantum walks on arrays of $\ell$ coupled chains of length $L$, featuring hopping governed by a power-law decay $J_{ij} \propto |r-r'|^{-\alpha}$ and incorporating dilution-induced disorder.

Key observables. Longitudinal spreading width ($\eta(t)$), finite-size localization crossover filling ($ ho_c$), and the characteristic transverse scale ($\ell_e$).

Important parameters / regimes. Hopping exponent ($\alpha$), transverse width ($\ell$), system length ($L$), and disorder filling ($ ho$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes open boundary conditions and averages over many disorder realizations; approximations are used to relate the crossover filling to the eigenstate structure.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the time evolution of spreading ($\eta(t)$) across different $\ell$ and $\alpha$, and compare the derived crossover scale ($\ell_e$) against the calculated transverse eigenstate extent ($ar{\xi}$).

Paper structure. The paper first analyzes the longitudinal spreading and identifies the crossover filling ($ ho_c$) using finite-size scaling. It then derives the characteristic scale $\ell_e$ from this fit and independently verifies this scale by comparing it to the transverse extent of the relevant eigenstates, establishing the microscopic link.

Abstract

We investigate single-excitation quantum walks across the one-to-two-dimensional crossover in diluted power-law hopping models. Using arrays of $\ell$ coupled chains of length $L$, we identify the finite-size localization crossover from the longitudinal spreading of an initially localized excitation. The crossover filling is well described by an exponential approach to its two-dimensional value with increasing $\ell$, defining a characteristic transverse scale $\ell_e$, and effectively two-dimensional behavior emerges already for $\ell\ll L$. An independent spectral analysis shows that $\ell_e$ is set by the transverse extent of the eigenstates participating in the dynamics, providing a microscopic interpretation of the dimensional crossover. Our results reveal an intrinsic eigenstate scale governing emergent dimensionality in diluted systems with power-law hopping, a regime directly relevant to recently developed arrays of polar molecules, Rydberg atoms, and other quantum simulators with long-range interactions.

Local-moment magnets on all close-packed lattices are equivalent

Authors: Sam Studdy, Alwyn Jose Raja, R. Ganesh
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.07158v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work demonstrates a hidden symmetry proving that the classical ground state energy and degeneracy are identical for local-moment magnets on all close-packed lattices. However, the authors show that while the classical physics is equivalent, quantum fluctuations, such as zero-point energy, are structure-dependent, suggesting that the true low-temperature order can distinguish between these seemingly equivalent lattices.

Why it may be interesting. The finding that classical symmetries break down when considering quantum fluctuations (zero-point energy) is a key concept in understanding order-by-disorder selection in frustrated quantum magnets, which is highly relevant to AMO physics and quantum magnetism.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether local-moment magnets on different close-packed lattices are physically equivalent regarding their classical ground state.

Main result. The classical ground state energy and degeneracy are identical across all close-packed lattices, but fluctuations (zero-point energy and free energy) are structure-dependent, indicating a breakdown of symmetry beyond the classical limit.

Method. The analysis uses the Luttinger-Tisza approach to determine classical ground states and compares the resulting energy landscapes and degeneracy spaces across different lattice geometries.

Model / system. The system is a local-moment magnet described by a Heisenberg antiferromagnet Hamiltonian restricted to nearest ($J_1$) and next-nearest ($J_2$) neighbor couplings on any close-packed lattice (e.g., fcc, hcp).

Key observables. Ground state energy ($E_{g.s.}$), ground state degeneracy, zero-point energy ($O(S)$ correction), and free energy minimum.

Important parameters / regimes. Coupling ratios ($J_1/J_2$), lattice geometry (fcc, hcp, etc.), and the spin magnitude $S$.

Assumptions / limitations. The equivalence is proven for the classical limit (Heisenberg model) and extends to XY and Ising models, but fluctuations are not related by the symmetry.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the geometry of close-packing, construct a unified phase diagram based on $J_1$ and $J_2$, and compare the location of LT minima and the calculated zero-point energy across different structures.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the hidden symmetry relating classical ground states via the Luttinger-Tisza method, constructing a unified phase diagram. It then analyzes the degeneracy and structure of the ground states in different phases, concluding by contrasting the robust classical equivalence with the structure-dependent nature of quantum fluctuations.

Abstract

The Heisenberg antiferromagnet on the fcc lattice is a canonical example of frustration. The fcc lattice is but one of an infinite number of close-packed structures, all of which have the same frustration-inducing local geometry. In this letter, we demonstrate a hidden symmetry that relates classical magnets on all close-packed lattices. This symmetry derives from a similar property in tight binding models that may explain the disordered low-temperature structures of lithium and sodium metals. In magnets, the symmetry holds when couplings are restricted to nearest ($J_1$) and next-nearest neighbours ($J_2$). It guarantees that all close-packed lattices are equivalent at the Luttinger-Tisza level. For any given values of $J_1$ and $J_2$, they have the same ground-state energy. Their ground state momenta are identical when projected onto the stacking plane. We construct a unified phase diagram as a function of $J_1$ and $J_2$ couplings for Heisenberg, XY and Ising magnets on any close-packed lattice. In each phase, the ground-state degeneracy is the same on all close-packed lattices, even after accounting for multi-Q spirals. Summary. This paper proves a separation between the resources needed by quantum versus classical models to simulate certain stochastic processes. Using a spin-one-half quantum memory, the authors show that enforcing a forbidden event time or generating specific decay patterns requires exponentially more states classically than quantum mechanically. This highlights the computational advantage of quantum memory in describing complex physical dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, physical realization (collective spin register) to demonstrate a resource separation, which is a core theme in quantum information theory, particularly concerning simulation complexity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental separation between the resources required by quantum memory versus classical memory to generate specific stochastic processes.

Main result. It demonstrates that generating a process with a forbidden interevent time requires an unbounded number of states in any classical Hidden Markov Model, while the quantum resource only requires a single relative phase shift.

Method. The analysis uses the framework of Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) and compares the Hankel rank of the process matrix (quantum side) against the required state count (classical side).

Model / system. The system is a quantum memory implemented in a spin-one-half register driven only by total-spin operators, analogous to an atomic ensemble or spin-squeezed sample.

Key observables. Interevent time distribution $d_n$, the number of required classical HMM states, and the number of decay channels generated by $K$ amplitudes.

Important parameters / regimes. The forbidden time $n^*$, the number of amplitudes $K$, and the relative phase $\pi$.

Assumptions / limitations. The classical cost comparison is restricted to finite-dimensional HMMs, and the quantum memory is driven only by collective operations.

Paper structure. The paper first analyzes the constraint imposed by a forbidden interevent time, establishing a lower bound on classical states. It then examines the decay channels generated by $K$ amplitudes, proving a separation between the quantum cost (related to $K$) and the classical cost ($K(K+1)/2$).

Abstract

A spin one-half driven only by total-spin operators generates a renewal process in which one interevent time $n^\ast$ is forbidden while all others occur. No classical hidden Markov model reproduces it with fewer than $1+\sqrt{n^\ast}$ states, an unbounded separation whose only resource is one relative phase of $π$. With $K$ amplitudes the same interference yields $K(K{+}1)/2$ decay channels, and for positive weights and generic rates the classical cost is exactly $K(K{+}1)/2$ states. Both are realized by a depth-three circuit whose parameter count does not grow with the register.

Summary. This work develops and applies a ghost rotationally invariant slave-boson (gRISB) theory to study the two-dimensional Hubbard model. By using a Gaussian-fluctuation formalism, the authors calculate response functions directly on the real-frequency axis, significantly improving upon existing approximations. The results successfully capture key physical excitations and track the magnetic phase boundary in agreement with advanced methods like DMFT.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter, the rigorous development of a conserving approximation (CQPA) and the ability to calculate dynamics directly on the real-frequency axis are highly valuable theoretical tools applicable to open quantum systems and many-body dynamics simulations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of accurately computing response functions (spin and charge susceptibilities) in strongly correlated electron systems, particularly capturing the real-frequency spectral structure relevant to inelastic scattering experiments.

Main result. The proposed ghost rotationally invariant slave-boson (gRISB) formalism significantly improves upon standard approximations (RISB, RPA) by tracking the magnetic phase boundary and resolving key excitations like paramagnons and sound modes.

Method. It employs a finite-temperature Gaussian-fluctuation formalism applied to the slave-boson theory, allowing direct calculation of susceptibilities on the real-frequency axis, thus bypassing problematic numerical continuation techniques.

Model / system. The study focuses on the two-dimensional Hubbard model on a square lattice, described by the standard Hubbard Hamiltonian, and utilizes slave-boson decomposition techniques.

Key observables. Static and dynamical spin and charge susceptibilities ($\chi_{sp}, \chi_{ch}$), magnetic phase boundary, paramagnon excitations, and low-energy sound modes.

Important parameters / regimes. Hubbard interaction strength ($U$), electron filling ($n$), and temperature ($eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes the validity of the gRISB theory and introduces a Conserving Quasiparticle Approximation (CQPA) to enforce physical constraints, though discrepancies with DMFT are noted due to finite auxiliary-bath truncation.

Figures summary. Figures compare the magnetic phase boundary across various theories (gRISB vs. DMFT); other figures show real-frequency spectra of spin and charge susceptibilities, comparing gRISB results against RISB and embedding formalisms.

Paper structure. The paper develops the gRISB formalism using Gaussian fluctuations, introduces the CQPA to enforce conservation laws, and validates the results by comparing static and dynamic susceptibilities against established methods like DMFT and field-derivative calculations.

Abstract

We propose a finite-temperature Gaussian-fluctuation formalism for response functions within ghost rotationally invariant slave-boson (gRISB) theory and apply it to the two-dimensional Hubbard model. The gRISB magnetic phase boundary and static spin and charge susceptibilities capture the trends of dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT), significantly improving upon standard RISB and the random phase approximation (RPA). At the RISB level, our formalism recovers the Kotliar-Ruckenstein response. The dynamical susceptibilities resolve paramagnons, low-energy sound modes, and high-energy Hubbard excitations. Because they are evaluated directly on the real-frequency axis, the results avoid Matsubara truncation, stochastic sampling, and numerical analytic continuation. We also introduce a conserving quasiparticle approximation that expresses the response in terms of dressed Green's functions and vertex corrections.
