Completing or Refusing Low-Dimensional Records of Structured Quantum Circuits: Measurement Loss, Compression Loss, and Hardware Drift

Authors: Gunhee Cho, Juhee Lee
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01303v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · QC/QI experiment 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work develops a comprehensive framework to audit low-dimensional records summarizing quantum circuits, quantifying information loss due to measurement or compression. It merges quantum information theory with algebraic geometry to create an executable 'approve/refuse/defer' decision protocol. This is vital for building reliable quantum hardware by certifying that simplified measurements retain sufficient fidelity to the true quantum process.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a mathematically rigorous, general framework for quantifying and certifying the information loss when abstracting complex quantum processes into simplified, hardware-friendly metrics, which is crucial for scaling quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of determining if a low-dimensional statistical record of a structured quantum circuit accurately preserves the full quantum information, accounting for measurement loss, compression loss, and hardware drift.

Main result. The authors establish a rigorous, executable 'completion-or-refusal' procedure based on algebraic criteria, allowing for finite-sample decisions (Approve, Refuse, or Defer) regarding the fidelity of the low-dimensional record.

Method. The methodology combines advanced quantum information metrics (Quantum Fisher Information, Hellinger residual) with tools from algebraic geometry (integral closures, Rees valuations) to create a multi-stage audit framework.

Model / system. The system involves structured quantum circuits implemented on quantum hardware, whose outcomes are summarized by low-dimensional records. The analysis models the process through stages: initial state, circuit evolution, measurement, and final record compression.

Key observables. Hellinger residual, simultaneous confidence bounds, quantum Fisher information metrics ($g_{SLD}, g_{Q,e}, g_{T,e,a}$), and the decision outcomes: Approve, Refuse, Defer.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit parameters ($ heta$), noise models (avoided assumption), finite-sample size, and the declared loss budget.

Assumptions / limitations. The method aims to avoid assuming a specific parametric hardware-noise model or a low-dimensional statistical family, though the final experimental validation is limited to specific backends and circuits.

Figures summary. Figure 1 summarizes two analysis routes: a default finite-data audit and an optional algebraic audit using response ideals, explicitly separating the two decision paths.

Paper structure. The paper develops a theoretical framework connecting all-arc criteria (algebraic/global) to executable, finite-sample decision procedures (local/experimental), culminating in hardware tests on specific circuit families.

Abstract

Structured quantum circuits are often summarized by low-dimensional records. Records lose control-dependent information when they merge outcomes whose probabilities respond differently to circuit parameters. We assess this loss without a parametric hardware-noise model or low-dimensional statistical family. Quantum Fisher information bounds premeasurement sensitivity; Fisher metrics of measurements and records describe device-attainable sensitivity. A Hellinger residual measures the response removed by a record, while its local quadratic term is the conditional covariance of the full-outcome score. Simultaneous confidence bounds support approval, refusal, or deferral. We prove a finite-library completion theorem: executable augmentations terminate with either a record preserving every declared response or proof that no library augmentation removes the loss. For an analytic control germ, integral closures characterize preservation along every analytic control arc, and finitely many Rees valuations detect failure. This state--measurement--record chain connects an all-arc criterion to executable completion or refusal, an attainable-Fisher local kernel criterion, and finite-sample decisions. A three-qubit calculation separates measurement loss from record loss. IBM experiments on Kingston and Marrakesh test decisions in fixed-particle-number and GHZ families, including negative controls. An end-to-end Kingston experiment reduces calibration shots by 33.3% while meeting prespecified noninferiority criteria on two held-out objectives. In two-epoch IQM Garnet data, median record-level drift is 0.0797 times full-distribution drift, but a significant residual remains in 35 of 36 settings. These experiments validate failure detection on the tested circuits; they do not establish universal compression performance or device quantum Fisher information.

Strengthened second law for periodic processes

Authors: Jake Schaefer, Ben Ansbacher, Jan Korbel, David H. Wolpert
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00639v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 3/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper derives a strict, physics-independent lower bound on the entropy production for any system undergoing periodic driving. By analyzing the Mismatch Cost (MMC) over multiple cycles, the authors establish a generalized second law applicable to diverse systems like spin models and digital automata. This result significantly strengthens current understanding of non-equilibrium thermodynamics in time-periodic settings.

Why it may be interesting. The derivation of a universal, physics-independent bound on entropy production for periodic systems provides a powerful new tool for analyzing non-equilibrium dynamics in complex systems, extending beyond standard Markovian assumptions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Deriving a physics-independent lower bound on the entropy production for any periodic process, strengthening the standard second law of thermodynamics.

Main result. A lower bound on entropy production is established using the Mismatch Cost (MMC) that depends only on the initial distribution and the driving map, regardless of the underlying physical details.

Method. The analysis uses the Mismatch Cost (MMC) decomposition of a general thermodynamic cost function, optimizing over all possible prior distributions to find a minimal dissipation bound.

Model / system. The framework applies to general stochastic processes evolving under periodic driving, modeled by repeatedly applying a stochastic map G. Specific applications include the Curie-Weiss model (Ising spins) and Deterministic Finite Automata (DFA).

Key observables. Entropy Production ($\Sigma_t$), Mismatch Cost (MMC), and the periodic-optimal prior ($ar{q}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The periodicity of the driving process, the initial distribution $p_0$, and the map $G$.

Assumptions / limitations. The process is modeled by the repeated application of a single map G, and the analysis relies on the general structure of the MMC decomposition.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by first establishing the general MMC decomposition, then specializing this to periodic processes, and finally applying the derived bounds to specific physical models like the Curie-Weiss model and DFAs.

Abstract

Many physical systems evolve under periodic driving: the same control protocol is applied again and again, even though the state of the system itself need not return to where it started after each cycle. We derive a physics-independent lower bound on the entropy production of \emph{any} periodic process modeled by the evolution of an initial distribution $p_0$ by a repeated application of the same map $G$. This is a strict strengthening of the second law of thermodynamics for periodic processes. It does not require that the single-period dynamics arise from a CTMC, satisfy (local) detailed balance, or be subject to other typical restrictions. We discuss its application to spins in the Curie-Weiss model and Deterministic Finite Automata, with possible extensions to other uniform computers.

A Dynamic Intermediate Representation for Hybrid Quantum-Classical Programs

Authors: Alex Rice, Chris Heunen, Tobias Grosser

Summary. This paper addresses the limitations of static quantum circuit models by proposing a Dynamic Intermediate Representation (IR). This new IR elevates quantum gates to first-class, runtime values, allowing classical computation to dynamically control quantum behavior. It enables the compact representation and optimization of complex hybrid quantum-classical algorithms.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial compilation framework for simulating and optimizing complex quantum algorithms that inherently mix classical decision-making with quantum evolution, which is vital for near-term quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The standard quantum circuit model is too static to effectively represent hybrid quantum-classical applications where gate choices depend on runtime data or measurement outcomes.

Main result. The authors introduce a new Intermediate Representation (IR) that treats quantum gates as first-class, dynamic values, enabling classical control flow to steer quantum computation.

Method. The core method is the design of a dynamic IR, realized using MLIR dialects, which incorporates dynamic gate operations (e.g., qssa.dyn_gate) alongside static quantum operations.

Model / system. The model concerns hybrid quantum-classical programs, where quantum operations are combined with classical data and control flow. The IR supports representing quantum states and operations using value semantics (SSA form).

Key observables. Gate values (e.g., I, X, Y, Z, S), phase flips, and the ability to model conditional quantum operations.

Important parameters / regimes. The structure of the quantum gates and the classical control flow determining gate execution.

Assumptions / limitations. The traditional circuit model is inherently static, and the XZ gadget is used to simplify the representation of conditional Pauli gates.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dynamic gate model's ability to represent complex sequences, such as two concatenated probabilistic phase-flip channels or double phase flips, using dynamic control flow compared to static methods.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the need for dynamic gates, details the XZ gadget for conditional Pauli gates, describes the IR structure using MLIR dialects (qssa.dyn_gate), and shows how this framework supports optimizations and analysis for hybrid programs.

Abstract

Quantum compilers typically follow the circuit model, representing programs as fixed sequences of gates. This static view breaks down in hybrid quantum-classical applications, where gate choices depend on runtime data or measurement results. We introduce a new Intermediate Representation (IR) that elevates gates to first-class values, enabling their dynamic creation, composition, and control. This unified representation allows classical computation to steer quantum behaviour, capturing phenomena including stochastic gate selection, adaptive error correction, and measurement-driven computation within a single framework. Case studies in noise modelling, randomised compilation, error correction, and measurement-based quantum computing show that our IR expresses these programs compactly and supports optimisations that were not possible in the circuit model. Evaluation on a benchmark suite of hybrid quantum-classical programs indicates that our IR represents programs compactly and facilitates compiler analysis and transformation.

Depth-1 expanders on the unitary group and applications

Authors: Anurag Anshu, Shankar Balasubramanian, Jonas Haferkamp, Aram W. Harrow, Xinyu Tan
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.01605v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

Summary. This paper develops efficient quantum expanders implementable with shallow circuits and applies them to construct 1D quantum Hamiltonians. The key achievement is demonstrating a construction that realizes the conjectured optimal relationship between the ground state entanglement entropy and the spectral gap ($S \propto \Delta^{-1/2}$). This advances the understanding of entanglement scaling in gapped quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit construction of Hamiltonians achieving the conjectured optimal entanglement-gap scaling ($\alpha=1/2$) is highly relevant for understanding the fundamental limits of entanglement in gapped quantum phases, which is central to AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses constructing quantum expanders with efficient circuit implementations and establishing optimal relationships between spectral gap and entanglement entropy in 1D quantum systems.

Main result. They constructed a family of frustration-free 1D Hamiltonians achieving the conjectured optimal entanglement-gap scaling $S = \Theta(\Delta^{-1/2})$, and developed depth-1 quantum expanders on the unitary group.

Method. The work combines the construction of quantum expanders (using Pauli/CNOT gates) with theoretical analysis of 1D Hamiltonians, relating spectral gaps to entanglement entropy via specific constructions.

Model / system. The models involve quantum expanders on the unitary group $U(d)$ and $SU(d)$, and 1D quantum many-body systems described by Hamiltonians $\{H_n\}$ that are engineered using these expanders.

Key observables. Spectral Gap ($\Delta$), Bipartite Entanglement Entropy ($S$), and the scaling exponent $\alpha$ relating $S$ and $\Delta$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension $n$ of the system, the constant gap $\Delta$, and the required circuit depth (achieved to be depth-1).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is the conjectured optimal scaling $S = \Theta(\Delta^{-1/2})$ for frustration-free 1D Hamiltonians, which the authors successfully achieve a construction for.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes, but the context implies figures illustrating the expander construction, the Hamiltonian family, and the scaling relationship between $S$ and $\Delta$.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first defining and constructing depth-1 quantum expanders. It then applies these expanders to build a family of frustration-free 1D Hamiltonians, proving the optimal entanglement-gap scaling. Finally, it extends the expander concept to the unitary group for applications like state testing.

Abstract

We construct a constant-degree and constant-gap quantum expander on $n$ qubits where each unitary can be implemented by a depth-$1$ and 1D circuit of Pauli or CNOT gates. We provide two applications of this expander. First, we use it to construct a family of frustration-free 1D Hamiltonians whose ground states obey the entanglement-gap relation $S = Θ(Δ^{-1/2})$; this is believed to be optimal, but achieving it had been open. Second, we use it to provide a streaming protocol that tests for closeness to a class of 1D volume-law entangled states. Moreover, we extend our quantum expander to a constant-degree and constant-gap expander on the unitary group where each unitary is a single $T$ gate, a single $T^{\dagger}$ gate, or a depth-$1$ Clifford circuit. This implies that a random sequence of unitaries from the expander yields a gapped walk on a dense subgroup of the unitary group. This improves upon previous work by Bourgain and Gamburd which did not control the dependence of the gap on the dimension.

Local Gaussian bounds on the non-destructive discrimination of two-mode squeezed states

Authors: Mi-Jung So, James Moran, Youngrong Lim, Mahn-Soo Choi, Hyukjoon Kwon

Summary. This paper develops a theoretical framework to quantify the trade-off between successfully identifying a quantum state and disturbing it during the measurement process. It focuses on two-mode squeezed vacuum states using local Gaussian measurements. The key finding is that incorporating pre-shared entanglement can enhance discrimination beyond the standard theoretical limits.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a crucial infinite-dimensional generalization of the information-disturbance tradeoff, extending established concepts from finite-dimensional quantum systems to continuous-variable quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The work analyzes the non-destructive discrimination of two-mode squeezed vacuum (TMSV) states using local Gaussian measurements.

Main result. The authors establish an explicit tradeoff relation between success probability and measurement-induced disturbance, and show that pre-shared entanglement allows exceeding the standard local Gaussian bound.

Method. The analysis involves deriving the success probability and fidelity using Gaussian state theory, optimizing a score function ($P \cdot F$), and comparing different measurement protocols.

Model / system. The system involves two-mode squeezed vacuum states, which are continuous-variable (CV) Gaussian states. The process is modeled using local Gaussian measurements and potentially pre-shared entanglement.

Key observables. Success Probability ($P$), Fidelity ($F$), and the combined score function ($P \cdot F$).

Important parameters / regimes. Squeezing Parameter ($\lambda$), beamsplitter angle ($ heta$), and the strength of pre-shared entanglement.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is restricted to the GLOCC1 class (at most one adaptive stage) for the primary bound derivation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the general local Gaussian circuit for discrimination, comparing performance across different measurement schemes (simplest vs. displacement vs. squeezing) as a function of the beam splitter angle $ heta$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general framework for local Gaussian discrimination, then optimizing the score function $P \cdot F$ for various measurement strategies, and finally deriving the optimal trade-off relation.

Abstract

Typical measurement setups in quantum systems are destructive, meaning that states are irretrievably altered after measurement. In this work, we analyse non-destructive discrimination of two two-mode squeezed vacuum states using local Gaussian measurements. We investigate a tradeoff relation between the success probability of discrimination and the fidelity of the resulting state with the initial state, and construct a protocol given by local Gaussian measurements, which is optimal within our numerically explored class. We also extend to the case where we allow for additional pre-shared entanglement, and show that this regime allows us to exceed the standard local Gaussian bound for the fidelity-success probability tradeoff. Our work provides a natural extension of the tradeoff between information gain and disturbance in entangled-state discrimination, previously established for finite-dimensional quantum systems, to infinite-dimensional continuous-variable systems.

Optimization Landscape Geometry in VQE for Frustrated Quantum Spin Models

Authors: Vojtěch Novák, Ivan Zelinka, Swagatam Das, Martin Beseda
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.00235v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work rigorously benchmarks various classical optimization algorithms for VQE applied to frustrated quantum spin models. By characterizing the geometry of the energy landscape, the authors demonstrate that optimization performance is highly dependent on the model structure and that the total error budget must account separately for optimization failure and the inherent variational gap. This guides the development of more robust quantum simulation protocols.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a deep, quantitative analysis of the limitations of classical optimization techniques when applied to complex, frustrated quantum many-body systems, which is crucial for designing practical quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper benchmarks classical optimization algorithms for Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) calculations to understand how the underlying Hamiltonian-ansatz landscape geometry dictates performance.

Main result. Optimizer performance varies significantly across different frustrated spin model hierarchies, and the study successfully separates optimization error from the inherent variational gap error.

Method. The authors benchmark eight classical optimizers (local, stochastic, evolutionary, etc.) using exact statevector simulation on controlled spin models, characterizing the energy landscape geometry.

Model / system. The study uses frustrated quantum spin models, including diagonal Ising glasses, transverse-field Ising, and anisotropic Heisenberg models. The Hamiltonian is parameterized to cover these different physical regimes.

Key observables. Optimization error ($\Delta E_{ ext{opt}}$), ground-state energy error ($\Delta E$), and the variational gap ($E_{ ext{ref}} - E_0$). Fidelity is also used as a diagnostic.

Important parameters / regimes. The comparison involves varying circuit depth (from product-state to hardware-efficient ansatz) and the model parameters ($\Gamma, \lambda$) defining the Hamiltonian.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on exact statevector simulation to isolate the intrinsic landscape from finite-shot noise, and it distinguishes between optimization difficulty and variational expressivity limits.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the two ansatz architectures (product-state vs. hardware-efficient) used for the different models.

Paper structure. The paper systematically benchmarks optimizers across a hierarchy of frustrated spin models (Q1, Q2, Q3), characterizing the landscape geometry and quantifying the sources of error in VQE performance.

Abstract

We benchmark eight classical optimizers for exact-statevector VQE calculations on a controlled hierarchy of frustrated spin models, ranging from a diagonal Ising glass to transverse-field Ising and anisotropic Heisenberg models. The benchmark includes local, stochastic-gradient, evolutionary, covariance-adaptation, and swarm-based optimization methods under matched function-evaluation budgets. To understand their performance beyond final energies, we characterize the underlying Hamiltonian--ansatz landscapes in terms of local minima, gradients, curvature, and ground-state reachability. We use simple variational circuits, from an $R_y$ product-state ansatz for the diagonal model to shallow $R_y$--CNOT hardware-efficient circuits for the noncommuting models, and study how increasing circuit depth changes their expressivity, reachability, and optimization geometry. We find that optimizer performance changes substantially across the model hierarchy and is closely connected to landscape structure, while the variational gap represents a separate source of error. These results show how classical optimization, variational expressivity, and landscape geometry jointly determine VQE performance for frustrated spin models.

Summary. This paper presents QILP-0, a method to construct 'observational declarative twins' for quantum circuits, aiming to solve the interpretability problem in QML. It builds a formal, logical theory that reconstructs observed quantum behavior by tracking observables through a defined scope. Validation on large-scale qubit systems and image data confirms the theory's ability to achieve perfect, conflict-free reconstruction.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a novel, logically rigorous framework for quantum circuit interpretability that moves beyond simple learned representations by grounding the explanation in traceable, discrete logical statements derived directly from observable measurements.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the opacity in Quantum Machine Learning (QML) by developing a method to reconstruct an explicitly scoped, traceable declarative theory of a quantum circuit's observable behavior.

Main result. The proposed QILP-0 theory successfully achieved complete, conflict-free reconstruction of observed relations with strict accuracy of one in both native-discrete (Bars & Stripes) and continuous (Low-Depth MNIST) validation settings.

Method. It introduces QXymb, a framework culminating in QILP-0, which constructs a finite multi-valued propositional logic program by traversing observables incrementally and mapping latent structure deterministically back to original observable columns.

Model / system. The system involves general quantum circuits, specifically utilizing Pauli observables for the QILP-0 specialization. Validation tests include product and grid-CZ embeddings up to 100 qubits and low-depth MNIST digit-0/1 instances.

Key observables. Observable responses, reference-relative coverage, and the resulting finite multi-valued propositional logic program (the 'observational declarative twin').

Important parameters / regimes. Observational scope, structural grading, observational reference horizon, and the number of qubits (16 to 100).

Paper structure. The paper introduces the QXymb framework, details the QILP-0 specialization for constructing declarative twins, outlines the incremental traversal and discretization process, and validates the method using two complementary settings: exhaustive Bars & Stripes experiments and Low-Depth MNIST analyses.

Abstract

This paper introduces QXymb, a general framework for constructing observational declarative twins of quantum circuits, and develops QILP-0, its first complete order-0 specialization. QILP-0 constructs a finite multi-valued propositional logic program from observed circuit behaviour within a declared observational scope. The pipeline traverses a declared family of quantum observables incrementally according to a reproducible structural grading and a declared observational reference horizon. Progress is quantified through reference-relative coverage against a fixed target-independent reference. Observable responses are organized through target-independent geometry, while retained latent structure is mapped deterministically back to original observable columns before symbolic processing, preserving observational semantics and provenance. Selected observable profiles are converted into a finite relation through admissible target-independent discretization. The target is used only afterwards to audit twin-admissibility and induce the declarative theory. A theory is certified as an exact observational declarative twin when it completely and correctly reconstructs the resulting finite task-conditioned discrete relation. Logical exactness is therefore separated from numerical, backend, provider, and discretization uncertainty, which is retained as audit metadata. Validation uses two complementary QML settings. Exhaustive Bars & Stripes experiments compare product and grid-CZ embeddings from 16 to 100 qubits and exercise the native-discrete branch. Low-Depth MNIST analyses all 14,708 digit-0/1 instances before and after a trained variational quantum transformation and exercises continuous discretization. In every reported relation, the induced QILP-0 theory achieves complete, conflict-free reconstruction with strict accuracy equal to one.

Abstract

Polar molecules can also be manipulated using microwave dressing fields to invert the sign of the dipole-dipole interaction. In this work, we study a two-dimensional system of antidipolar particles by calculating its equation of state and structural properties. The system behaves as a liquid even for scattering lengths significantly greater than the dipolar length. For large enough densities, the system transitions to a solid with one particle per lattice site via a first-order phase transition at a significantly smaller density than its dipolar counterpart. Moreover, motivated by the recent realization of a strongly axially trapped, bilayer geometry [Science 384, 546-551 (2024)], we study the properties of the bilayer liquid phase as the inter-layer distance is tuned, and provide the range of parameters where one layer can influence the properties of the other.

Authors: Alex Ben Ishay, Yuval Eyal, Yuval Cohen, Nati Erez

Summary. This work applies the QAOA algorithm to optimize gas throughput in complex pipeline networks, transforming the continuous physical problem into a discrete QUBO format. The authors demonstrate the feasibility of this approach by running the circuit on a trapped-ion quantum processor, achieving near-optimal results with surprisingly shallow circuit depth. This establishes a tangible, end-to-end quantum-assisted optimization workflow for critical infrastructure.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, real-world engineering optimization problem (gas flow) that is successfully mapped onto a quantum algorithm (QAOA) and tested on current hardware, serving as a proof-of-concept for quantum computation in industrial physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the computationally hard problem of maximizing gas throughput in natural gas transmission networks subject to complex hydraulic and operational constraints.

Main result. The QAOA approach successfully recovered near-optimal solutions on a trapped-ion quantum processor using only $p=2$ layers, demonstrating feasibility with significantly shallower circuits than simulated.

Method. The problem is mapped to a QUBO formulation and solved using the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA), comparing simulator results ($p=30$) with direct hardware execution ($p=2$).

Model / system. The physical system is a natural gas pipeline network modeled as a graph, governed by the Panhandle-B hydraulic equation. The optimization is framed by a cost Hamiltonian combining delivery objectives and physical constraint penalties.

Key observables. Maximum gas throughput, physically valid nodal pressure assignments, and the probability distribution of candidate solutions.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of QAOA layers ($p$), the pressure discretization step ($\Delta\pi$), and the physical parameters of the gas network (e.g., $P_s=70$ barg).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary approximations involve discretizing continuous nodal pressures and approximating the non-linear Panhandle-B flow equation using a polynomial fit.

Figures summary. The figures illustrate the QAOA circuit diagram and show the comparison between simulated and hardware-derived candidate solutions relative to the continuous classical optimum.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the complex optimization problem, derives the QUBO/cost Hamiltonian, details the QAOA framework, presents simulator results, and concludes with an end-to-end demonstration on the IonQ Forte-1 quantum processor.

Abstract

Maximizing gas throughput in transmission networks under hydraulic and operational constraints is a combinatorial problem whose complexity grows exponentially with network size, making it computationally intensive to solve exactly. This paper addresses the graph-based optimization problem by optimizing nodal-pressure assignments under the Panhandle-B hydraulic equation. By framing the problem as a search over discretized nodal-pressure assignments coupled with a cost Hamiltonian that encodes both the delivery objective and physical-constraint penalties, we establish a unified formulation suitable for the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). The mathematical model is adapted to a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) formulation and implemented using the Classiq quantum software platform. In simulator-based experiments, QAOA recovered the maximum-throughput valid operating point, consistent with classical exhaustive evaluation and classical hydraulic simulation reference solutions. A distinctive contribution of this work is the end-to-end execution of a reduced problem instance on the IonQ Forte-1 trapped-ion quantum processor. Remarkably, the hardware implementation used only $p=2$ QAOA layers, substantially fewer than the $p=30$ layers used in the simulator-based study. Despite this significant reduction in circuit depth, the QPU produced physically valid and interpretable candidate solutions that bracketed the continuous classical optimum, with each located within one pressure-discretization step of it. These results demonstrate that meaningful gas-network optimization behavior can be obtained using considerably shallower QAOA circuits than initially expected and provide an end-to-end proof of concept for near-term quantum-assisted gas-network optimization.

Abstract

Quantum metrology exploits quantum states to achieve an estimation sensitivity beyond classical limits. In the continuous-variable (CV) regime, the squeezed state has been used to implement deterministic quantum sensing, but the quantum metrology sensitivity of this state is significantly affected by losses or detection inefficiencies, which restrict its applications. In this work, quantum distributed sensing is proposed using optical parametric amplified multimode entanglement generated from squeezed states. It is found that the sensitivity is robust to loss or detection inefficiency when large-gain optical parametric amplification (OPA) is introduced, where a two-mode Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen-entangled state and a four-mode cluster state are exploited for analysis. The quantum sensitivity is greatly improved compared to that without OPA in almost all loss scenarios. The quantum Fisher matrix is calculated for both states to obtain the optimal bound in comparison with our scheme, and it is found that even with a small or moderate OPA gain, quantum sensing can be improved compared with the traditional scheme. The states are also compared with corresponding single-mode squeezed states, finding the parameter ranges where entangled states perform better. This study provides a method for realizing large-scale quantum metrology in real-world applications despite losses or detection inefficiencies.

Abstract

In quantum metrology, adaptive and causal-superposition strategies are proven to be beneficial over parallel schemes for a finite number of channel uses, but their advantages disappear in the asymptotic limit. We show that quantum operations with indefinite time direction, specifically, time-flip (TF)-assisted strategies, referred to as indefinite time-directed metrology (ITDM), can overcome this asymptotic equivalence. Using semidefinite programming, we rigorously demonstrate that TF-assisted protocols can achieve quantum Fisher information (QFI) strictly exceeding the maximum value attainable by parallel, adaptive, and causal-superposition strategies, both for finite and asymptotically many channel uses. Moreover, we identify a class of Pauli noise channels for which ITDM achieves Heisenberg scaling, while all parallel, adaptive, and causal-superposition strategies remain restricted to standard scaling. We call this phenomenon as metrological activation. Interestingly, this activation can be used to exhibit that the quantum time-flip and transposition supermaps cannot be simulated by conventional quantum circuits or causal-superposition strategies using any finite number of channel queries, thereby establishing indefinite time direction as a genuine resource for quantum metrology.
