Imaging how fluctuations destroy superconductivity in two dimensions

Authors: Logan Bishop-Van Horn, Teng Zhang, Sara Metti, Tyler Lindemann, Michael J. Manfra, Kathryn A. Moler
Type: experiment · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28837v1

Summary. This paper experimentally investigates the destruction of superconductivity in 2D Josephson junction arrays by measuring local phase stiffness. The results show that superconductivity is suppressed by quantum fluctuations, not just thermal ones, and reveal that anomalous metallic behavior coexists with spatial disorder at low carrier densities. This provides key insights into the nature of quantum phase transitions in superconducting materials.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly probes the quantum phase transition boundary in a controlled solid-state system, providing experimental evidence for quantum fluctuation effects beyond simple thermal models, which is relevant to understanding quantum critical points in many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how thermal and quantum fluctuations destroy superconductivity in two-dimensional systems, and whether an intermediate 'anomalous metal state' represents a novel phase of matter.

Main result. The superconducting critical temperature and phase stiffness are suppressed more strongly than expected from thermal fluctuations alone, indicating that quantum fluctuations play a crucial role. Furthermore, anomalous metal behavior emerges alongside large-scale spatial inhomogeneity at low carrier densities.

Method. The research employs scanning SQUID microscopy to directly image the local phase stiffness ($ ho_s$) and measures electrical transport properties across Josephson junction arrays.

Model / system. The physical system is a Josephson junction (JJ) array patterned on a high-mobility InAs two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG), serving as a model for a 2D superconductor. The analysis compares experimental measurements against theoretical models like the classical XY Hamiltonian.

Key observables. Local phase stiffness ($ ho_s$), superconducting critical temperature ($T_{c,\varphi}$), sheet resistance ($R_s$), and the in-phase magnetic response ($M'$).

Important parameters / regimes. Carrier density (tunable via gate voltage $V_g$), temperature ($T$), and Josephson coupling energy ($E_J$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis compares results against predictions from classical XY models and assumes that the measured phase stiffness is a reliable thermodynamic measure of superconducting order.

Figures summary. Figures show SQUID maps of diamagnetic response, temperature dependence of $ ho_s$ and $R_s$, and comparisons of measured phase stiffness against predictions from thermal fluctuation models.

Paper structure. The paper systematically presents measurements of phase stiffness and resistance across varying carrier densities and temperatures, contrasting the observed suppression of superconductivity with predictions based solely on thermal fluctuations, and detailing the emergence of spatial inhomogeneity.

Abstract

Two-dimensional superconductors are model systems for thermal and quantum fluctuations. Key questions persist: whether existing models quantitatively describe the destruction of superconductivity, and whether an intermediate "anomalous metal state" represents a new phase of matter. We use scanning magnetic susceptibility to directly image the local phase stiffness, a thermodynamic measure of superconducting order, in gate-tunable Josephson junction arrays, a model two-dimensional superconductor. Across a broad range of carrier densities, we find that the superconducting critical temperature and phase stiffness are suppressed more strongly than expected from thermal fluctuations alone. At low carrier densities, we find that anomalous metal transport and large-scale spatial inhomogeneity emerge together. These results indicate that quantum fluctuations suppress superconductivity and that anomalous metal behavior emerges from phase slips in spatially disordered regions.

Conductance of silicon nanotube junctions in high magnetic fields

Summary. This theoretical study characterizes the quantum electronic transport through silicon nanotube junctions under strong magnetic fields. Using a tight-binding model and NEGF formalism, the authors find that the magnetic response is highly sensitive to the field's orientation and junction geometry. The results establish unique magnetotransport signatures for SiNTs, distinguishing them from carbon nanotubes.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed study of magnetotransport in semiconductor nanostructures provides a platform to test theoretical models of quantum confinement and orbital coupling in low-dimensional systems, which has parallels with superconducting or quantum wire physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates coherent quantum transport through silicon nanotube (SiNT) junctions when subjected to high external magnetic fields.

Main result. SiNT junctions exhibit irregular transmission oscillations, are highly sensitive to magnetic fields perpendicular to the tube axis, and show distinct field-dependent in-gap features in semiconducting junctions, differing significantly from carbon nanotube behavior.

Method. The research employs a tight-binding (TB) model solved using the Non-Equilibrium Green's Function (NEGF) formalism, incorporating magnetic fields via the Peierls substitution.

Model / system. The system consists of SiNT junctions, modeled using a four-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonian derived from DFT calculations. The junctions studied are metallic (6,0)+(6,0) and semiconducting (9,9)+(9,9) types.

Key observables. Electronic transmission spectrum T(E), oscillation frequency, and the presence/absence of in-gap transmission features.

Important parameters / regimes. Magnetic field strength up to 60 T, magnetic field direction (perpendicular vs. parallel to the axis), overlap length, and inter-tube distance.

Assumptions / limitations. The Zeeman contribution is neglected, focusing solely on the orbital response to the magnetic field. TB parameters are fitted to initial DFT results.

Figures summary. Figures show band structures comparing TB to DFT for both junction types, and transmission spectra illustrating the dependence on overlap length, inter-tube distance, and applied magnetic field strength.

Paper structure. The paper follows a structure of establishing the physical model (DFT/TB parametrization), applying the NEGF formalism to calculate magnetotransport, and systematically analyzing the results based on junction geometry and field orientation.

Abstract

We investigate coherent quantum transport through silicon nanotube (SiNT) junctions in high magnetic fields up to 60 T using a tight-binding model combined with the non-equilibrium Green's function formalism, and magnetic field included via Peierls substitution. We consider junctions of metallic nanotubes (6,0)+(6,0) and semiconducting ones (9,9)+(9,9), and examine the effects of the overlap length, inter-tube distance, magnetic-field direction, and field strength on the electronic transmission. In contrast to carbon nanotube junctions, the SiNT systems exhibit irregular transmission oscillations and do not show the emergence of highly conductive gateway states. The transmission is substantially more sensitive to a magnetic field perpendicular to the nanotube axis than to a parallel field, while increasing the field strength progressively modifies the transmission spectrum. Increasing the overlap length results in more frequent transmission oscillations, whereas increasing the inter-tube distance modifies their positions and amplitudes without changing their overall character. Generally, similar trends are observed for both types of junctions, involving metallic and semiconducting nanotubes. However, in the junction of semiconducting nanotubes, one observes peculiar additional field-dependent in-gap transmission features. These results demonstrate that the magnetic-field response of SiNT junctions is strongly governed by their geometry and differs qualitatively from that of pristine carbon nanotube junctions.

Summary. This theoretical paper rigorously analyzes the conditions for pattern formation in activator-inhibitor reaction-diffusion systems undergoing a Turing bifurcation. By developing high-order normal form theory, the authors determine that the bifurcation can be degenerate, leading to the formation of localized structures via a homoclinic snaking mechanism. This work provides deep mathematical insight into pattern selection in non-equilibrium physical models.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical reaction-diffusion systems, the mathematical techniques used—analyzing pattern formation via bifurcation theory and normal forms—are foundational to understanding phase transitions and pattern selection in non-equilibrium statistical mechanics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper provides precise mathematical conditions for the existence and criticality of Turing bifurcations in general activator-inhibitor reaction-diffusion systems, focusing on the degenerate bifurcation leading to localized patterns.

Main result. The analysis shows that depending on the parameter scaling ($\delta o 0$), the bifurcation can be subcritical or supercritical, leading to a codimension-two degenerate bifurcation characterized by the sign of a fifth-order normal form coefficient.

Method. The authors employ weakly nonlinear perturbation theory, deriving and analyzing the normal form equations up to the fifth order to determine the stability and nature of the pattern formation transition.

Model / system. The study concerns two-component activator-inhibitor reaction-diffusion equations, exemplified by generalized Schnakenberg and Brusselator models, defined on a one-dimensional infinite domain.

Key observables. Turing bifurcation, localized patterns (homoclinic snaking), subcritical/supercritical nature of the bifurcation, and the Maxwell point.

Important parameters / regimes. The diffusion ratio $\delta$, and the parameters governing the reaction kinetics ($\alpha, eta, \gamma$, etc.).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on Taylor expansion and the assumption that the system can be reduced to an amplitude equation via multiple scales, particularly in the limit $\delta o 0$.

Figures summary. Figure 1(a) illustrates the dispersion relation at a Turing bifurcation, while Figure 1(b) shows the energy functional $E(A)$ used to define the Maxwell point condition.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the general conditions for Turing instability (T1 & T2), then derives the amplitude equation, and finally performs high-order normal form calculations to determine the coefficients governing the degenerate bifurcation and the emergence of localized structures.

Abstract

Precise conditions are provided for the existence and criticality of Turing bifurcations in a general class of activator-inhibitor reaction-diffusion equations on a one-dimensional infinite domain. The class includes generalised Schnakenberg and Brusselator models, as well as other models with cubic autocatalytic nonlinear terms. Previous numerical work suggests the existence of a bifurcation structure containing localised patterns due to the so-called homoclinic snaking mechanism. This paper provides explicit calculations to justify those results. Two distinct scalings of parameters that lead to tractable normal-form coefficients are considered in the limit that the diffusion ratio $δ\to 0$. First, under a small-parameter scaling, the Turing bifurcation is shown to be always subcritical. Second, a large-parameter scaling reveals the Turing bifurcation to be supercritical, leading, by continuity, to the existence of a codimension-two degenerate bifurcation. The sign of a 5th-order normal form coefficient is also computed, which is shown to have the correct sign for the local birth of homoclinic snaking. For the case of the Brusselator, two such codimension-two points can be found explicitly, as can the leading-order expression for the Maxwell point, in a parameter wedge about which localised patterns emerge. Numerical results are found to be consistent with the theory.

Summary. This work constructs a large library of carbon nanotube junctions and uses advanced computational methods to map out their magnetotransport behavior. It reveals that junction conductance is sensitive to the junction's average chiral angle, while the energy gap is dictated by the electronic nature (metallic vs. semiconducting) of the connected tubes. These findings establish crucial, statistically robust rules for designing CNT-based electronic networks.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed, first-principles study of quantum transport in nanoscale junctions, particularly the separation of descriptors governing conductance versus gap size, provides valuable insights into mesoscopic physics relevant to quantum devices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how the specific structure of individual carbon nanotube (CNT) junctions dictates their magnetotransport properties across a diverse population of junctions.

Main result. The average transmission step is primarily governed by the mean chiral angle, while the junction energy gap depends predominantly on the metallic or semiconducting nature of the constituent CNTs.

Method. The authors employed an automated, high-throughput workflow combining molecular dynamics, tight-binding theory, Peierls magnetic coupling, and non-equilibrium Green's functions, followed by machine-learning analysis.

Model / system. The system consists of single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)–SWCNT junctions, with a library of 146 junctions constructed spanning various structural and electronic types. The calculations utilize an empirical tight-binding model incorporating curvature and inter-nanotube coupling.

Key observables. Averaged first transmission-step value, junction energy gap, and transport characteristics as a function of temperature and perpendicular magnetic field.

Important parameters / regimes. Mean chiral angle, metallic/semiconducting character of constituent CNTs, temperature (up to 300 K), and perpendicular magnetic field strength.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis uses an empirical tight-binding model and makes approximations regarding orbital directions (radial approximation) and hopping integrals.

Figures summary. Figures summarize the workflow, showing how transport properties (transmission spectra and band gaps) are calculated and analyzed across varying magnetic fields, temperatures, and junction metallicities.

Paper structure. The paper first details the construction of the diverse junction library and the complex computational workflow. It then presents the analysis of key transport descriptors, establishing distinct structure-transport hierarchies, and concludes by providing statistically robust trends for network-scale modeling.

Abstract

Electronic transport in carbon nanotube (CNT) assemblies is controlled by a heterogeneous population of CNT--CNT junctions, yet most microscopic studies consider only a few representative systems. Here, we construct a library of 146 single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT)--SWCNT junctions spanning broad structural and electronic diversity and analyse their magnetotransport using an automated workflow combining molecular dynamics, tight-binding theory, Peierls magnetic coupling, and non-equilibrium Green's functions.The resulting transport data are subsequently analysed using machine-learning methods. Two complementary transport descriptors reveal distinct structure--transport hierarchies. The averaged first transmission-step value is governed primarily by the mean chiral angle of the two nanotubes forming a junction, whereas the energy gap of the junction depends predominantly on the metallic or semiconducting character of the constituent CNTs. Temperature generally suppresses the averaged transmission while reducing the extracted gap, whereas a perpendicular magnetic field affects transmission much more strongly than the gap. Signatures of interference-driven transport remain visible even at $300~\mathrm{K}$. Notably, semiconducting--semiconducting junctions retain the largest gaps but, once shifted into their conducting regime, can exhibit transmission comparable to or exceeding that of metallic junctions. These results establish statistically robust junction-level trends that can inform future network-scale models of CNT assemblies.

Summary. This paper proposes a hybrid quantum computing platform coupling a Kitaev parity qubit to a spin qubit using Majorana fermions in a quantum dot array. Abstract

Spin is typically traced out in the description of quantum-dot-based Kitaev chains to simplify the construction of Majorana fermions. Yet the intrinsic spin structure of poor-man's Majorana modes in minimal Kitaev chains under finite Zeeman fields offers a natural interface for the Kitaev parity qubit to interact with other spinful systems. Here, we establish such a platform to bridge the parity qubit and a quantum-dot spin qubit, with the effective coupling governed by the spin-dependent delocalization of the Majorana modes. Depending on whether the spin qubit is coupled to one or two chains constituting the parity qubit, the parity-spin coupling exhibits distinct forms: an anisotropic parity-conserving exchange interaction or a nontrivial exchange tensor tunable via the interchain superconducting-phase bias. Leveraging fast spin-qubit manipulation, we further demonstrate universal parity-qubit control, high-fidelity qubit-state readout, and entangling operations between the parity and spin qubits. These results turn the spinful structure of poor-man's Majoranas from a finite-field imperfection into a resource for hybrid quantum control.

Summary. This work develops a renormalized Ginzburg-Landau theory to study superconducting phase transitions in high-Tc superconductors under magnetic fields. By self-consistently renormalizing the theory, the authors regularize divergences and accurately predict the finite, rounded specific-heat anomaly. This framework successfully models the dimensional crossover induced by the magnetic field, providing quantitative agreement with experimental data from YBCO.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous treatment of fluctuation effects and dimensional crossover using renormalization techniques provides a powerful theoretical tool for understanding critical behavior in complex, layered quantum materials, which shares conceptual links with critical phenomena studied in AMO systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper theoretically analyzes phase transitions and critical phenomena in high-temperature superconductors, specifically addressing the limitations of standard Ginzburg-Landau theory in low-dimensional systems and under magnetic fields.

Main result. The renormalized framework successfully regularizes mean-field divergences, yielding a finite, dimensionality-dependent specific-heat anomaly that accurately captures the suppression and rounding of the mean-field discontinuity observed in cuprates.

Method. The authors employ a renormalized Ginzburg-Landau framework, treating the quadratic coefficient self-consistently via Hartree-renormalization to account for fluctuation-loop corrections.

Model / system. The system is high-temperature superconductors, modeled using an anisotropic-mass Ginzburg-Landau Hamiltonian. The theory incorporates minimal coupling to an external magnetic field, which quantizes fluctuations into Landau levels and effectively reduces the system's dimensionality.

Key observables. Fluctuation-specific heat anomaly, critical Ginzburg region width, specific-heat peak position, and upper-critical-field crossover boundary.

Important parameters / regimes. Dimensionality (D), magnetic field strength (B), and the fluctuation-coupling strength ($\eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The model focuses on orbital coupling, neglecting the Zeeman (spin-paramagnetic) pair-breaking term for simplicity. The analysis is performed in the long-wavelength, coarse-grained limit near $T_{c0}$.

Abstract

This paper presents a theoretical analysis of phase transitions and critical phenomena in high-temperature superconductors using a renormalized Ginzburg-Landau framework. Rather than assuming a conventional linear temperature dependence, we treat the quadratic coefficient as a self-consistent, Hartree-renormalized quantity determined by fluctuation-loop corrections. This renormalization regularizes mean-field divergences and yields a finite, dimensionality-dependent specific-heat anomaly near the transition. When an external magnetic field is applied, minimal coupling quantizes order-parameter fluctuations into discrete Landau levels, reducing the effective dimensionality via an effective spectral dimension. In this framework, the intrinsic fluctuation-coupling strength self-consistently determines the temperature width of the critical Ginzburg region, while the cyclotron energy of the quantized fluctuations uniquely establishes both the specific-heat peak position and the upper-critical-field crossover boundary. Comparison with $\text{YBa}_2\text{Cu}_3\text{O}_{7-δ}$ experimental data demonstrates that this approach captures the suppression of the sharp mean-field discontinuity and accurately reproduces the vortex-lattice topological structure under finite magnetic fields.

Summary. This work introduces a powerful theoretical framework that maps the vibrations of physical lattices onto effective multi-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonians. By doing so, it allows the use of electronic transport tools to study phononic phenomena like mode conversion and gap physics. The study demonstrates that geometric features, such as domain walls, can engineer robust, resonant transport channels within otherwise insulating frequency windows.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to translate classical lattice dynamics into a quantum-mechanical, graph-based Hamiltonian framework provides powerful tools for analyzing wave transport and localization in condensed matter systems, bridging phononics and electronic transport theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a framework to map complex harmonic vibrational systems (phononics) onto the mathematical structure of multi-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonians.

Main result. This mapping successfully describes geometry-induced phenomena, such as mode conversion at bends and the emergence of resonant transport channels via domain walls, mirroring SSH-like physics.

Method. The method involves deriving effective onsite energies and hopping amplitudes from the system's force-constant matrix, allowing the application of electronic transport tools like the scattering matrix method.

Model / system. The system is a lattice of point masses connected by elastic interactions, analyzed through its vibrational spectrum and scattering properties. The model is represented by a multi-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonian, $\hat{H}$.

Key observables. Polarization-resolved transmission and reflection probabilities, spectral gaps, and the presence/absence of localized edge/domain-wall states.

Important parameters / regimes. System geometry (e.g., bends, zigzag patterns, domain walls), bond angles, and frequency ($\omega$).

Assumptions / limitations. The system dynamics can be accurately described by an effective multi-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonian derived from the harmonic approximation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the graph representation of the Hamiltonian, compare scattering results for bent chains, and show transmission spectra demonstrating resonant peaks induced by domain walls in zigzag chains.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the mapping between phononic dynamics and tight-binding models, validates this mapping using a bend benchmark, then applies it to zigzag chains to study SSH-like physics and domain walls, and finally analyzes mode conversion using scattering matrices.

Abstract

We present a framework that maps harmonic vibrational systems onto effective multi-orbital tight-binding Hamiltonians, establishing a direct correspondence between phononic degrees of freedom and graph-based lattice models. Within this mapping, the Cartesian displacement components of each mass become internal orbitals, while elastic interactions generate effective onsite energies and hopping amplitudes determined by the geometry of the system. This representation enables the application of numerical and conceptual tools originally developed for electronic transport to the study of phononic systems. The method is first applied to a monoatomic chain containing an angular bend. For this benchmark system, analytical expressions for the polarization-resolved transmission and reflection probabilities are derived and compared against calculations performed using a recursive scattering-matrix method within the mapped tight-binding representation. Excellent agreement is obtained, validating the mapping and demonstrating its ability to describe geometry-induced mode conversion and the influence of evanescent states. We then investigate zigzag chains, where alternating bond orientations generate effective dimerized Hamiltonians reminiscent of the Su--Schrieffer--Heeger (SSH) model. Although the uniform zigzag chain develops a spectral gap, no localized edge states are observed. We show that modified boundary parameters generated by the phononic mapping suppress the edge-state formation expected from the ideal SSH picture. Introducing a geometric domain wall restores localized domain-wall states inside the gap, which give rise to resonant transmission channels across an otherwise insulating frequency window. The resonance energies remain robust against system-size variations and the corresponding transmission approaches unity.

Summary. This work develops a geometric framework to describe optical transitions in interacting quantum materials, classifying excitations into localized (flat geometry) and delocalized (curved geometry) types. The key finding is that the flat geometry of excitons forces the third-order photoconductivity to zero, offering a powerful diagnostic tool for understanding many-body optical response.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit connection drawn between geometric properties (flatness/curvature) and measurable nonlinear optical responses (photoconductivity) provides a powerful, unifying theoretical framework for understanding many-body optical phenomena.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how electron-electron interactions modify the resonant quantum geometry of optical transitions and establishes a link between this geometry and nonlinear optical response.

Main result. Interactions induce a rich structure in the quantum geometry, leading to localized excitations (excitons) possessing a uniform and flat geometry ($K=0$), which causes the third-order circular photoconductivity to vanish.

Method. The analysis employs a many-body length gauge formulation to characterize the geometry using Hermitian metrics, connections, and curvature tensors, contrasting localized and delocalized excitation regimes.

Model / system. The study models optical transitions in interacting many-body electronic materials, using concepts like particle-hole excitations and relative coordinate localization to parameterize the quantum geometry.

Key observables. Hermitian curvature tensor ($K$), third-order circular photoconductivity, and the Berry curvature ($\Omega$).

Important parameters / regimes. The distinction between localized (excitonic) and delocalized excitations; the limit of vanishing frequency ($\omega_\Sigma o 0$).

Abstract

The Riemannian geometry of optical transition dipoles has become a useful picture for understanding linear and nonlinear optical response. Here we argue that the interacting quantum geometry of optical transitions possesses a rich structure and can be naturally delineated into two types: localized and delocalized particle-hole excitations. The former possess uniform quantum geometry with flat (vanishing) Hermitian curvature; the latter possess non-uniform quantum geometry with non-vanishing Hermitian curvature. As a striking example, we find that uniform quantum geometry can be produced by electron-hole interactions: even when composed from extended Bloch states in the particle and hole bands, we find excitons have uniform and flat quantum geometry. By developing a many-body length gauge formulation of nonlinear response, we find this uniform and flat excitonic quantum geometry zeros its third-order circular photoconductivity in non-magnetic materials in stark contrast to its non-interacting counterparts. Similarly, the zero Hermitian curvature of localized optical transitions locks their Hall response to that of the ground state. This demonstrates the rich landscape of many-body optical response controlled by an interacting quantum geometry.

Abstract

Spin-orbit coupling (SOC) plays a central role in modern condensed matter physics and is crucial for the development of spin-based devices and quantum technologies. In particular, the interplay between Rashba SOC (R-SOC), arising from structural inversion asymmetry, and Dresselhaus SOC (D-SOC), due to bulk inversion asymmetry, significantly influences spin coherence and manipulation in two-dimensional and topological systems. Disentangling these SOC components is essential for spintronics, superconducting spintronics, or topological superconductivity. In this study, we experimentally investigate in-plane low-bias conductance anisotropy in epitaxial Fe/MgO/V/MgO/Fe/Co junctions under varying temperature and magnetic field conditions. By applying a theoretical model that accounts for distinct R-SOC and D-SOC contributions, we interpret the observed anisotropy as a reliable method to disentangle these effects. Comparison between experiment and theory reveals a 5 % contribution of D-SOC to total SOC, attributed to periodic interfacial lattice mismatch defects confirmed by high-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM). Our findings offer a practical approach to isolate interfacial SOC components in superconducting spintronic systems with implications for engineering in spintronic and quantum devices, and provide further evidence of superconducting spin-triplet pairing in Fe/MgO/V-based superconductor/ferromagnet heterostructures.

Abstract

Valley Hall effect is a signature effect in the field of valleytronics, and recent studies have pushed this effect into the nonlinear regime. Here, we reveal a previously unexplored type of nonlinear valley Hall effect which arises from a valley Berry curvature dipole (vBCD) mechanism. We show this nonlinear valley Hall effect is forbidden for conventional time-reversal-connected valleys, but is supported in the class of valleytronic systems featuring time-reversal-invariant valleys. The candidate layer groups and detailed symmetry constraints on vBCD are obtained. It shows the nonlinear valley Hall response, as well as the nonlinear charge Hall response, can be well controlled by tuning the driving field direction. We demonstrate our proposal in an effective model study and in a concrete material example, strained Nb$_{3}$SBr$_{7}$, by first-principles calculations. The nonlocal transport signature of this vBCD induced nonlinear valley Hall effect is also discussed.
