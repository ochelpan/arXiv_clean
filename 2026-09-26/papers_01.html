Summary. This work introduces nonlinear spectroscopic and fluctuation-based protocols to determine the sign of quasiparticle charge carriers in strongly correlated systems, bypassing the heating issues associated with traditional Hall measurements in quantum simulators. By analyzing second-order density responses and density skewness, the authors demonstrate a direct, complementary route to characterizing carrier type in models like the Fermi-Hubbard model. This offers quantum simulators a robust diagnostic tool for fundamental condensed matter questions.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful, experimentally viable diagnostic tool for strongly correlated physics in AMO platforms, linking measurable nonlinear optical/density responses directly to fundamental electronic properties like carrier type.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of characterizing the sign of quasiparticle charge carriers in strongly correlated systems without relying on the conventional Hall effect, which is problematic in cold-atom quantum simulators due to heating.

Main result. Nonlinear spectroscopic responses (second-order density response) and equilibrium non-Gaussian fluctuations (density skewness) provide complementary, direct probes to determine the sign of the charge carriers, successfully reproducing known sign changes observed in the Hall coefficient.

Method. The authors employ theoretical modeling combined with numerical simulations, utilizing nonlinear response functions ($\chi^{(2)}$) and higher-order cumulants ($C^{(3)}$) applied to quantum simulators.

Model / system. The primary model is the Fermi-Hubbard Model (FHM), relevant to Mott physics and high-Tc cuprates. The methods are shown to be applicable to bosonic systems, such as hard-core bosons, in cold-atom quantum simulators.

Key observables. Second-order density response ($ ho_2$), equilibrium density skewness ($C^{(3)}$), and the Hall coefficient ($R_H$).

Important parameters / regimes. Doping level (especially near half-filling), temperature ($T$), and interaction strength ($U/t$).

Assumptions / limitations. The protocols assume single-site resolution is available, which is achievable in cold-atom quantum simulators, bypassing the need for complex transport measurements.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the sign determination via $ ho_2$ and $C^{(3)}$ for both fermionic (FHM) and bosonic systems, showing crossovers matching expected physical transitions.

Paper structure. The paper develops the concept by first proposing nonlinear probes to replace the Hall effect. It then applies these methods to the FHM, demonstrating the sign change upon doping, and extends the methodology to bosonic systems using nonlinear spectroscopy.

Abstract

Characterizing carrier type in strongly correlated quantum systems conventionally relies on the Hall effect. In cold-atom quantum simulators, implementing Hall transport measurements typically requires synthetic gauge fields, which introduces significant heating. Here, we present nonlinear spectroscopic and fluctuation-based protocols that establish an alternative route to identifying the sign of quasiparticle charge. We demonstrate that second-order density and current responses to finite-momentum quenches and drives-as well as equilibrium third-order density cumulants-distinguish electron- from hole-like quasiparticles. For a Fermi-Hubbard multi-leg ladder on a square lattice, numerical simulations reveal a crossover from hole- to electron-like carriers upon hole-doping away from half-filling, matching the sign change in the Hall coefficient. To demonstrate the applicability of our protocols beyond fermionic systems, we show that the nonlinear density response to a finite-momentum, finite-frequency drive reveals the sign of charge carriers in a hard-core boson ladder, and that the nonlinear signal can be significantly enhanced when driving near resonance with a nonlinear collective mode. Our results establish nonlinear density and current response and equilibrium non-Gaussian fluctuations as complementary probes, offering quantum simulators a direct route to characterize the carrier sign, without the experimental hurdles of conventional transport setups.

Summary. This paper details a theoretical scheme to achieve fast, simultaneous entangling gates between polar molecules by mediating the interaction through microwave-dressed Rydberg atoms. By carefully tuning the microwave dressing parameters, the detrimental dipolar interactions are suppressed, allowing a resonant iSWAP gate to be implemented. The results show significant speedups over direct coupling and propose error mitigation strategies using state detection.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the engineering of quantum gates in complex molecular systems using highly controllable Rydberg platforms, which is a major frontier in quantum simulation and quantum computing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper proposes a method to mediate fast, simultaneous entangling gates between pairs of polar molecules using Rydberg atoms.

Main result. The mediated gate is predicted to be significantly faster (by $>10^2$) than direct molecule-molecule gates, and gate errors can be partially mitigated using post-selection based on Rydberg atom state detection.

Method. The scheme utilizes microwave dressing to nullify detrimental dipolar interactions between Rydberg atoms and tune the Rydberg-molecule interaction into resonance to mediate a modified iSWAP gate.

Model / system. The system involves polar molecules and Rydberg atoms, modeled using Hamiltonians describing dipolar, van der Waals, and resonant molecule-Rydberg interactions. The dynamics are analyzed using master equations to account for decay and motion.

Key observables. Gate speed ($t_G$), gate infidelity ($1-F$), and post-selection success-weighted fidelity ($ar{F}_{ps}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Microwave dressing parameters ($\alpha, \Omega_\pi$), molecular and atomic separation distances ($r_{Rm}$), and interaction strengths ($J$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on engineering dressed states to achieve destructive interference of dipolar interactions, and assumes the ability to model motion and decay using master equations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the energy level diagrams for molecular and Rydberg atoms, showing the resonant excitation exchange mechanism and the microwave dressing scheme used to nullify dipole-dipole interactions.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theoretical framework by first introducing the dressing scheme to nullify Rydberg-Rydberg interactions, then deriving the resonant interaction Hamiltonian for the iSWAP gate, and finally analyzing gate performance by modeling decay and motion.

Abstract

We propose a scheme for mediating many simultaneous, fast entangling gates between pairs of polar molecules using Rydberg atoms. By using microwave drives, the dipolar interactions between Rydberg atoms are nullified. Using the freedom left in the microwave dressing parameters, the Rydberg van der Waals interactions are minimized and the Rydberg-molecule interaction is tuned into resonance, allowing for the mediation of a modified iSWAP gate between molecules. In the example of mediating gates between $\rm{{}^{23}Na {}^{133}Cs}$ molecules with $\rm {}^{133}Cs$ atoms, the gate is more than two orders of magnitude faster than an equivalent direct molecule-molecule gate. We model the decay of the dressed Rydberg states and the motion of the atom and molecules and obtain a leading-order estimate of the resulting gate infidelities. Finally, we show that by detecting the state of the Rydberg atom after a gate, a subset of gate errors can be converted into erasure errors and mitigated by post-selection or quantum error-correction.

Summary. This paper provides an exact solution for the Nonequilibrium Steady State (NESS) of a boundary-driven transverse-field Ising model by exploiting a hidden time-reversal symmetry. The method constructs the NESS using a Matrix-Product Operator (MPO) representation, showing that the steady-state properties, such as magnetization and correlations, are dictated by the local field at the dissipative boundary. This offers a powerful analytical tool for understanding non-equilibrium quantum dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides an exact, analytical framework for calculating steady states in complex, driven-dissipative quantum many-body systems, which is a major challenge in AMO and quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Determining the Nonequilibrium Steady State (NESS) of open quantum systems, which is generally a formidable numerical task.

Main result. The NESS can be exactly constructed due to the presence of hidden time-reversal symmetry, leading to an MPO representation and revealing boundary-governed spatial organization.

Method. The solution utilizes the concept of Hidden Time-Reversal Symmetry (HTRS) by constructing an exactly solvable doubled system whose pure steady state purifies the original system's NESS.

Model / system. The system is a 1D boundary-driven transverse-field Ising (TFI) model, whose dynamics are governed by a Lindblad master equation incorporating nearest-neighbor interactions, a transverse field, and boundary dissipation.

Key observables. z-magnetization and connected two-point correlations.

Important parameters / regimes. Boundary fields ($h_L, h_R$), dissipation rate ($\gamma$), and the weak-driving limit where long-range correlations can emerge.

Assumptions / limitations. The model possesses Hidden Time-Reversal Symmetry (HTRS), which is the key mathematical property enabling the exact solution.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic domain and bond structure of the NESS for small chains, and the matrix-product representation of the steady state.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical model and the difficulty of finding the NESS, then introduces the HTRS to construct an exactly solvable doubled system. The core methodology involves deriving recurrence relations for the coefficients, leading to the final MPO representation of the NESS, followed by analyzing the resulting spatial correlations in different parameter regimes.

Abstract

The dissipative transverse-field Ising (TFI) model provides a paradigmatic setting for nonequilibrium quantum many-body physics. We show that a class of boundary-driven TFI models subject to dissipation at only one boundary possesses hidden time-reversal symmetry, which enables an exact construction of their nonequilibrium steady states. The solution admits a matrix-product representation and defines a nonequilibrium partition function from which steady-state observables can be evaluated efficiently. We use the exact solution to characterize the microscopic structure of the steady state through its z-magnetization and two-point correlations. A striking feature is that the local field at the dissipative boundary governs the spatial organization of the steady state throughout the chain. The steady state typically exhibits boundary-localized magnetization profiles and exponentially decaying correlations, whose characteristic length scales are set by the dissipative boundary. In the weak-driving limit, suitably tuned boundary fields can reorganize the NESS into a delocalized single-interface structure, giving rise to long-range correlations that decay linearly with distance.

Summary. The authors propose a Variational Multi-Gaussian Quantum Trajectory method (sVMG) to simulate open quantum systems governed by a master equation. By projecting the dynamics onto a Gaussian superposition ansatz, they analytically solve the variational equations. The method successfully predicts a dimension-dependent symmetry-breaking phase transition in 2D Bose-Hubbard lattices, validating its utility for studying non-equilibrium quantum criticality.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, analytically tractable tool (sVMG) to simulate non-equilibrium quantum dynamics in many-body systems, allowing for the study of complex phenomena like quantum phase transitions beyond standard semiclassical limits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the efficient simulation of stochastic quantum dynamics in interacting bosonic systems, particularly focusing on quantum trajectories derived from a master equation.

Main result. The variational method successfully captures the emergence of a symmetry-breaking phase transition in 2D Bose-Hubbard lattices, a feature absent in 1D chains, demonstrating dimension-dependent criticality.

Method. A stochastic time-dependent variational principle (sTDVP) is employed, utilizing a wavefunction ansatz composed of a coherent superposition of Gaussian wavepackets to analytically solve the variational equations of motion.

Model / system. The system is modeled using interacting bosonic systems, specifically the Bose-Hubbard model, studied on dimers and extended lattices in 1D and 2D. Dynamics are governed by a Lindblad master equation incorporating single-body loss.

Key observables. Antibonding mode population ($\langle \hat{n}_\pi angle$) and bipartite Rényi-2 entanglement entropy ($S_2$).

Important parameters / regimes. The study explores different lattice dimensions (1D vs 2D) and varying coupling/interaction parameters relative to the dissipation rate ($\gamma$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on approximating the quantum state using a finite superposition of Gaussian wavepackets ($N_c$), and the analytical tractability stems from the Gaussian structure allowing the evaluation of the quantum geometric tensor.

Figures summary. Figure 2 compares the calculated $\langle \hat{n}_\pi angle$ and $S_2$ for the Bose-Hubbard dimer against exact solutions and other approximations. Figure 3 shows single-trajectory expectation values for 1D and 2D lattices, highlighting the 2D checkerboard pattern.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the sTDVP framework and the multi-Gaussian ansatz. It then benchmarks the method on a Bose-Hubbard dimer, demonstrating accuracy. Finally, it applies the method to 1D and 2D lattices to study phase transitions and compare results across dimensions.

Abstract

We propose a variational framework for the efficient simulation of stochastic quantum dynamics in interacting bosonic systems. Our method generalizes the time-dependent variational principle to accommodate quantum state diffusion processes and is tailored for describing quantum trajectories unraveled from a master equation. We adopt a wavefunction ansatz composed of a coherent superposition of Gaussian wavepackets, which allows the fully analytical assembly of the variational equations of motion while capturing both the quantum trajectory dynamics and the Lindblad evolution beyond semiclassical approximations. The method is carefully benchmarked on a Bose-Hubbard dimer, and applied to extended Bose-Hubbard lattices in both 1D and 2D. In particular, we show the emergence of a symmetry-breaking phase transition in 2D lattices, which is absent in 1D chains.

Summary. This paper develops a novel framework by mapping the complex entanglement dynamics of a measured qubit chain onto a simple classical stochastic cellular automaton. By analyzing this classical model, the authors analytically determine the steady-state scaling of entanglement entropy, revealing distinct volume-law, fractal, and area-law regimes controlled by the measurement parameters. This provides deep insight into how measurement processes govern quantum correlations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, non-Hamiltonian framework to study entanglement decay and steady-state properties in open quantum systems, which is highly relevant for understanding decoherence and measurement backaction in quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of analytically capturing and solving the entanglement dynamics and transitions in a quantum system monitored only by local measurements.

Main result. The entanglement structure is exactly mapped onto a classical stochastic cellular automaton, allowing the determination of steady-state entanglement scaling laws (volume-law, fractal, and area-law) based on the measurement process parameters.

Method. The authors use a mapping technique to translate quantum entanglement dynamics into a classical stochastic process (a matching problem), enabling analytical solutions in specific limits and extensive numerical simulations.

Model / system. The system is a one-dimensional qubit chain subjected to competing single-site and long-range Bell measurements, where the entanglement state is tracked by a classical matching of the sites.

Key observables. Bipartite entanglement entropy ($S_A(N_A)$), fraction of unentangled sites ($n_1(t)$), and the entanglement scaling exponent ($eta$).

Important parameters / regimes. Measurement probability ($p$), power-law exponent ($\alpha$) governing pair selection, and the system size ($N$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the entanglement dynamics can be exactly represented by a classical stochastic cellular automaton, which avoids the computational cost of trajectory postselection.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the automaton's elementary moves, the entanglement phase diagram showing volume-, fractal-, and area-law regimes, and time evolution plots comparing theoretical scaling predictions with numerical results.

Paper structure. The paper builds from defining the classical automaton mapping to deriving analytical solutions for specific limits ($\alpha=0$, $p o 1^-$), establishing the scaling laws based on $\alpha$, and finally analyzing the time evolution of the transition points between these regimes.

Abstract

We introduce a generalized classical long-range stochastic cellular automaton that exactly captures the entanglement dynamics and transitions of a measurement-only monitored quantum system. The corresponding quantum model consists of a one-dimensional qubit chain subject to competing single-site and long-range Bell measurements, with separations drawn from a power-law distribution. At every step of each trajectory, the state remains a tensor product of Bell pairs and unpaired qubits, with bipartite entanglement entropy fully encoded in a classical matching of the sites. This exact representation allows us to solve the dynamics analytically in several limits and to determine the steady-state scaling of the entanglement entropy. We find volume-law, fractal, and area-law regimes. Numerical results show that these regimes persist beyond the analytically solvable limit. Because Bell outcomes affect only Bell-state labels and not the matching, all subsystem entropies are outcome independent, avoiding the exponential cost of trajectory postselection.

Summary. This research uses a trapped-ion quantum simulator to experimentally verify the theoretical prediction of universal, hierarchical relaxation in interacting quantum systems. By measuring autocorrelation functions for operators of increasing complexity, the authors confirm that the relaxation decay rate is systematically governed by the operator's overlap order, extending fundamental concepts like the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis to systems with multiple conserved quantities.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges advanced theoretical concepts (ROI, generalized ETH) with cutting-edge experimental capabilities (trapped ions) to probe fundamental non-equilibrium dynamics, which is highly relevant for understanding quantum thermalization.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to experimentally characterize the dynamical properties and relaxation to equilibrium in interacting many-body quantum systems by demonstrating universal and hierarchical relaxation of autocorrelation functions.

Main result. The authors experimentally established the universal hierarchical relaxation regimes predicted by the saturated relaxation-overlap inequality, showing that the decay rate depends systematically on the spatial extent (overlap order) of the measured operators.

Method. The study uses a trapped-ion quantum simulator to measure time-dependent autocorrelation functions of local observables, analyzing their algebraic decay rates.

Model / system. The physical system is a one-dimensional chain of trapped $^{40} ext{Ca}^+$ ions governed by a long-range XX Hamiltonian. The theory is extended to systems conserving multiple quantities, specifically energy and total magnetization.

Key observables. Autocorrelation functions of local observables ($\langle \hat{O}(t) \hat{O}^\dagger angle_c$), relaxation exponents ($ u$), and overlap orders ($\mu$).

Important parameters / regimes. Diffusive regime, multiple conserved charges (Energy and $\hat{S}_z$), and the overlap order $\mu$ which dictates the decay exponent ($ u \propto \mu/2$).

Assumptions / limitations. The theory assumes the existence of a finite family of commuting conserved charges and postulates a generalized Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH) relation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the ion trap setup, the hierarchy of autocorrelation function decays, and data collapses confirming the universal scaling behavior of the relaxation rates for different observables.

Paper structure. The paper builds from theoretical predictions (ROI extension to multiple charges) to experimental implementation on the trapped-ion simulator, measuring correlators for increasing operator complexity (overlap order $\mu$) to confirm the predicted hierarchical decay rates.

Abstract

Autocorrelation functions play a key role in the theoretical characterization of the dynamical properties of interacting many-body quantum systems. Recently, bringing together the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis and hydrodynamics, it was theoretically predicted that the relaxation of autocorrelators can be described by the \textit{relaxation-overlap inequality}, which, when saturated, predicts a hierarchy of relaxation exponents for a set of operators that are easily identified and constructed. Here, we employ a trapped-ion quantum simulator to experimentally demonstrate it in the diffusive regime; to do so, we have extended the theory of the overlap-relaxation inequality to systems with multiple conservation laws. Our study thus opens the path to a thorough characterization of the relaxation to equilibrium and appearance of hydrodynamic behavior in quantum matter.

Summary. This work proposes realizing a thermal quasi-Devil's staircase in an anisotropic triangular-lattice Ising antiferromagnet simulated on a finite Rydberg array. By showing that thermal fluctuations overcome topological barriers, the authors demonstrate an observable sequence of winding-sector crossovers. This provides a direct, tunable platform for studying topological physics in quantum simulators.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, experimentally accessible mechanism (thermal activation in Rydberg arrays) to observe a complex topological phenomenon that is otherwise only predicted in idealized, non-interacting models.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses how to observe a Devil's staircase, a sequence of topological transitions, in a physically realizable system.

Main result. Thermal fluctuations overcome topological barriers in a finite Rydberg array, leading to an observable quasi-Devil's staircase of winding-sector crossovers.

Method. The study combines theoretical analysis (dimer mapping, exact thermodynamics) with numerical simulations (Monte Carlo) to characterize the crossover points.

Model / system. The system is an anisotropic triangular-lattice Ising antiferromagnet realized using a finite Rydberg-atom array. The low-energy physics is mapped onto dimer coverings and noncrossing strings on the dual lattice.

Key observables. Winding distribution P(W, T), structure factor S(q), and directional correlations C_alpha(r) are used to diagnose the crossover.

Important parameters / regimes. The effect is dependent on finite lattice size and tunable effective temperature, contrasting with the thermodynamic limit.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the system is restricted to configurations with exactly one frustrated bond per triangle at low temperatures.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the winding diagnostics (P(W, T)), the movement of incommensurate peaks in the structure factor S(q), and the anisotropic decay of directional correlations C_alpha(r).

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the physical system and the concept of topological protection. It then develops the theoretical framework using dimer/string mappings and calculates the free energy for different winding sectors. Finally, it uses Monte Carlo simulations and multiple diagnostics to confirm the existence and nature of the quasi-Devil's staircase.

Abstract

A Devil's staircase is a sequence of transitions between topological sectors as a control parameter is varied. Such staircases are predicted in frustrated magnets and lattice gauge theories, but observing them is hard: the winding number that labels each sector is topologically protected, so local quantum dynamics alone cannot move the system from one step to the next. Here we propose and analyze an experimentally feasible way to realize a thermal quasi-Devil's staircase in a finite Rydberg-atom array. We study the anisotropic triangular-lattice Ising antiferromagnet using a dimer mapping, a directed-string description, exact thermodynamics, and Monte Carlo simulations. In the defect-free string manifold, the commensurate--incommensurate onset follows from a simple energy--entropy balance, and the exact Ising critical relation reduces to the same condition when triangle-rule defects are rare. On a finite lattice, this onset appears as a quasi-Devil's staircase of winding-sector crossovers, which we resolve through winding distributions, structure factors, and directional correlations. The key point is that thermal fluctuations overcome the topological barriers that block sector changes under purely quantum dynamics, and the staircase exists precisely at the finite sizes and tunable effective temperatures of current Rydberg platforms. This makes the effect directly observable in existing experiments. Our comparison also shows that the finite-size steps are not phase transitions: in the thermodynamic limit only a single phase transition survives, and the system crosses over to a defect-dominated paramagnet.

Summary. This work analyzes how the uncertainty (measured by Shannon entropy) of a diffusing particle decreases when continuously monitored by noisy measurements. By mapping the problem to directed polymers and using the replica method, the authors derive distinct asymptotic scaling laws for the entropy based on how the monitoring strength decays over time. This provides a powerful theoretical tool for understanding information loss in stochastic physical systems.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between information theory (entropy), stochastic processes (diffusion), and advanced statistical mechanics tools (replica method, integrable Hamiltonians like LL) provides deep insights into non-equilibrium dynamics and measurement back-action.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper studies the Bayesian monitoring of a single particle diffusing in one dimension, aiming to quantify the remaining uncertainty using the average Shannon entropy.

Main result. The asymptotic scaling of the entropy depends critically on the power-law decay exponent $\alpha$ of the monitoring rate, yielding distinct logarithmic behaviors for $\alpha > 1/2$ versus $0 < \alpha < 1/2$.

Method. The problem is mapped onto calculating moments of directed polymer partition functions, which is solved using the replica method and related to the attractive Lieb-Liniger Hamiltonian.

Model / system. The system is a single particle undergoing diffusion on a line, monitored by noisy local measurements. The mathematical framework involves stochastic heat equations and the replica formalism.

Key observables. Average Shannon entropy $\overline{S(t)}$, which quantifies the uncertainty remaining after measurement.

Important parameters / regimes. The monitoring rate decay exponent $\alpha$ (where $c(t) \sim t^{-\alpha}$), and the critical value $\alpha = 1/2$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the model setup and plot the calculated rate of Shannon entropy versus the exponent $\alpha$, comparing different monitoring protocols.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the entropy in the diffusive monitoring problem to mapping it onto directed polymers and the Lieb-Liniger Hamiltonian. It then analyzes the asymptotic behavior of the entropy in different regimes defined by the monitoring rate's decay exponent $\alpha$.

Abstract

We study the Bayesian monitoring of a single particle diffusing along a line, while an observer tracks it continuously using noisy measurements at each spatial point. The average Shannon entropy $\overline{S(t)}$ associated with the posterior distribution of the particle's position quantifies the uncertainty that remains after conditioning on the sequence of measurements. Abstract

Random matrix spectral statistics are widely used to diagnose quantum chaos in open systems, but whether chaotic eigenvalue correlations are accompanied by Haar-typical eigenoperators remains unclear. We study the operator-entanglement statistics of Liouvillian eigenoperators with near-maximal entanglement, analogous to states near the middle of the spectrum in Hamiltonian systems. Using the Kullback-Leibler divergence, we show that Haar-random statistics accurately describe both the Ginibre and class-AI$^\dagger$ Gaussian ensembles. For purely dissipative random Lindbladians, we find that locality of the jump operators drastically alters the eigenoperator-entanglement statistics. Nonlocal Lindbladians remain relatively close to Haar-random behavior, whereas local Lindbladians deviate increasingly from random-matrix statistics with system size. Moreover, unlike in Hamiltonian systems, the most highly entangled eigenoperators of local Lindbladians do not generally lie in the region of largest density of states, owing to the clustered structure of the complex eigenspectrum. We introduce an iterative $σ$-clipping scheme to extract the high-entanglement distribution without preselecting a spectral window, and find that it is Gaussian for nonlocal Lindbladians but log-normal for local ones. Remarkably, the same log-normal distribution, with no additional fitting, also describes dissipative mixed-field Ising chains belonging to distinct non-Hermitian symmetry classes. Our results therefore point to a universal eigenoperator-entanglement distribution for local Lindbladians that is not captured by generic non-Hermitian random-matrix ensembles.
