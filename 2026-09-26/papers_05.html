Logarithmic transport at topological phase transitions of one-dimensional chiral quantum systems

Authors: Dragan Markovic, Hermann Schulz-Baldes
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28688v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper analyzes quantum transport in disordered, one-dimensional chiral quantum systems near topological phase transitions. It proves that the quantum dynamics cause the position moments to grow logarithmically with time, mirroring classical Sinai diffusion. The analysis achieves this by relating the system's lowest energy eigenstate to the exponential of a random walk.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between quantum transport, localization phenomena, and classical diffusion models (Sinai) in the presence of disorder is highly relevant to understanding energy transport in disordered quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the quantum dynamics and transport properties at disorder-driven topological phase transitions in one-dimensional chiral quantum systems.

Main result. The quantum dynamics leads to a logarithmic growth of the moments of the position operator, similar to classical Sinai diffusion, providing a rigorous lower bound for this transport.

Method. The analysis uses spectral theory, approximating the lowest energy eigenfunction by the exponential of a random walk, and deriving lower bounds for time-averaged position moments.

Model / system. The system is modeled by a random hopping Hamiltonian ($H_N$), which serves as a toy model for disorder-driven topological phase transitions in quasi-one-dimensional chiral Hamiltonians.

Key observables. Time- and disorder-averaged $q$-th moments of the position operator ($M_N^q(T)$), the smallest positive eigenvalue ($E_{N,1}$), and the eigenfunction components ($\phi_E$).

Important parameters / regimes. The time scale $T$ is analyzed in a specific regime, $e^{c_1 N^\alpha} \le T \le e^{c_2 N^\alpha}$, and the system size $N$ is crucial.

Assumptions / limitations. The proof relies on approximating the eigenstate by the exponential of a random walk and focusing on the diagonal terms in the moment expansion.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the spatial profile of the smallest eigenvalue eigenfunction and associated random walks, and compare the distributions of the maximal eigenfunction values against zero-energy state predictions.

Paper structure. The work progresses by analyzing the spectral properties of the chiral Hamiltonian, establishing the relationship between the eigenstate and random walks, and finally deriving a rigorous lower bound for the position moments using these approximations.

Abstract

The random hopping Hamiltonian is a toy model for a disorder-driven topological phase transitions in one-dimensional chiral Hamiltonians. It has a vanishing Lyapunov exponent and a Dyson peak in the density of states at zero energy. This work shows how the quantum dynamics leads to a logarithmic growth of the moments of the position operator, similar as in classical Sinai diffusion. The mechanism at the origin of this phenomenon is that the eigenfunction of the smallest eigenvalue is well-approximated by the exponential of a classical random walk, leading to two essentially independent subexponential localization centers.

Quantum Feature Selection for Biomedical Data Analysis

Authors: Hongbin Liu, Robert Lahmann, Benjamin Campbell, Zhemin Zhang, Zhiding Liang, Siona Bapat, Juergen Hahn
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30256v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work proposes using quantum optimization techniques to solve feature selection for complex biomedical data. It formulates the problem as minimizing a QUBO objective that balances feature importance and redundancy. The authors test this on quantum simulators and hardware, demonstrating feasibility while comparing performance against state-of-the-art classical methods.

Why it may be interesting. While the application is in biomedicine, the core methodology involves mapping a complex combinatorial optimization problem (feature selection) onto quantum hardware models (QUBO/Ising), which is a direct application of quantum computation theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of feature selection in high-dimensional biomedical data, specifically metabolomics, where the number of features vastly exceeds the number of samples.

Main result. The proposed QUBO formulation achieves competitive classification performance compared to classical methods and demonstrates hardware feasibility on NISQ devices, although it does not claim quantum advantage.

Method. The problem is formulated as minimizing a QUBO objective function that balances feature relevance (using Fisher score) against feature redundancy (using covariance). This optimization is solved using quantum algorithms like QAOA and BF-DCQO.

Model / system. The core model is the QUBO formulation, which is mapped onto an Ising cost Hamiltonian ($H_C$). This Hamiltonian is then solved using quantum optimization algorithms (QAOA, BF-DCQO) on a quantum gate-based computer.

Key observables. LOOCV accuracy across multiple classifiers (LDA, SVM, RF, etc.), circuit gate counts, and the convergence behavior of the quantum sampling distribution.

Important parameters / regimes. The balancing parameter $\alpha \in [0, 1]$, which weights the trade-off between feature relevance and redundancy.

Assumptions / limitations. The results are limited by current NISQ hardware constraints and do not claim a quantum advantage; the QUBO formulation only accounts for quadratic interactions.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the entire quantum feature selection pipeline from data input to quantum solver output. Figure 2 compares the gate counts required by BF-DCQO and QAOA for a 24-feature instance. Figure 7 shows the convergence of BF-DCQO sampling distribution over iterations.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the need for feature selection in metabolomics. It then details the novel QUBO formulation using Fisher scores and covariance. The methodology section describes mapping this to an Ising Hamiltonian and applying quantum solvers (QAOA, BF-DCQO) alongside classical baselines (ITS, filter/wrapper methods). Finally, results are presented comparing performance and feasibility.

Abstract

Feature selection is an essential step for reducing complexity of high dimensional data, usually in preparation for developing machine learning models such as computational biomarkers. However, there are limitations associated with feature selection such as for metabolomic data where there are hundreds or even thousands of features per study participant, while the available number of participants in a clinical trials is limited. Classical methods such as exhaustive search require evaluation of all possible feature combinations, making them costly in terms of computation and runtime, or even infeasible, as feature dimensionality increases. In this study, we propose a novel Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) coefficient formulation and pose metabolomic feature selection as a QUBO problem that selects a specified number of features by balancing their relevance against redundancy among the selected variables. To evaluate our proposed QUBO objective function, we conducted a series of experiments using Bias-Field Digitized Counterdiabatic Quantum Optimization (BF-DCQO) and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) on a quantum gate based computer. We also compared the method to several classical methods on three metabolomic datasets associated with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) on a classical computer. Our method reduces runtime compared with exhaustive search and Iterative Tabu Search (ITS) and achieves competitive performance across classifiers compared to classical filter, wrapper, and embedded methods. These results do not claim quantum advantage; rather, they establish hardware feasibility and demonstrate the current capabilities of Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) devices.

Summary. This work demonstrates the ability to selectively control the ultrafast dynamics of coupled ferroelectric and magnetic orders in $ ext{BiFeO}_3$ by applying tensile strain. Using ultrafast electron diffraction, the researchers showed that strain specifically dampens the ferroelectric response without affecting the magnetic (AFD) response. This establishes strain as a powerful tool for engineering non-equilibrium phase control in correlated multiferroics.

Why it may be interesting. The selective control demonstrated here—tuning one order parameter while preserving another—is a key concept in designing quantum materials for advanced applications, relevant to understanding coupled dynamics in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is achieving selective, non-equilibrium control over multiple coupled order parameters (like ferroelectric and magnetic) in quantum multiferroic materials.

Main result. Tensile strain selectively suppresses the ultrafast photoinduced dynamics associated with the ferroelectric order parameter, while leaving the dynamics related to the magnetic order parameter largely unaffected.

Method. The study uses in situ tensile strain tuning applied to freestanding $ ext{BiFeO}_3$ membranes, probed by MeV ultrafast electron diffraction following photoexcitation.

Model / system. The system is the multiferroic $ ext{BiFeO}_3$, which exhibits coupled ferroelectric (FE) and antiferrodistortive (AFD) orders. The experiment probes the ultrafast lattice dynamics associated with these two distinct order parameters.

Key observables. Photoinduced lattice dynamics, specifically the time evolution of FE displacement and AFD rotation, measured via changes in Bragg peak intensities ($\overline{I}_{ ext{odd}} - \overline{I}_{ ext{even}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Tunable uniaxial tensile strain ($\sim 0.2\%$), ultrafast time scales (sub-picosecond), and varying pump laser fluences.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis distinguishes between structural changes (FE/AFD reduction) and transient lattice heating (Debye-Waller effect).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental setup, the comparison of measured intensity changes against simple Debye-Waller scaling, and the time evolution of FE and AFD dynamics under varying strain and fluence.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the experimental platform, presents measurements showing the differential strain response of FE vs. AFD dynamics, and uses modeling (Ginzburg-Landau) to attribute the selective tuning to strain-mediated changes in carrier screening effects.

Abstract

Quantum materials are characterized by intertwined orders, and a fundamental goal in condensed matter physics is to achieve their selective control in such a way that one order parameter can be tuned while leaving others largely unaffected. Although significant progress has been made in thermal equilibrium, realizing such control out of equilibrium remains highly challenging. Here we employed \textit{in situ} tensile strain to selectively manipulate the photoinduced lattice dynamics associated with ferroelectric and magnetic orders in multiferroic BiFeO$_3$. By applying MeV ultrafast electron diffraction to freestanding BiFeO$_3$ membranes under tunable strain, we showed that tensile strain markedly suppresses the ultrafast photoinduced reduction of the ferroelectric displacement. By contrast, the photoinduced dynamics of the antiferrodistortive rotation of the oxygen octahedra, which modulates the magnetic order, remains insensitive to strain. Not only do these findings reveal distinct microscopic pathways underlying nonequilibrium multiferroic dynamics, they also establish tunable strain as an effective route for engineering ultrafast phase control in correlated materials.

A Block Decomposed QUBO Workflow for Chromosome-Y Phylogeny Reconstruction

Authors: Giuliana Siddi Moreau, Riccardo Berutti, Manuela Profir, Lorenzo Pisani, Maria Laura Clemente, Lidia Leoni
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29856v1

Summary. This work presents a novel computational workflow to reconstruct Y-chromosome phylogenies by formulating the problem as two QUBOs. It overcomes the limitations of single quantum processors by employing an Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) decomposition. The blocks are solved using a Digitized Counter-Diabatic Quantum Optimization (DCQO) solver, proving a scalable quantum approach for population genomics.

Why it may be interesting. The paper demonstrates a sophisticated hybrid quantum-classical approach (ADMM + DCQO) to tackle a large-scale combinatorial optimization problem, showing how quantum techniques can scale beyond current hardware limitations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the computational challenge of reconstructing the phylogeny of human Y-chromosome populations from genetic data.

Main result. The authors developed a scalable workflow that successfully applies QUBO optimization, decomposed via ADMM and solved using a DCQO quantum solver, to solve large-scale phylogenetic problems.

Method. The core method involves casting the topology and root placement decisions into two QUBO formulations, which are then solved using an ADMM decomposition strategy combined with a DCQO gate-based quantum optimizer.

Model / system. The problem is modeled using biallelic SNP data from VCF files, leading to QUBOs whose variables encode candidate splits and edges in the phylogenetic tree.

Key observables. Support Score ($\sigma(S)$), Rooting Cost ($\hat{c}_e$), and the final reconstructed Nexus-annotated rooted tree.

Important parameters / regimes. Penalty parameters $\lambda=5$, $\mu=2.5$, and $\eta=10$; the ADMM decomposition allows solving problems exceeding single QPU qubit budgets.

Abstract

This paper sets out a computational workflow that reconstructs the phylogeny of human Y-chromosome populations from a Variant Call Format (VCF) file of biallelic Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNP). The two classical phylogenetic decisions - topology selection and root placement - are cast as Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation (QUBO) problems. The workflow combines two QUBO formulations with an Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) decomposition strategy and a plug-in Digitized Counter-Diabatic Quantum Optimization (DCQO) solver, enabling large phylogenetic optimization problems to be executed across multiple classical or quantum computing resources. The DCQO gate-based optimizer drives each ADMM-block QUBO with short-depth counter-diabatic circuits in the impulse regime, without the necessity for an outer variational loop. The combination of ADMM decomposition and the DCQO block solver facilitates problem sizes that surpass the qubit budget of any individual digital quantum processing unit call, while maintaining the integrity of the original objective. The workflow extracts genotype information from VCF files, reconstructs topology and rooting through two QUBO formulations, and annotates the resulting tree using PhyloTree. The resultant data set comprises a Nexus-annotated rooted tree, in addition to diagnostic figures. The workflow demonstrates the potential of modest-scale QUBO formulations combined with ADMM decomposition to serve as a scalable alternative to greedy heuristics in the domain of population genomics.

Summary. This paper resolves a major open problem in Boolean function complexity by proving that the approximate degree of block-composed functions composes multiplicatively. The authors establish the tight bound $\widetilde{deg}(f\circ g) = \Theta(\widetilde{deg}(f)\,\widetilde{deg}(g))$ using sophisticated tools from harmonic analysis and polynomial approximation theory.

Why it may be interesting. While the topic is in computational complexity theory, the use of polynomial approximation, spectral methods, and the connection to quantum query complexity makes it highly relevant to theoretical quantum information.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the open question of whether the approximate degree of block-composed Boolean functions composes multiplicatively.

Main result. It proves that for arbitrary total Boolean functions $f$ and $g$, the approximate degree satisfies $\widetilde{deg}(f\circ g) = \Theta(\widetilde{deg}(f)\,\widetilde{deg}(g))$.

Method. The proof involves establishing a matching lower bound by using advanced techniques involving trigonometric polynomials, squarefree extraction, and constructing specific probability measures derived from dual witnesses.

Model / system. The analysis is purely mathematical, focusing on the complexity measure known as the constant-error approximate degree ($\widetilde{deg}$) for total Boolean functions $f:\{0,1\}^n o\{0,1\}$ and $g:\{0,1\}^m o\{0,1\}$.

Key observables. The approximate degree ($\widetilde{deg}$), the composition $f \circ g$, and the relationship $\widetilde{deg}(f\circ g) = \Theta(\widetilde{deg}(f)\,\widetilde{deg}(g))$.

Important parameters / regimes. The approximation error $\epsilon$ (specifically $\epsilon=1/3$), the dimensions $n$ and $m$, and the degrees of the functions.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of an approximating polynomial $Q$ and the ability to control the frequency and structure of averaged functions $R$ derived from probability measures $ ho_ heta$.

Paper structure. The paper builds from establishing upper bounds (known results) to tackling the lower bound. It develops tools using trigonometric polynomials and spectral decomposition to relate expectations over product distributions to expectations under specific, structured distributions ($ u(1+z)/2$).

Abstract

A longstanding open question in Boolean function complexity asks whether approximate degree composes multiplicatively under block composition. Although a general multiplicative upper bound is known, matching lower bounds have previously been established only for restricted classes of functions. We resolve this question for all total Boolean functions by proving the matching lower bound. Together with Sherstov's upper bound, our result shows that, for every pair of total Boolean functions $f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ and $g:\{0,1\}^m\to\{0,1\}$, \[ \widetilde{deg}(f\circ g) = Θ\!\left( \widetilde{deg}(f)\,\widetilde{deg}(g) \right), \] where $\widetilde{deg}$ denotes constant-error approximate degree.

Summary. This work establishes a rigorous connection between pseudoentanglement, a concept in quantum information theory, and the existence of cryptographic primitives called EFI pairs. By proving the equivalence between these concepts, the authors show that pseudoentanglement serves as a minimal, fundamental assumption for quantum cryptography. This bridges insights from quantum resource theory directly into the field of computational security.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a deep theoretical link between resource theories (entanglement) and computational complexity assumptions, which is foundational for understanding the limits of quantum cryptography.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the fundamental connection between the concept of pseudoentanglement and the necessary assumptions required for computational cryptography.

Main result. The existence of pseudoentanglement, under two different operational definitions, is shown to be a sufficient and necessary condition for the existence of EFI pairs, thereby bridging quantum information theory and cryptography.

Method. The authors establish mathematical relations between operational instances of pseudoentanglement and the hierarchy of minimal assumptions for computational cryptography, utilizing technical lemmas on entanglement measures and state distinguishability.

Model / system. The study focuses on quantum states, specifically families of states exhibiting pseudoentanglement, and the cryptographic primitive known as EFI pairs (efficiently preparable, statistically far, and computationally indistinguishable pairs).

Abstract

The advent of pseudoentanglement and computational entanglement theory bootstrapped a wave of research at the intersection of computer science and information theory. In parallel, computational cryptography has undergone substantial development, prompted by the introduction of pseudorandom states and followed by the establishment of a baseline for the computational hardness required for quantum cryptography, from which EFI pairs emerge as a central primitive. We study the connection between pseudoentanglement and computational cryptography through EFI pairs. Our goal is to enable the use of resources arising from computational entanglement theory in the field of cryptography. For this, we establish the relation between operational instances of pseudoentanglement and the hierarchy of minimal assumptions for computational cryptography. We show that the existence of pseudoentanglement under two different operational definitions, with efficient state generation, is a sufficient condition for the existence of EFI pairs. Combined with a previously established result that the converse also holds under the second definition, this allows us to also demonstrate their equivalence. This places pseudoentanglement alongside other minimal assumptions in cryptography, not only offering an alternative perspective on this fundamental problem, but also building a bridge that allows insights from either area to inform the other. While proving these theorems, we introduce and demonstrate technical lemmas in quantum information and computational entanglement theory, relating the computational entanglement measures to the distance between states, establishing distinguishing conditions for mixtures of two families given pairwise distances between their states, and demonstrating the first continuity relation for a computational entanglement measure.

Summary. This work analyzes the theoretical link between quantum generative models and the hardness of sampling from unknown data sources. It proves that transferring sampling hardness requires solving a difficult distribution certification problem, which demands exponentially many samples. Consequently, practitioners cannot generally assume that polynomial sampling efforts are enough to justify claiming source-level sampling hardness.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous theoretical constraint on the claims made in quantum machine learning, showing that simply training a quantum model does not guarantee that the unknown data source possesses the desirable computational hardness property.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the sampling hardness property of a quantum generative model can be reliably transferred to an unknown, real-world data-generating distribution when trained on finite classical samples.

Main result. Transferring sampling hardness requires certifying a global relationship between the two distributions, which necessitates an exponentially large number of samples for high-entropy distributions, meaning polynomial samples are generally insufficient.

Method. The authors reduce the hardness transfer problem to a distribution certification problem, utilizing existing lower bounds from identity testing and total-variation distance analysis.

Model / system. The focus is on Quantum Generative Models (QGMs) based on quantum circuit families, comparing the inherent hardness of the generator itself (generator-level hardness) versus the hardness of the unknown source distribution (source-level hardness).

Key observables. Total-Variation Distance ($d_{TV}$), Sample Requirement ($m$), Min-Entropy ($h$), and the $\ell_1$-distance threshold ($ ho$).

Important parameters / regimes. The required sample complexity is shown to scale exponentially with the entropy of the distribution, and the analysis distinguishes between the complexity of the known-target certification problem and statistical fitting.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the most favorable certification setting (known target distribution, unlimited computational power) and relies on established lower bounds for identity testing.

Abstract

Quantum generative models are often motivated by circuit families whose output distributions are believed to be classically hard to sample from. When such models are trained on ordinary classical datasets, however, this hardness does not automatically transfer to the unknown data-generating distribution. We show that transferring sampling hardness via total-variation closeness from a quantum model to an unknown source requires certifying a global relation between the two distributions, thereby reducing the problem to distribution certification. Combining this reduction with existing certification lower bounds yields an exponential sample requirement for the high-entropy distributions relevant to many sampling-hardness proposals. Consequently, polynomially many samples cannot, in general, justify attributing sampling hardness to an unknown data-generating distribution. Moreover, even classically trivial distributions, such as the uniform distribution and product distributions, require exponentially many samples to certify in the absence of structural assumptions. Our results clarify the role of sampling hardness in quantum generative modeling and distinguish generator-level hardness from source-level hardness when learning from ordinary datasets.
