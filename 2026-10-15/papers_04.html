Altermagnetic Ground States on the Ammann--Beenker Tiling

Authors: SeungBeom Hong, YunHo Kim, Moon Jip Park, GiBaik Sim
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18441v1

Summary. This paper determines the exact altermagnetic ground state of a Heisenberg model on the Ammann-Beenker quasicrystal. The authors find that the magnetic order locks into a coplanar, canted state with specific spin-group symmetry. This magnetic structure is then experimentally verified by calculating spin-resolved electronic transport, which exhibits characteristic g-wave symmetry.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a concrete, geometric realization of altermagnetism in a non-periodic system, linking the exact magnetic ground state structure to measurable quantum transport signatures (g-wave symmetry).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to identify and characterize the exact altermagnetic ground states of a classical Heisenberg model defined on the geometrically complex Ammann-Beenker quasicrystal tiling.

Main result. The system exhibits a coplanar altermagnetic order characterized by an orthogonal locking of the two primary ordering axes ($\phi_{AB} = \pi/2$), which is confirmed by spin-resolved transport measurements showing g-wave symmetry.

Method. The authors combine analytical energy minimization techniques with detailed electronic structure calculations using the Green's function formalism to probe spin-resolved transport.

Model / system. The system is a classical Heisenberg spin model on the Ammann-Beenker tiling, which is derived from projecting a 4D hypercubic lattice. The model is analyzed by coupling the spins to itinerant electrons via an onsite exchange interaction.

Key observables. Spin-resolved transmission ($\Delta T^{\hat{a}}$), spin-group symmetry ($[C_{2x} \parallel C_{8z}]$), and the angle ($\phi_{AB}$) between the magnetic ordering axes.

Important parameters / regimes. The coupling ratio parameterized by $ heta$ ($J_1 = \sin heta, J_4 = \cos heta$), and the contact rotation angle $\phi$ for transport calculations.

Assumptions / limitations. The electronic structure analysis relies on coupling the system to semi-infinite leads (Source S and Drain D) and assuming the ground state spin configuration is determined by energy minimization.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the AM/N phase structure, the orthogonal locking of ordering axes via second-order canting, and the dependence of spin-resolved transmission ($\Delta T^{\hat{z}}$ and $\Delta T^{\hat{x}}$) on the contact angle $\phi$.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the magnetic ground state by analyzing the Hamiltonian's parity structure and finding the minimum energy configuration ($\phi_{AB} = \pi/2$). It then transitions to electronic structure calculations, using the determined spin configuration to calculate spin-resolved transport properties.

Abstract

We identify exact altermagnetic ground states of a classical Heisenberg model on a quasicrystal. Considering the Ammann--Beenker tiling as an example, we determine the ground states by exploiting the parity structure of its parent four-dimensional hypercubic lattice. The ground state hosts coplanar altermagnetic order characterized by the preserved spin-group symmetry $[C_{2x}\parallel C_{8z}]$. Without translational symmetry, the momentum-space spin splitting that usually identifies altermagnets is not directly accessible. We instead couple the spins to itinerant electrons and show spin-resolved transport with $g$-wave symmetry, confirming the altermagnetic nature in a nonperiodic system. Our results establish a geometric route to altermagnetic order in quasicrystals, connecting exact magnetic ground states to their spin-resolved transport signatures.

Curie-Weiss cell fluid model: Fitting to experimental critical-point data

Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18411v1

Summary. This paper applies the Curie-Weiss cell fluid model to analyze the critical and triple-point behavior of various real fluids. By fitting the model's microscopic parameters to experimental critical data, the authors demonstrate that the model provides a good description, particularly for large molecules, and can be successfully extended to describe complex phase coexistence regions.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical fluids, the methodology of parameterizing complex many-body interactions (like the Curie-Weiss term) and mapping them onto measurable macroscopic phase transitions is relevant for developing effective Hamiltonians in quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To establish a quantitative link between the microscopic parameters of the Curie-Weiss cell fluid model and the macroscopic critical and triple-point data of real fluids.

Main result. The model shows the best agreement with experimental data for polyol ester lubricants, suggesting its suitability for soft-matter systems, and can be generalized to describe the triple point region.

Method. The authors fit the model's microscopic parameters ($J_1, v$) by matching the model's predictions for the critical temperature and density to experimental values.

Model / system. The study uses the Curie-Weiss cell fluid model, characterized by global attraction ($J_1$) and local repulsion ($J_2$), applied to real fluids like water, $ ext{CO}_2$, and polyol ester lubricants.

Key observables. Critical temperature ($T_c$), critical density ($ ho_c$), critical pressure ($P_c$), critical compressibility factor ($Z_c$), and the ratio $T_c/T_{tr}$.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio of local repulsion to global attraction ($f=J_2/J_1$), and the temperature- and phase-dependent attraction parameters ($\alpha_\pi$).

Assumptions / limitations. The model relies on a mean-field approximation, and initial parameter fitting is done using only the critical point data, requiring generalization to address the triple point.

Figures summary. The notes mention figures illustrating the phase diagram with coexistence lines and the comparison of model predictions versus experimental data for critical and triple points.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the model parameters using critical point data, noting deviations in $P_c$ and $Z_c$. It then generalizes the model by introducing temperature-dependent attraction to successfully describe the triple point and reproduce the range of $T_c/T_{tr}$ observed in real fluids.

Abstract

We examine how the Curie--Weiss cell fluid model can be quantitatively related to real fluids by determining its microscopic parameters from experimental critical-point data for water, carbon dioxide, and three polyol ester lubricants (POE5, POE7, and POE9). The microscopic parameters of the model are determined from the experimental values of the critical temperature and critical density: the global attraction coupling constant is fixed by the critical temperature, and the cell volume by the critical density. The critical pressure then follows as an independent model prediction, deviating from experimental values by 10--41$\%$ across the studied substances. Varying the ratio of local repulsion to global attraction $f=J_2/J_1$ over a wide range has only a weak effect on this deviation. For $f\geq1$, the model's critical compressibility factor lays within a narrow range $Z_c\approx0.382-0.386$, remaining close to the mean-field van der Waals value $Z_c=3/8=0.375$ and consistently overestimating experimental values. The best agreement with experiment is obtained for the polyol ester lubricants and improves along the POE5--POE7--POE9 series with increasing molar mass, suggesting that multiple-occupancy cell fluid models are well suited to systems of large molecules, particularly soft-matter systems with bounded interaction potentials. We further show that generalizing the model by introducing a temperature- and phase-dependent effective attraction allows it to describe a triple point and to reproduce the broad range of critical-to-triple temperature ratios $T_c/T_{\mathrm{tr}}$ characteristic of real fluids.

Imaging the topological sign of a Dirac mass with spin-polarized tunneling

Summary. This paper presents a novel method to locally determine the sign of the Dirac mass in magnetic topological materials using spin-polarized scanning tunneling microscopy. By exploiting crystalline rotation symmetry, the technique allows researchers to map the sign of the Berry curvature in real space, moving beyond merely identifying where a spectral gap exists. This capability is crucial for understanding the full topological character of materials.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, measurable spectroscopic signature ($ ext{sgn}(\Omega_{ ext{occ}})$) that goes beyond simple gap measurements, offering a powerful tool for mapping topological phase transitions in real space, which is highly relevant for understanding topological materials in condensed matter physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop a local spectroscopic technique capable of determining the topological sign of a Dirac mass ($M$), which is crucial because current methods only measure the gap magnitude $|M|$.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that spin-resolved tunneling, particularly when exploiting crystalline rotation symmetry on a $C_3$ axis, can directly and locally measure the sign of the occupied-band Berry curvature, $ ext{sgn}(\Omega_{ ext{occ}})$.

Method. The method combines theoretical modeling of magnetic topological surfaces with proposed experimental measurements using spin-polarized scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) spectroscopy.

Model / system. The study models magnetic topological surfaces exhibiting a Dirac cone gapped by an exchange mass $M$. The analysis uses tight-binding models, continuum Hamiltonians, and incorporates effects from spin-polarized tunneling tips and domain wall structures.

Key observables. Spin asymmetry $A(E)$, the sign of the Berry curvature ($ ext{sgn}(\Omega_{ ext{occ}})$), and the energy/spin characteristics of chiral domain-wall modes.

Important parameters / regimes. The mass $M$, the tip polarization $P$, the crystalline symmetry axis ($C_3$), and the disorder strength ($\delta M$).

Assumptions / limitations. The technique relies on the existence of crystalline rotation symmetry ($C_n$ axis) to simplify the spin-orbital entanglement and ensure the band-edge state is fully spin-polarized.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the readout mechanism using spin-polarized tips on $C_3$ axes, showing spin reversal with $M$, and confirming the relationship between measured spin asymmetry and the Berry curvature sign. Other figures detail domain wall physics and the robustness of the sign measurement against disorder.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the physical necessity of measuring the mass sign to detailing the symmetry-protected measurement protocol using spin-polarized STM, and finally validating the technique by analyzing domain walls and disordered systems.

Abstract

A Dirac mass is characterized not only by its magnitude but also by its sign, which influences the Berry curvature, Hall response, and the propagation direction of chiral boundary modes. However, local spectroscopy of magnetic topological surfaces has so far accessed only the gap magnitude $|M|$. Here we show how the topological sign of $M$ can be determined locally. We first establish the sign information that spin-resolved tunneling can access and the reference required to obtain an absolute Hall sign. Although spin--orbital entanglement generally obscures the relation between measured spin and Dirac pseudospin, crystalline rotation symmetry alleviates this ambiguity at the band edge. On a $C_3$ axis, an axial tip couples to a fully spin-polarized state whose tunneling asymmetry directly reveals the sign of the occupied-band Berry curvature, independent of orbital composition. We further show that the zeroth Landau level provides an independent mass-sign measurement with an unpolarized tip, while opposite domains and their chiral domain-wall modes provide self-consistency checks. These results pave the way for imaging the locations where the topological character changes within a material, rather than merely identifying where a spectral gap appears.

Pairing from repulsive interactions in twisted WSe2 from functional renormalization

Summary. This work analyzes superconductivity in twisted WSe2, showing that pairing arises from inter-valley coherent spin fluctuations acting as the pairing glue. Using FRG and RPA, the authors demonstrate that the superconducting state is a mixed d-wave/p-wave pairing, and their continuum model successfully predicts this superconductivity persists even at zero displacement field, aligning with experimental findings.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed theoretical mapping of pairing mechanisms in 2D van der Waals heterostructures using FRG is highly relevant for understanding emergent superconductivity in correlated quantum materials, bridging theory to experimental measurements.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the superconducting instability and pairing mechanism in twisted bilayer WSe2, particularly how superconductivity emerges from repulsive interactions.

Main result. The pairing instability is driven by inter-valley coherent spin fluctuations, leading to a mixed d-wave singlet and p-wave triplet superconducting state. The continuum model successfully predicts superconductivity down to zero displacement field, matching experiments.

Method. The authors employ advanced many-body techniques, including Functional Renormalization Group (FRG), Random Phase Approximation (RPA), and Hartree-Fock calculations, comparing results across different theoretical models.

Model / system. The system is twisted bilayer WSe2 (tWSe2) at hole filling $\nu = -1$. Calculations utilize continuum models derived from first principles, comparing them to realistic three-orbital Wannier models with Hubbard and Coulomb interactions.

Key observables. Superconducting critical temperature ($T_c$), pairing symmetry (mixed d-wave/p-wave), and the presence/strength of inter-valley-coherent spin-density-wave (IVC-SDW) order.

Important parameters / regimes. Hole filling $\nu = -1$, twist angle $\theta = 3.65^\circ$, displacement field ($D$), and interaction strength ($10/\epsilon$ or $\alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. Approximations include the Hubbard approximation (neglecting momentum/orbital dependence) and the assumption that RPA fluctuations capture the main pairing glue contribution.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate FRG phase diagrams showing the competition between IVC-SDW and superconducting regimes, and display the structure of the leading pairing eigenfunction, confirming its d-wave/p-wave mixed character.

Paper structure. The paper systematically compares results from multiple theoretical frameworks (HF, RPA, FRG) applied to the tWSe2 system. It first establishes the existence of competing ordered states (SDW) and then pinpoints the pairing glue by comparing FRG results with RPA projections, culminating in the continuum model's agreement with experimental observations.

Abstract

Motivated by the observation of superconductivity in twisted bilayer WSe$_2$ over an extended range of displacement fields, reaching from the correlated insulator down to zero field, we analyze the correlated electron phases of this system at hole filling $ν= -1$ and twist angle $3.65^\circ$ using Hartree--Fock, random phase approximation, and functional renormalization group calculations. We derive a continuum model with gate-screened Coulomb interaction from first principles and compare its instabilities to those of a realistic three-orbital Wannier model with a Hubbard interaction and an artificial single-orbital Hubbard model. In both cases, we find a superconducting instability of mixed $d$-wave singlet and $p$-wave triplet character, arising adjacent to inter-valley-coherent spin-density-wave order in the phase diagram spanned by displacement field and interaction strength. Our additional analysis of the pairing mechanism points to inter-valley coherent spin fluctuations as the pairing glue. The extent of the pairing regime and the associated critical temperatures depend on the model input, and we trace differences to the interaction range entering the projected interaction. Within the continuum model, the gate-screened Coulomb interaction yields pairing down to zero displacement field, bringing theory into agreement with experiment.

Quantum Reference Frames in terms of Tetrads

Authors: Jeremy Butterfield, Daniel A. Turolla Vanzella
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17225v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

Summary. This paper develops a rigorous mathematical framework for Quantum Reference Frames (QRFs) by treating the reference frame itself as a quantum object defined over the space of tetrads. It uses fibre bundle theory to distinguish between scenarios where the QRF superposes perspectives on one geometry versus superposing over multiple possible geometries. The work culminates in deriving a unique, invariant volume element on the general frame bundle.

Why it may be interesting. While highly mathematical, the concept of a QRF is foundational for quantum gravity and quantum field theory in curved spacetime, making it relevant to theoretical physics concerned with spacetime structure.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper adapts the relativistic concept of a reference frame as a tetrad to the quantum domain, formalizing the state of a Quantum Reference Frame (QRF). It aims to distinguish between superpositions of perspectives on a single geometry versus superpositions of geometries themselves.

Main result. The formalism is constructed using the language of fibre bundles, distinguishing between the orthonormal frame bundle (for fixed geometry) and the general frame bundle (for variable geometry). The construction yields a unique, invariant volume element on the general frame bundle.

Method. The authors use differential geometry, specifically analyzing the structure and invariance of volume elements on the frame bundle $F(M)$ by stipulating that the basis field acts as a tetrad field.

Model / system. The system is the Quantum Reference Frame (QRF), whose state is proposed to be a complex amplitude assignment over the space of tetrads. The mathematical framework is built upon fibre bundles over spacetime, specifically the frame bundle $F(M)$.

Key observables. The state of the QRF, which is a complex function on the relevant set of tetrads, and the expectation values of observables derived from coupling the QRF to measured systems.

Important parameters / regimes. The choice between the orthonormal frame bundle (restricted QRF) and the general frame bundle (general QRF) dictates the physical interpretation of the superposition.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumptions involve treating the reference frame via tetrads, and mathematically, the determination of the invariant volume element relies on stipulating orthonormality (a tetrad condition) to fix the constant in the Haar measure.

Paper structure. The paper is structured conceptually, outlining the proposal's scope by contrasting the restricted (single geometry) and general (superposition of geometries) QRFs. It then delves into the mathematical details of constructing the invariant volume element on the general frame bundle $F(M)$.

Abstract

We adapt to the context of quantum theory the treatment, familiar in relativity theory, of a reference frame as a tetrad. Since a tetrad is a set of four orthonormal vectors at a spacetime point, it encodes a timelike direction, three orthogonal spatial directions, and units; and so it gives a configuration in spacetime (a spatial orientation and state of motion) of an idealized infinitesimal apparatus (rulers and a clock"). So a naturalfirst guess" is that the state of a quantum reference frame should be an assignment of complex amplitudes to a set of tetrads. We develop this idea, in terms of bundles over spacetime of bases of the tangent spaces. This paper is a companion to two other papers. It emphasises the main conceptual aspects of our proposal, while the other two papers give physical details. It also explains the main contrast between those two papers. Namely: (i) the first considers a single spacetime geometry, so that a quantum reference frame, on our proposal, gives a superposition of perspectives on that geometry; while (ii) the second considers the spacetime manifold with no geometry given a priori, which will imply that a quantum reference frame, on our proposal, can describe a superposition of perspectives on a superposition of geometries---not ``just" a superposition of perspectives on a single geometry. The main mathematical idea for representing this contrast will be choosing between (i) the orthonormal frame bundle, for a given spacetime geometry; (ii) the frame bundle in the usual sense of containing all bases of all spacetime points' tangent spaces.

Nearly Optimal Quantum Sample Complexity of Learning Fourier-Sparse Boolean Functions

Authors: Zekun Ye
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.17648v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes the quantum sample complexity required to learn Boolean functions whose Fourier spectrum is sparse. It achieves a near-optimal quantum sample bound by combining techniques like multilinear majority polynomials for support detection and established sparse recovery theorems for coefficient estimation. This advances the understanding of quantum data requirements for learning structured functions.

Why it may be interesting. The techniques developed for robust quantum learning and sparse recovery are fundamental tools applicable to quantum machine learning models or characterizing quantum states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the minimum number of uniform quantum examples required to exactly learn a Boolean function that is sparse in its Fourier representation.

Main result. It establishes a near-optimal quantum sample complexity of O(k * Lambda(k)) for learning k-Fourier-sparse functions, significantly improving previous bounds.

Method. The approach involves constructing a candidate set containing the true Fourier support using multilinear majority polynomials and then applying sparse recovery theorems.

Model / system. The model involves Boolean functions f: {0, 1}^n -> {-1, 1} sampled via quantum states, analyzed using Fourier analysis techniques.

Key observables. The primary observable is the sample complexity (number of quantum examples) needed for exact learning.

Important parameters / regimes. Key parameters include the sparsity bound k, the input dimension n, and the desired failure probability delta.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the ability to perform separate measurements on individual examples and relies on established results like the Candès–Plan recovery theorem.

Paper structure. The paper is structured around two main parts: first, constructing a candidate set containing the true Fourier support using random approximations; second, using this set to exactly recover the unknown sparse Fourier coefficients.

Abstract

We study the number of uniform quantum examples needed to exactly learn a Boolean function with at most $k$ nonzero Fourier coefficients. We give a learner with near-optimal $\widetilde{O}(k)$ sample complexity independent of the input length $n$. The learner uses only separate measurements of individual examples. This improves the previous $O(k^{3/2}\log^2 k)$ upper bound (Arunachalam, Chakraborty, Lee, Paraashar, and de Wolf, 2021). The main ingredient is a candidate set formed by symmetric differences of subsets from independent groups of Fourier samples. A multilinear majority polynomial combines unbiased random approximations into an accurate witness whose Fourier support lies in this set. Granularity then forces the set to contain the entire Fourier support, including subsets never directly sampled. For comparison, we derive the tight classical sample complexity $Θ(nk)$ by combining known sparse-recovery and lower-bound results.

Summary. This paper investigates the second law of thermodynamics for closed quantum systems by defining and analyzing the quantum Boltzmann entropy. It develops formalisms—including weak and strong second laws—and tests them on various model Hamiltonians. The results confirm that entropy tends to increase, suggesting that macroscopic irreversibility is a robust feature of quantum dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles the quantum foundations of thermodynamics, providing rigorous theoretical tools to understand how macroscopic irreversibility emerges from unitary quantum evolution, which is central to open quantum systems theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the formulation and confirmation of precise versions of the second law of thermodynamics for closed macroscopic quantum systems.

Main result. The analysis suggests that the weak and strong second laws hold for several model systems, with probability currents showing a tendency to flow from smaller to larger dimension macro spaces as time progresses.

Method. The methodology involves analyzing the time evolution of the probability distribution across macrostate subspaces using concepts like probability currents and deriving approximate master equations.

Model / system. The system is a closed macroscopic quantum system evolving unitarily in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, decomposed into orthogonal macrostate subspaces. Models include random block-band Hamiltonians and those based on Gaussian Unitary Ensembles (GUE).

Key observables. Quantum Boltzmann Entropy ($S_{qB}$), probability distribution weights ($p_ u(t)$), and probability currents ($\delta_\ell(t)$ and $\delta_r(t)$).

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension of the Hilbert space ($D$), the ratio of dimensions of macrospaces ($d_ u/D$), and the time scale ($t o \infty$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the assumption of a macrostate decomposition, the use of approximations (like the Markovian approximation), and taking limits ($D o \infty$ or $t o \infty$).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the time evolution of probability flow functions, such as $\varphi_2^1(t) = (J_1(2t)/t)^2$, showing a predominantly decreasing trend, and comparing positive versus negative contributions to probability flow.

Paper structure. The paper first defines the quantum Boltzmann entropy and the two proposed second laws. Abstract

We consider a closed macroscopic quantum system in a pure state $ψ$ evolving unitarily with Hamiltonian $H$ in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathscr{H}$. Following von Neumann, we assume a decomposition of $\mathscr{H}$ into mutually orthogonal subspaces $\mathscr{H}_ν$, where each $ν$ represents a macro state, and $\mathscr{H}_ν$ contains the pure states compatible with $ν$. We define the quantum Boltzmann entropy for a macro state $ν$ as $S_\mathrm{qB}(ν) = k_{\mathrm{B}} \log \dim \mathscr{H}_ν$, where $k_\mathrm{B}$ is the Boltzmann constant. Goldstein et al. [http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.11870] have suggested, as a version of the second law of thermodynamics, that $S_\mathrm{qB}$ predominantly increases with time for realistic macroscopic systems. Here, we discuss precise versions of this statement and confirm them for several simple models of $H$ and $\mathscr{H}_ν$.

Summary. This paper introduces QEDIA, a novel framework in Lean 4 designed to formally verify quantum programs that mix classical control flow with quantum operations. It achieves this by formalizing an extended Quantum Hoare Logic (QHL+) that consistently handles the classical-quantum interplay. This advancement provides a powerful, automated tool for ensuring the correctness of complex quantum algorithms.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, machine-checkable methodology for verifying complex quantum algorithms, which is crucial for building reliable quantum hardware and software stacks.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is the formal verification of quantum programs that involve complex interleaving between classical control flow and quantum computation.

Main result. The authors present QEDIA, a novel framework built in Lean 4 that formalizes and proves the soundness of an extended Quantum Hoare Logic (QHL+) capable of handling this classical-quantum interplay.

Method. The method involves developing a formal proof assistant library based on QHL+ and utilizing an LLM-based prover agent to automate the construction of the formal proofs for various quantum circuits.

Model / system. The system models general quantum programs, including circuits for the Quantum Fourier Transform, Phase Estimation, and Hamiltonian simulation, where classical variables dictate gate execution and measurement outcomes influence subsequent classical steps.

Important parameters / regimes. The framework handles parameters related to circuit depth, error bounds ($\epsilon$), and the structure of classical control flow (e.g., loop invariants).

Abstract

Practical quantum programs interleave quantum and classical computation: classical data shape the quantum operations that are performed, and measurement outcomes shape the classical computation that follows. Formal verification of such programs in a proof assistant requires mechanizing a program logic that handles this interplay consistently at every level, from the mathematics of quantum mechanics, through program syntax and semantics, to specifications and proof rules. Such mechanizations, along with the program proofs built on them, typically require substantial manual effort. We present QEDIA, a framework for verifying quantum programs with classical variables in Lean 4. At its foundation is an open library that formalizes the quantum Hoare logic QHL+ across all these levels and proves the logic sound for both partial and total correctness. We construct the library and the program proofs, under human supervision, using an LLM-based prover agent, whose general-purpose core we extend for quantum program verification. We illustrate QEDIA with verified programs including the quantum Fourier transform, phase estimation, repeat-until-success circuits, and Hamiltonian simulation.

Abstract

Quantum Machine Learning (QML) has a theoretical advantage over classical algorithms for specific synthetic data that is computationally hard. However, it remains unclear whether such theoretical advantages play a meaningful role for realistic classical data, such as natural images. Quantum structural properties induced by unitary operations or entanglement introduce inductive biases that differ from those of classical models, potentially leading to distinct learning behavior on real-world data. In a fully quantum learning pipeline, classical real-world data are first encoded into quantum states via a quantum feature mapping, analogous to a classical feature extractor, and a quantum classifier then classifies the resulting states. Quantum feature mappings generate representations that are difficult to realize classically, while also inducing representation-level biases that do not necessarily match the structure of realistic data. To enable a fair comparison at the classifier level, we focus on the classifier and use classical feature extractors that already provide powerful representations for natural images. By controlling the data scale and supervision strength, we analyze how the unitary structure of quantum classifiers affects generalization, and show that their effectiveness depends strongly on the geometry of the input representations. These results indicate that quantum classifiers benefit from classical features that are aligned with quantum feature spaces. Our analysis shows that kernel alignment identifies representations compatible with quantum feature spaces, providing insights into translating classical models into quantum algorithms.

Abstract

Dissipation dilution has driven a revolution in the quality factors ($Q$) of top-down nanomechanical resonators. Yet it has not been leveraged for van der Waals (vdW) materials. Here we report quality factors up to $372{,}000\pm37{,}000$ in monolayer MoS$_2$ drums at 4 K, nearly an order of magnitude beyond the previous best for any vdW material at this temperature, enabled by a clean, polymer-free exfoliation technique. We develop a dissipation-dilution model for circular drums and analyze the contributing intrinsic and extrinsic dissipation mechanisms. The MoS$_2$ monolayer exhibits a dilution factor of order $1,000$, establishing sub-nanometer-thin resonators as ultimate dilution materials. The intrinsic quality factor, however, is limited by surface losses at atomic thickness. Conversely, multilayer drums show a lower dilution factor but a high intrinsic quality factor, on par with established top-down materials of similar dimensions, both resulting from the increased thickness. A two-level-system analysis of the temperature-dependent damping confirms this bulk-versus-surface picture and provides first insight into its microscopic origin. Our results identify dissipation dilution as the governing mechanism for high-$Q$ mechanical vdW resonators. Combined with strategies such as soft clamping or stress engineering, they establish atomically thin resonators as a platform for ultrahigh-$Q$ quantum transduction and sensing.
