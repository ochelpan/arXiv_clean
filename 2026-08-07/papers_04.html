Quantum-information fingerprints of partial dynamical symmetry in the interacting boson model

Authors: Dhritimalya Roy
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04486v1

Summary. This work introduces the 'label variance' ($ ext{Var}\,\C[G]$) as a novel and robust quantum information diagnostic to detect Partial Dynamical Symmetry (PDS) in the Interacting Boson Model. It demonstrates that this structural measure successfully distinguishes between solvable and mixed eigenstates, unlike standard entanglement measures. The authors validate this method by encoding the model onto qubits, paving the way for its direct evaluation on near-term quantum hardware.

Why it may be interesting. The development of label variance as a measurable, structure-revealing quantum invariant provides a powerful tool for classifying complex many-body systems whose symmetries are only partially preserved in nature or theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining if Partial Dynamical Symmetry (PDS) possesses a purely structural signature within quantum eigenstates, independent of entanglement magnitude.

Main result. The label variance ($ ext{Var}\,\C[G]$) serves as this robust, fit-independent diagnostic, successfully separating solvable states (zero variance) from mixed states ($\mathcal{O}(N^2)$).

Method. The authors compare structural diagnostics like label variance against entanglement measures and test the method's feasibility by encoding the Hamiltonian onto a qubit register for variational state preparation.

Model / system. The analysis is based on the Interacting Boson Model (IBM) applied to even-even nuclei, specifically anchored in $^{168} ext{Er}$. The model Hamiltonian supports various symmetry limits, including those exhibiting PDS.

Key observables. Label variance ($ ext{Var}\,\C[G]$), block-coherence entropy ($S_G$), and block impurity ($P_G$).

Important parameters / regimes. The total boson number $N$, and the Hamiltonian parameters defining critical points (e.g., $eta_0$ for first/second order transitions).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the label variance correctly captures the structural distinction between solvable and mixed spectral subsets, even when entanglement measures fail to do so.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how label variance distinguishes solvable (rigid) from mixed states, and later show the successful application of VQE/VQD protocols on a qubit register to prepare these distinct eigenstates.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical equivalence between vanishing variance, zero entropy, and unit purity for PDS. It then compares this structural diagnostic against entanglement measures using the IBM spectrum. Finally, it details the implementation by encoding the model onto qubits and preparing states variationally on a quantum device.

Abstract

Partial dynamical symmetry (PDS) is an algebraic structure in which a prescribed symmetry is neither exact nor completely broken: a subset of eigenstates keeps good quantum numbers and remains solvable while the rest of the spectrum mixes. PDS is currently identified from spectroscopic data, band-head energies, level systematics, and $B(E2)$ ratios. We ask whether it also has a purely structural signature in the eigenstates, and find that it does, though not in the magnitude of entanglement. The natural diagnostic is the variance of a symmetry Casimir, the label variance $\Var\,\C[G]$, which we show coincides with a block-coherence entropy and a block impurity: all three vanish exactly when a state carries a single irreducible-representation label. Resolved state by state, this quantity is zero on the solvable subset and of order $N^2$ on the mixed states, at stable symmetry points and at Leviatan's first- and second-order critical points, where it takes two distinct forms set by the order of the transition. The magnitude of bipartite entanglement, by contrast, does not separate solvable from mixed states and drifts even where the labels are exact. We anchor the analysis in $^{168}$Er, connect the block purity to the ``purity/coherence'' language of the quasi-dynamical-symmetry literature, and show the label variance is uncorrelated with multipartite entanglement and with magic. Finally we encode the model on a qubit register and prepare its solvable and mixed eigenstates variationally, as a step toward evaluating the diagnostic on a quantum device.

Main problem. The study establishes scaling relations governing the equilibrium thermodynamics, specifically caloric and magnetic responses, for non-Hermitian Dirac fermions subjected to a magnetic field.

Main result. The core finding is that spectral compression allows complex Non-Hermitian thermodynamic quantities (like chemical potential and heat capacity) to be mapped onto rescaled forms of their Hermitian counterparts using an effective magnetic field.

Method. The analysis employs similarity transformations to relate the quasi-Hermitian Hamiltonian to an effective Hermitian model, allowing the use of standard statistical mechanics techniques on the transformed spectrum.

Model / system. The system is modeled by real-spectrum Non-Hermitian (NH) Dirac fermions, often represented using graphene as a platform, and analyzed in the presence of a magnetic field. The Hamiltonian is quasi-Hermitian but maps to an effective Hermitian model with reduced velocity.

Key observables. Chemical potential ($\mu$), entropy ($S$), heat capacities, orbital magnetic response, and the grand partition function ($\Xi$).

Important parameters / regimes. The non-Hermiticity parameter $eta$ (where $|eta| < 1$), temperature ($T$), chemical potential ($\mu$), and applied magnetic field ($B$). Scaling factors like $\lambda = 1 / (1 - eta^2)$ are crucial.

Paper structure. Abstract

We establish scaling relations governing the equilibrium thermodynamics of real-spectrum non-Hermitian Dirac fermions in a magnetic field. Assuming thermalization with respect to the quasi-Hermitian Hamiltonian, a similarity transformation maps the system at the same applied field onto a Hermitian Dirac model with reduced velocity, while the Landau-level spectrum also admits a representation in terms of a reduced effective magnetic field. This structure yields scaling relations for the chemical potential, entropy, heat capacities, and orbital magnetic response in different thermodynamic ensembles. At fixed projected filling factor (PFF), the self-consistent chemical potential follows the compressed ladder of Landau levels, and the canonical thermodynamic functions are rescaled Hermitian responses. At fixed chemical potential, Landau-level crossings generate oscillatory caloric and magnetic responses governed by the same spectral compression. Quasistatic non-Hermitian deformation at fixed PFF further yields adiabatic temperature scaling. More broadly, these results establish a thermodynamic framework for real-spectrum non-Hermitian quantum matter and provide a starting point for incorporating the effects of interactions and disorder within the same formalism.

Summary. This theoretical paper establishes a 'Toda–Arnoldi correspondence' showing that the evolution of non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, tracked via Arnoldi reduction, is governed by the mathematics of the two-dimensional Toda lattice. It provides deep insights into how geometric quantities such as the Berry curvature and Fubini–Study metric are encoded in the system's spectral coefficients.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects non-Hermitian quantum dynamics (relevant to open systems) directly to integrable systems theory (Toda lattice), providing powerful mathematical tools to analyze spectral flow and geometric invariants like the Chern number.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper establishes a deep mathematical correspondence between the dynamics generated by non-Hermitian Hamiltonians via Arnoldi reduction and the structure of the two-dimensional Toda lattice.

Main result. It proves that Krylov Gram determinants satisfy the $ au$-function relations of the 2D Toda lattice, linking geometric quantities like the Fubini–Study metric and Berry curvature to these integrable systems.

Method. The analysis combines concepts from numerical linear algebra (Arnoldi reduction), complex geometry (holomorphic deformations, Grassmannians), and soliton theory (Toda lattice $ au$-functions).

Model / system. The study concerns a fixed, finite-dimensional, generally non-Hermitian Hamiltonian $H$ whose evolution is tracked using holomorphic deformation of a cyclic state vector. The mathematical framework involves the Krylov subspace generated by this system.

Key observables. Krylov Gram determinants ($ au_n$), Arnoldi coefficients ($\alpha_n, eta_n$), Fubini–Study metric components ($b_n^2$), and Berry curvature.

Important parameters / regimes. The complex deformation parameter $z$ and the dimensionality of the Hamiltonian/subspace. The correspondence is restricted to cyclic states.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes a cyclic seed state vector and relies on the validity of the Arnoldi method, noting breakdowns when the Krylov dimension drops.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the connection between holomorphic evolution $ ightarrow$ Krylov data $\leftrightarrow$ 2D Toda variables; they also show Stokes-Gauss relations linking area integrals (related to metric) to boundary integrals (related to coefficients).

Paper structure. The paper builds from defining the dynamics via holomorphic deformation and Krylov methods, establishing the correspondence with the 2D Toda lattice. It then explores geometric consequences like the Fubini-Study metric and Berry curvature, culminating in an electrostatic analogy for understanding these structures.

Abstract

Although Arnoldi reduction of a generally non-Hermitian Hamiltonian yields an upper Hessenberg matrix rather than the tridiagonal form of Hermitian Lanczos theory, we show that a closed Toda sector survives in its diagonal and subdiagonal coefficients. For a fixed finite-dimensional Hamiltonian and a cyclic state vector deformed holomorphically, the Krylov Gram determinants are $τ$ functions of the finite two-dimensional Toda lattice, whose Flaschka variables coincide exactly with these Arnoldi coefficients. The Toda dynamics therefore closes on this sector without determining the remaining upper Hessenberg entries. The subdiagonal part of the same sector also has a direct geometric meaning: the squared subdiagonal coefficients determine both the Fubini--Study metric and the Berry curvature of holomorphic Krylov subspaces, whereas the geometric quantities associated with subspaces lost at Arnoldi breakdown cease to be defined. Along a smooth real path in the cyclic region, the Arnoldi-frame connection further provides a Hermitian tridiagonal generator of exact isospectral transport. When added to the Arnoldi matrix, this generator cancels transitions between instantaneous eigenspaces and realizes counterdiabatic driving whenever the matrix is diagonalizable with a nondegenerate spectrum.

Summary. The paper investigates how quantum walkers behave on lattices with fractal geometry, specifically the Sierpiński carpet. It shows that compared to uniform lattices where particles spread ballistically, the fractal structure causes strong, self-similar trapping near the starting point. This highlights how geometric constraints alone can suppress quantum transport.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a clean theoretical demonstration of how geometric constraints, even without explicit disorder potentials, can fundamentally alter quantum transport properties by creating hierarchical barriers that suppress long-range coherence.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study compares quantum particle transport dynamics on a fractal lattice (Sierpiński carpet) against its counterpart on a uniform lattice of the same size.

Main result. While particles move ballistically on the uniform lattice, they become increasingly trapped near their starting point on the Sierpiński carpet as the fractal order increases.

Method. The dynamics are simulated by solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation using numerical methods like Runge-Kutta, focusing on zone-integrated probabilities for comparison.

Model / system. A single quantum particle is modeled moving via nearest-neighbor tunneling on a square lattice structure that is iteratively modified into a Sierpiński carpet fractal. The dynamics are compared against the same system on a standard uniform lattice.

Key observables. Zone-integrated probabilities for finding the particle in the initial zone ($S(t)$) versus the opposite corner zone ($F(t)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Fractal order ($R$), lattice size, and time ($t$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes continuous-time dynamics governed by nearest-neighbor tunneling only to sites belonging to the defined lattice structure.

Figures summary. Figures compare $S(t)$ and $F(t)$ for different fractal orders, showing confinement on the Sierpiński carpet versus ballistic spread on the uniform lattice. Other figures analyze the scaling of characteristic crossing times across various zone sizes ($K$).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the model by defining the dynamics on both lattices, presents key observables (zone-integrated probabilities), and then systematically compares the time evolution results to demonstrate the qualitative difference between ballistic transport and fractal trapping.

Abstract

We study the dynamics of a single quantum particle on a finite-size square lattice with a fractal structure resembling the Sierpiński carpet, and compare it to the dynamics on a uniform lattice of the same size. For a particle initially localized at a corner of the lattice, we monitor the probability of finding it near the initial and opposite corners using zone-integrated probabilities, allowing a consistent comparison across fractal orders. While on the uniform lattice the particle reaches the opposite corner ballistically, in a time proportional to the lattice size, on the Sierpiński lattice it becomes increasingly confined to the vicinity of its initial position. We show that this trapping builds up self-similarly across the whole hierarchy of corner zones of the lattice.

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes structural properties of Random Recursive Trees by defining 'rank' and 'layer' distributions. Using advanced mathematical techniques like recursive relations and traveling wave analysis, the authors derive precise asymptotic formulas showing that these distributions decay factorially (for rank) and geometrically (for layer). The work provides a powerful framework for analyzing network structure in complex systems.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on graph theory, the mathematical machinery—solving complex recursions, analyzing wavefront propagation via PDEs, and deriving precise asymptotic scaling laws—is highly relevant to non-equilibrium statistical mechanics problems involving pattern formation or relaxation dynamics in physical systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is deriving exact recursive expressions and asymptotic distributions for two structural measures, 'rank' and 'layer', within Random Recursive Trees (RRTs).

Main result. The rank distribution decays factorially, while the layer distribution exhibits a geometric decay characterized by complex exponential forms involving constants like $1/e$.

Method. The authors employ recursive methods, tree-splitting techniques, and advanced asymptotic analysis, including solving differential equations using a traveling wave ansatz.

Model / system. The model is the Random Recursive Tree (RRT), a growing network structure where nodes are added sequentially by connecting to an existing node chosen uniformly at random. The analysis focuses on structural properties of this graph ensemble.

Key observables. Rank ($r_k$): distance to the closest leaf; Layer ($\ell_k$): number of leaf-stripping rounds required for a node to become a leaf. Also analyzed are their respective fractions and variances.

Important parameters / regimes. The size of the tree, $n$, is taken to infinity ($n o \infty$) for asymptotic results. The index $k$ (rank/layer) is also studied in the large-$k$ limit.

Assumptions / limitations. Key assumptions include the validity of the traveling wave ansatz and the assertion that $\ell_k \sim ar{\ell}_k$, which cannot be rigorously derived from the main asymptotic equations provided.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the concepts of rank and layer on small trees, compare computed vs. simulated distributions for $r_k$ and $\ell_k$, and show convergence rates related to variances and wavefront growth.

Paper structure. The paper builds from deriving exact recursive expressions (Theorems 1 & 2) to establishing self-averaging properties (Theorem 5). It then tackles the asymptotic limits, using advanced differential equation techniques (Traveling Wave Ansatz) to derive the final exponential decay laws for $ar{\ell}_k$ and conjectures for $\ell_k$.

Abstract

The distribution of node depths in a network is crucial for analyzing network structure. Two measures, rank and layer, quantify how deep inside a network a node is. The rank is the node's distance to the closest leaf, a node of degree 1. The layer is the number of times all leaves must be stripped off for the node to become a leaf. We derive exact recursive expressions for the rank and layer distributions in random recursive trees. We show that the rank and layer distributions decay factorially and geometrically, respectively, and prove the self-averaging of the numbers of nodes with fixed rank or layer. Unlike previous studies, our approach does not depend on labels or a root node, providing a more versatile framework for analyzing rank and layer distributions in complex networks.

Summary. The paper presents a scalable method to simulate exotic Fractional Quantum Hall states on quantum processors. It demonstrates that non-Abelian clustered FQH states can be prepared with constant circuit depth, contrasting sharply with the linear depth required for simpler Abelian states. This establishes a powerful new route for studying topological matter beyond current hardware limitations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides concrete quantum circuit blueprints for simulating highly complex, strongly correlated electron phenomena (FQH physics) that are otherwise inaccessible due to exponential resource scaling in traditional methods.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is developing a scalable method to prepare exotic Fractional Quantum Hall (FQH) states, particularly those exhibiting non-Abelian anyon statistics, on programmable quantum hardware.

Main result. Clustered non-Abelian FQH states can be prepared using parallel quantum circuits with a two-qubit depth independent of system size (constant depth), unlike the Abelian Laughlin state which requires linearly growing circuit depth.

Method. The authors use a systematic framework to catalog FQH states by constructing parent Hamiltonians from pattern-of-zeros data, translating these into quantum circuits for implementation on hardware like IBM Heron processors.

Model / system. The study focuses on FQH matter, modeling various states including the Abelian Laughlin state and non-Abelian Read–Rezayi $\mathbb{Z}_3$ and $\mathbb{Z}_4$ states. These are analyzed using thin-torus circuits derived from pseudopotential parent Hamiltonians.

Key observables. Fractional quasihole charges, non-Abelian braiding data (measured interferometrically), and circuit depth scaling.

Important parameters / regimes. System size (up to 156 qubits), two-qubit gate depth, and the clustering property of the state wavefunction.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation at large scales is identified as per-qubit readout error rather than the gate count itself; constant depth relies on junction squeezes forming disjoint blocks.

Figures summary. Figures compare circuit depth scaling, illustrating the dichotomy between linear depth (Laughlin) and constant depth (clustered non-Abelian states) for various FQH models.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing a systematic framework to cataloging states, demonstrating the circuit construction pipeline, comparing depth scalings based on clustering properties, and finally validating results by measuring topological observables like fractional charge and braiding data.

Abstract

Non-Abelian anyons arise as exotic excitations in fractional quantum Hall (FQH) matter and have proved very elusive to realize in conventional platforms. In this work, we show that on a programmable quantum hardware platform, the more exotic FQH excitations are the less costly ones to prepare: clustered non-Abelian FQH states admit parallel quantum preparation circuits whose two-qubit depth is independent of system size, while constructing the more common Abelian Laughlin state requires a sequential circuit chain with linear depth. The centerpiece of this work is our new systematic framework for cataloging possible FQH states and preparing them on quantum circuits at unprecedented scale and variety. Our prepared parafermionic Read--Rezayi $\mathbb{Z}_3$ state holds depth 3 from 8 to 118 qubits, and full root sampling extends to a 154-qubit, 104-electron Read--Rezayi $\mathbb{Z}_4$ state. In all, our demonstrated 18-family catalog of prepared FQH states extends to all 156 qubits of an IBM Heron processor, limited only by existing hardware scale. Measurements on the prepared states recover the expected fractional quasihole charges, with the charge estimator exact in every symmetry-selected shot for the clustered states, and braiding data of the non-Abelian $e/4$ quasihole measured via interferometric extensions. Our work establishes a scalable route to studying FQH physics on quantum processors and opens new avenues for preparing and probing non-Abelian topological matter far beyond the reach of conventional platforms.

Summary. The authors study symmetry-projected Gibbs states in a 3D cluster model to characterize the resulting phase diagram. Abstract

We study Gibbs states projected onto the symmetric sector of contractible one-form symmetries. In a three-dimensional cluster-model interpolation, this projection stabilizes symmetry-protected topological and projected-paramagnetic phases, both sharply distinct from thermal disorder. These values are exact on the endpoint, self-dual, zero-temperature, and infinite-temperature lines; elsewhere their quantization requires positive spatial-sheet, winding-line, and interface tensions. Quantum Monte Carlo supports the quantization through tension diagnostics and direct finite-size estimates.

Abstract

These results establish a potential route toward solving differential equations without the discretization overhead inherent in qubit-based implementations.

I analyse how historical narratives and structural separations of expertise slowed the formation of this coupling and outline what it takes to build it: explicit interfaces between technical teams and domain context and intermediate layers that translate quantum outputs into decision-relevant observables without suffocating foundational innovation. Framed this way, quantum computing's opportunity is clearest where deep physical modelling meets high-value decisions. Provided the field co-designs both sides from the outset.

Summary. This paper establishes that imaginary coherences between computational basis states are a necessary resource for training parameters in QAOA. By deriving rigorous bounds using adjoint channels and testing them against Max-Cut simulations under various noise models, the authors quantify how quantum state structure dictates algorithmic trainability.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the necessary quantum resources required for a non-zero gradient when training parameters in the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). It specifically investigates the role of imaginary coherences between candidate solutions.

Main result. Imaginarity, quantified by specific coherence measures, is shown to be a necessary but not sufficient condition for obtaining a non-zero gradient during QAOA optimization. The derived bounds successfully constrain the magnitude of the gradient across various noise models.

Method. The authors derive analytical upper bounds on the gradient using quantum calculus and the adjoint channel formalism, comparing these theoretical limits against numerical simulations in Max-Cut instances.

Model / system. The study focuses on QAOA applied to combinatorial optimization problems like Max-Cut. The system involves a cost Hamiltonian ($H_C$) and a transverse-field mixer ($H_B$), analyzed under various noise channels (phase-flip, amplitude damping, depolarizing).

Abstract

The quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) tackles combinatorial problems by tuning a quantum circuit in a classical loop, often guided by gradients. We show that the gradient used to tune the circuit's final parameter is bounded by imaginarity, which weights phase relationships between candidate solutions by how strongly the circuit connects them and how differently the problem scores them. Imaginarity is necessary but not sufficient for a nonzero gradient. We extend the bound to three common noise models and compare it numerically with the gradient in Max-Cut simulations.

Abstract

Understanding the effectiveness of quantum compilation techniques requires visibility into the entire transpilation process, not just the final circuit metrics. This demonstration presents an MLflow-inspired autologging framework for Qiskit that automatically captures compiler provenance, including transpilation stages, pass-level execution data, backend characteristics, compiler configuration, and execution results. The framework extends the QProv provenance model with compiler-specific information and stores the collected data in an MLflow Tracking Server for analysis and visualization. By eliminating manual instrumentation, the proposed approach improves compiler observability and supports reproducible evaluation of quantum compilation workflows.
