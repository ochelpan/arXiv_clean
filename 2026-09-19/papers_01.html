Summary. This paper introduces TetrisCNN, an interpretable neural network designed to detect quantum phase transitions from experimental simulator data. By learning representations directly from measurable spin correlators, it overcomes the 'black box' problem of standard NNs. The key achievement is deriving the phase boundaries as explicit, symbolic formulas based on experimentally accessible quantities.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, interpretable bridge between advanced machine learning techniques and fundamental condensed matter physics problems, offering a pathway to extract physical laws from complex quantum simulation data.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Detecting quantum phases of matter is difficult, as traditional methods rely on physical intuition, while black-box neural networks fail to provide physical understanding or generalize to noisy experimental data.

Main result. The proposed TetrisCNN framework successfully detects phase transitions and crossovers and, crucially, expresses the decision boundaries as symbolic formulas built directly from measurable spin correlators.

Method. The authors introduce TetrisCNN, a convolutional architecture using parallel branches with varied filters to learn sparse, interpretable latent representations based on spin correlators.

Model / system. The method is applied to experimental snapshots from 2D quantum simulators, specifically modeling the Ising and XY Hamiltonians using Rydberg-atom arrays in optical tweezer lattices.

Key observables. Spin correlators (e.g., $ ext{mstag}$, $ ext{CNN}$), magnetization, and the classification/regression output defining the phase boundary.

Important parameters / regimes. Lattice spacing ($a=10 \mu ext{m}$), interaction strength ($U/h \approx 1.95 ext{ MHz}$), and varying external fields ($\delta, \Omega$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on fitting a surrogate model (like logistic regression) to approximate the complex decision boundary learned by the deep network.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the CNN architecture mapping filters to correlators, the comparison of linear vs. quadratic approximations of the phase boundary, and the stability of the learned representations.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the limitations of current phase detection methods, introduces TetrisCNN, demonstrates its application to 2D Ising/XY models, and concludes by showing how the learned decision boundaries can be analytically approximated using measurable correlators.

Abstract

Detecting phases of matter in general relies on identifying the correct order parameter - a task that remains notoriously difficult for unknown transitions and traditionally is guided by physical intuition and educated guess. Neural networks have recently offered an alternative route by locating phase transitions in known models without any a priori physical knowledge. Yet these approaches remain black boxes and only identify phases without elucidating their properties. Moreover, they often struggle when confronted with realistic, noisy experimental data, which constitute the ultimate testbed for automated methods in physics. Here, we bridge these perspectives by introducing TetrisCNN, a convolutional architecture with parallel branches of differently shaped filters, reminiscent of Tetris blocks, that learns sparse, interpretable latent representations directly in terms of spin correlators. Applied to experimental snapshots of two-dimensional Ising and XY quantum simulators measured in multiple bases, the network not only detects phase transitions and crossovers but also expresses its latent representation and decision boundaries as symbolic formulas built from experimentally measurable spin correlators. This framework opens the way to integrating interpretable neural networks with quantum simulators to uncover and understand new phases of matter.

Highlighted author(s): Igor Lesanovsky

Summary. This work analyzes the impact of shot noise on simulating quantum many-body systems using NMR spectra as a test case. By quantifying the required number of measurement shots, the authors show that the resource scaling is weak with system size. This suggests that statistical noise may not fundamentally limit the potential for quantum advantage in such simulations.

Why it may be interesting. It directly addresses a critical practical hurdle for quantum computation—measurement noise—by providing quantitative bounds on resource scaling for a physically relevant many-body problem like NMR.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the statistical noise inherent in quantum measurements (shot noise) affects the ability to simulate complex quantum systems, specifically focusing on the scaling of required computational resources.

Main result. The required number of measurement shots needed to achieve a desired accuracy scales only weakly with the system size, suggesting that shot noise does not pose a fundamental barrier to achieving quantum advantage in NMR simulation.

Method. The authors analyze the scaling of the infidelity between the noisy quantum simulation result and the exact reference result by expanding the cosine similarity in powers of the noise.

Model / system. The test case is the simulation of Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra, modeled by coupled nuclear spin systems governed by Heisenberg or Ising Hamiltonians. The simulation is mapped onto a qubit register for quantum computation.

Key observables. The required number of shots ($R$), the spectral noise covariance ($ ext{Cov}[\epsilon_R]$), and the resulting infidelity ($1 - C(s_R, s)$).

Important parameters / regimes. System size ($L$, number of nuclei), desired accuracy, and the number of shots ($R$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis focuses solely on shot noise, neglecting other error sources like decoherence or Trotterization errors, and relies on expanding the fidelity metric to second order in the noise.

Figures summary. Figure 1 compares NMR spectroscopy across physical, classical (exponential), and quantum (shot-noise limited) regimes. Other figures detail numerical results for both Ising and Heisenberg model Hamiltonians across varying system sizes.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem by comparing classical and quantum simulation scaling, then develops the theoretical framework by calculating the single-shot variance and deriving the scaling of the infidelity with $R$ using the Central Limit Theorem.

Abstract

Recent advances in the field of quantum computing hardware motivate the search for applications which demonstrate so-called quantum advantage. One promising use case that has been identified is the simulation of quantum many-body systems. The computational resources required for performing such a simulation on a classical computer generally grow exponentially with the size of the system being modeled, which is ultimately due to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space in which the dynamics of such a system take place. While digital quantum computers natively evolve quantum states directly in such a Hilbert space, thus naively avoiding this problem, the result of such a computation is typically not obtained as a deterministic output. Rather, it requires projective measurements which are fundamentally affected by shot noise. Any desired expectation values must therefore be reconstructed from repeated measurements, making the number of those measurements a relevant computational resource, and thus an important consideration for any potential claims of quantum advantage. In this work, we study how this resource scales with system size (a scaling which itself depends on the desired accuracy of the final result), using the simulation of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra as a test case. We study this scaling for both real-world molecules, as well as a class of model NMR Hamiltonians which allow for efficient large-scale simulations with one-dimensional, two-dimensional, and all-to-all interactions. We find that the required resources increase only weakly with molecular size, far below the exponential growth of the underlying Hilbert space. This result suggests that shot noise should not pose a fundamental barrier to achieving quantum advantage in the simulation of NMR systems, and perhaps for many-body systems more broadly.

Abstract

Simple analytic pulse-shaping techniques are of great practical utility in quantum control, with prime examples being the DRAG method for suppressing leakage in superconducting microwave gates and the transitionless-driving approach to shortcuts-to-adiabaticity. Standard versions of these methods require two orthogonal control channels, with the second channel effectively breaking time-reversal symmetry. This appears to rule out their use in settings with only a single real-valued control field, such as the kind of baseband flux control that is common in many superconducting circuit architectures. We show here that a simple analytic pulse-shaping technique derived via a Magnus expansion is effective even with just a single baseband control channel. We demonstrate its efficacy by simulating a two-qubit gate between transmons realized with a tunable coupler and baseband flux pulses. Our corrections dramatically reduce non-adiabatic leakage caused by ramping the coupler: for realistic device parameters, leakage in a fast iSWAP gate is suppressed by up to three orders of magnitude. Our approach is general, goes beyond simply suppressing unwanted spectral weight at leakage transitions, and can be applied to a variety of platforms.

Abstract

Abstract
