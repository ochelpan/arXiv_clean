Experimental demonstration of finite-size general security via discrete-modulated CVQKD with real time postprocessing

Summary. This paper presents the first experimental demonstration of a discrete-modulated CVQKD system achieving finite-size composable security against general attacks. By integrating advanced techniques like variable-length decision and modern entropy accumulation, the system operates in real-time on near-commercial hardware. This advances the practical viability of quantum key distribution by addressing critical security loopholes.

Why it may be interesting. This work pushes the boundaries of practical quantum communication by tackling the complex theoretical requirement of general security in a physical, real-time experimental setting.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge addressed is experimentally demonstrating finite-size general security for Continuous-Variable Quantum Key Distribution (CVQKD) using discrete-modulated (DM) protocols.

Main result. The authors report the first experimental demonstration of such a system, achieving composable secret keys against general attacks in real time using QPSK modulation.

Method. The protocol employs a variable-length, general security framework, integrating modern entropy accumulation techniques and conic optimization for key rate estimation.

Model / system. The system is a CVQKD implementation using coherent states modulated via QPSK, operating over standard optical fiber and utilizing near-commercial hardware for real-time postprocessing.

Key observables. Secret key rate ($\kappa$), Secret Key Fraction (SKF), and the final secret key length ($\ell_\varphi$).

Important parameters / regimes. Detector efficiency ($\eta_D$), detector noise ($ u_{el}$), block length ($\sim 10^6$ rounds), and the constellation parameters ($\Delta_S, \Delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The security analysis relies on the marginal-constrained entropy accumulation theorem (MEAT), and the post-processing requires an authenticated classical channel.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the overall experimental pipeline (initialization, quantum communication, post-processing steps) and show the distribution of the SKF at 5 km for $n=10^6$.

Paper structure. The paper details the experimental setup, outlines the variable-length security framework, describes the post-processing steps (reconciliation, VLD, privacy amplification), and presents the results demonstrating finite-size security.

Abstract

Continuous-variable quantum key distribution (CVQKD) is compatible with telecommunication infrastructure, but implementing composable security with experimentally practical resources has remained challenging, particularly for discrete-modulated (DM) protocols. We report the first experimental demonstration of a DM CVQKD system that generates composable secret keys against general attacks with finite-size block lengths on the order of $\sim 10^{6}$ rounds via a quadrature phase shift keying (QPSK) system. Our implementation follows a variable-length, general security framework enabled by modern entropy accumulation techniques and conic optimization, whose experimental pipeline allows real-time operation on near-commercial hardware.

Summary. This theoretical study examines 'bad metallicity' in the Hubbard model, proposing that the unusual temperature-linear resistivity arises from local quantum dynamics rather than simple thermal effects. The key finding is the emergence of a 'semi-quantum regime' characterized by the formation of spin cages around doped holes, which dictates the incoherent charge transport properties.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of a 'semi-quantum regime' where thermodynamics and transport decouple, governed by local quantum structures (spin cages), provides an analogy to exotic quantum states in condensed matter, which is relevant for understanding non-equilibrium dynamics in strongly correlated systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the origin of 'bad metallicity'—a state characterized by temperature-linear resistivity exceeding the Ioffe–Regel limit—to determine if it is a generic feature of strong local correlations.

Main result. The bad metal behavior in the Hubbard model is linked to the formation of quasi-local ferromagnetic 'spin cages' around doped holes, which govern incoherent charge quantum dynamics in an intermediate 'semi-quantum regime'.

Method. The study employs exact diagonalization on finite clusters and utilizes theoretical tools like the Nernst–Einstein decomposition and high-temperature expansions to analyze transport and thermodynamic properties.

Model / system. The system is the infinite-$U$ Hubbard model studied on a two-dimensional square lattice. The Hamiltonian incorporates the constraint of no double occupancy, allowing the investigation of strongly correlated electron physics.

Key observables. dc resistivity ($ ho$), compressibility ($\chi_c$), charge diffusivity ($D_c$), and the connected hole–spin-bond correlator ($C_h$).

Important parameters / regimes. Three distinct temperature regimes are identified: high-T bad metal, the intermediate 'semi-quantum regime', and the low-T Fermi liquid regime. Key scales include $T_{ ext{quant}}$ and $T_{ ext{coh}}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the low-doping ground state approaches a Nagaoka ferromagnetically ordered state at low temperatures.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the characteristic temperature dependencies of $ ho$ and $\chi_c$ across the three regimes, showing the shift in the source of temperature dependence from thermodynamics to dynamics in the semi-quantum regime. Other figures detail the evolution of spin correlations and the caging free-energy cost.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the temperature dependence of transport properties, first establishing the bad metal behavior, then identifying the crossover scales ($T_{ ext{quant}}$), and finally linking the dynamics in the intermediate regime to the formation of local spin structures (spin cages).

Abstract

Bad metals exhibit approximately $T$-linear dc resistivity beyond the Ioffe--Regel limit. That this behavior occurs in systems with radically different ground states suggests it is a generic manifestation of strong local correlations. We test this hypothesis in the infinite-$U$ Hubbard model with small hole densities using exact diagonalization to compute thermodynamic and transport properties of finite clusters. Upon cooling, we find an intermediate temperature range, an electronic analogue of the semi-quantum regime'' of liquid helium, in which quantum effects produce a roughly $T$-independent compressibility, yet the resistivity is $T$-linear and exceeds the Ioffe--Regel limit. Entry into this regime is accompanied by the formation of quasi-local ferromagneticspin cages'' around doped holes, regions that facilitate local quantum motion embedded in a fluctuating spin background, analogous to the transient crystalline cages thought to control incoherent transport in semi-quantum liquid helium. Remarkably, despite the simplicity of the model, the bad metal behavior found here resembles that seen in various material platforms.

Deterministic Hypergraph SYK: Melonic Dominance and Maximal Chaos Without Disorder Averaging

Authors: Shiroman Prakash
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19255v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper constructs a deterministic hypergraph realization of the SYK model, aiming to show that its characteristic chaotic and melonic physics does not require random disorder averaging. By analyzing the two-point function and OTOCs using advanced techniques like the cavity formalism, the authors demonstrate that the model successfully reproduces the key theoretical predictions of SYK in a structured, non-random setting.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, geometrically constrained, and disorder-free platform to study universal quantum chaotic dynamics (like those seen in SYK), which is highly relevant for understanding strongly correlated quantum matter.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine if the essential melonic and chaotic features of the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) model can be realized in a sparse, deterministic setting without relying on disorder averaging.

Main result. The deterministic hypergraph SYK model (hSYK) successfully recovers the standard SYK bilocal effective action and exhibits characteristic chaotic signatures like Wigner-Dyson statistics, providing a disorder-free realization.

Method. The analysis uses Schwinger-Dyson equations, the cavity formalism, and spectral expansion techniques to evaluate correlation functions in the large-$d$ limit.

Model / system. The model is a deterministic hypergraph SYK (hSYK) defined on a $q$-uniform, $d$-regular interaction hypergraph with $N$ Majorana fermions. The interactions are sparse, scaling as $O(dN)$ rather than exponentially.

Key observables. Two-point function, connected four-point function, Out-of-Time-Order Correlators (OTOCs), and spectral statistics (Wigner-Dyson).

Important parameters / regimes. Large-$d$ limit, fixed $q \ge 4$, and the structural constraints imposed by the hypergraph geometry.

Assumptions / limitations. The model relies on structural axioms concerning the hypergraph, such as the 'one-overlap' condition ($|e_a \cap e_b| \le 1$) and spectral separation gaps.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the physical hypergraph, its line graph, and the relationship between these structures.

Paper structure. The paper develops the analysis by first establishing the two-point function via the Schwinger-Dyson equation and cavity formalism, then deriving the effective action, and finally confirming chaotic features using OTOCs and spectral statistics.

Abstract

We show that SYK-like melonic solvability and maximal chaos survive in a sparse deterministic model, without disorder averaging. Our deterministic hypergraph SYK model (hSYK) consists of $N$ Majorana fermions on a $q$-uniform, $d$-regular interaction hypergraph with only $O(dN)$ interaction terms and uniform positive couplings. Under explicit geometric and spectral expansion conditions, its two-point function satisfies the standard melonic Schwinger-Dyson equation up to $O(d^{-1})$ corrections, while the leading bilocal effective action coincides with that of ordinary SYK at the dominant large-$d$ saddle. Its connected four-point function factorizes into a temporal SYK ladder kernel and a line-graph random walk. A finite line-graph spectral gap controls the visibility of the maximally chaotic uniform mode in generic localized OTOCs and, by gapping non-uniform spatial modes in the bilocal spectrum, excludes additional light fluctuation modes beyond the Schwarzian. Exact diagonalization further shows melonic two-point scaling, single-instance Wigner-Dyson statistics, and the predicted graph-controlled spatial decay of OTOCs. The model therefore offers a disorder-free realization of the essential melonic and chaotic features of SYK, in a form more amenable to quantum simulation.

Non-Abelian Anyon Condensation: a Path-Integral Monte Carlo Approach

Authors: Rafael Flores-Calderón, Frank Pollmann, Michael Knap
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19282v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

Summary. This work develops a novel, sign-free path-integral Monte Carlo approach to study transitions out of non-Abelian topological order in Kitaev quantum double models. By mapping the system to a gauge-Higgs theory, the authors successfully diagnose non-Abelian anyon condensation and confinement transitions. The results provide a unified numerical tool for understanding complex topological phase transitions in condensed matter systems.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous development of a sign-free path-integral method to study topological phase transitions in lattice gauge theories is highly relevant to understanding emergent phenomena in strongly correlated quantum matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the difficulty of describing transitions out of non-Abelian topological order using standard numerical methods at large scales.

Main result. The authors provide a unified numerical framework demonstrating non-Abelian anyon condensation and confinement transitions in Kitaev quantum doubles for $G=S_3$. Specifically, electric anyon proliferation drives a first-order condensation transition.

Method. The methodology involves developing a sign-free path-integral framework by mapping the link-only model onto a $G$ gauge–Higgs theory via an exact matterization isometry. Quantum Monte Carlo simulations are used to analyze the resulting lattice gauge-Higgs model.

Model / system. The system is based on Kitaev quantum doubles $\mathcal D(G)$ defined on a lattice, which are perturbed by single-link operators to probe anyon condensation. This model is mathematically transformed into a $(2+1)$D $G$ gauge–Higgs theory.

Key observables. Generalized Fredenhagen–Marcu (FM) order parameters (for condensation) and Wilson loops (for confinement).

Important parameters / regimes. The group $G$ (specifically $S_3$), and coupling ratios like $\lambda_a/\kappa$ controlling the type of anyon proliferation.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of a unitary transformation and an isometry to map the quantum double model to a classical gauge-Higgs theory, and the use of the Trotter approximation in the path integral.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the path integral lifting to a gauge-Higgs theory, the action of local operators via the matterization circuit, and the diagnostic plots showing the onset of the Wilson-loop area law and the finite jump in FM order parameters.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first establishing the physical problem and the need for a tractable numerical framework. It then details the mathematical machinery: the matterization isometry and the transformation of the Hamiltonian to the gauge-Higgs form. Finally, it applies this framework to analyze specific transitions (condensation and confinement) using Monte Carlo simulations and order parameters.

Abstract

Transitions out of non-Abelian topological order are difficult to describe in microscopic quantum models with numerical methods that remain tractable at large scales. We develop a sign-free path-integral framework for Kitaev quantum doubles $\mathcal D(G)$ by organizing single-link perturbations in terms of non-invertible electric and magnetic 1-form symmetries that proliferate distinct anyon species. For any finite group $G$, an exact $\textit{matterization}$ isometry introduces vertex degrees of freedom and maps the link-only model onto a $G$ gauge--Higgs theory. Furthermore, the 1-form symmetries of the fixed point allow us to define generalized Fredenhagen--Marcu order parameters that become finite when the corresponding anyons condense. For $G = S_3$, quantum Monte Carlo simulations show that proliferating a non-Abelian electric anyon drives a first-order transition in which all nontrivial electric anyons condense. In the purely magnetic limit, the model reduces to a $(2+1)$D pure $G$ gauge theory; for $G=S_3$, it exhibits a first-order confinement transition, diagnosed by the onset of a Wilson-loop area law and the restoration of an emergent magnetic 1-form symmetry. These results provide a unified numerical framework for non-Abelian anyon condensation, confinement, and generalized symmetry breaking.

Transduction-Enabled Superconducting Quantum Repeater: Toward Deterministic Entanglement Distribution with High-Fidelity Gates

Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19784v1

Summary. This paper introduces the Transduction-Enabled Superconducting Quantum Repeater (TESQR) to overcome the probabilistic limitations of current quantum repeaters. By integrating superconducting circuits to perform deterministic entanglement swapping, the scheme significantly boosts entanglement distribution rates and maintains high fidelity over practical distances. It represents a crucial step toward building scalable quantum communication networks.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the engineering challenge of scaling quantum networks by proposing a concrete, high-fidelity physical implementation that bridges the gap between long-haul photonics and solid-state quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Long-distance entanglement distribution is limited by photon loss and the probabilistic nature of Bell State Measurements (BSMs) in traditional quantum repeaters.

Main result. The proposed Transduction-Enabled Superconducting Quantum Repeater (TESQR) improves entanglement distribution rates by up to 159% and achieves end-to-end fidelities exceeding 0.8 over 20 km after purification.

Method. The authors propose a hybrid architecture combining photonic qubits with superconducting circuits to enable deterministic entanglement processing, avoiding probabilistic BSMs.

Model / system. The system integrates three components at the repeater node: quantum transduction (optical to microwave), coupling to a superconducting QPU, and local entanglement swapping performed via microwave gates. The analysis uses quantum channel formalism and density matrix evolution.

Key observables. Output-state fidelities, success probabilities of entanglement distribution, and end-to-end fidelities.

Important parameters / regimes. Fiber length (up to 20 km), transduction efficiency ($\eta_t$), coupling efficiencies ($\eta_{ph}, \eta_{qb}$), and gate error rates.

Assumptions / limitations. Ideal memory performance is assumed, and the analysis initially focuses on quantum-processing performance metrics rather than resource costs.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the TESQR node architecture (Transduction $ ightarrow$ Coupling $ ightarrow$ Superconducting QPU). Other figures analyze the degradation of fidelity due to asymmetries in transduction efficiency, fiber length, and thermal noise.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the limitations of current quantum repeaters, then details the TESQR architecture, models the state evolution through sequential steps (Source $ ightarrow$ Transmission $ ightarrow$ Transduction $ ightarrow$ Coupling $ ightarrow$ Swapping), and finally analyzes the performance under various noise and asymmetry conditions.

Abstract

Long-distance entanglement distribution is hindered by photon loss in optical fibers and the nocloning theorem. Optical quantum repeater (QR) protocols rely on Bell state measurements (BSMs), they are intrinsically limited to probabilistic photon operations and fail 50% of the time. We propose a hybrid approach to building quantum repeaters that combines the high transmission speed of photonic qubits in optical fiber with the high-fidelity quantum processing capabilities enabled by superconducting circuits. The transduction-enabled superconducting QR (TESQR) architecture eliminates the need for probabilistic BSMs and allows deterministic processing operations. The TESQR framework always yields a final state at the remote nodes rather than aborting on photon loss, manifesting deterministic entanglement distribution within certain parameter regimes. We evaluate the performance by assessing output-state fidelities and success probabilities of entanglement distribution using realistic noise models. Additionally, we integrate an entanglement purification scheme and evaluate the performance through numerical simulations in QuTiP environment. Our results show that, for entanglement swapping, the proposed scheme improves the entanglement distribution rate by an average of 63% and by up to 159% compared with photonic-only architectures. Moreover, after purification, the end-to-end fidelities exceed 0.8 over distances up to 20 km.

Summary. This work uses molecular dynamics simulations to study energy fluctuations in solvated DNA across three topologies. The key finding is that the energy time-series exhibit universal 1/f noise, with the degree of low-frequency spectral power directly correlating with the structural flexibility of the DNA conformation. This highlights how topological constraints govern the stochastic dynamics of biomolecules in solution.

Why it may be interesting. The finding of universal $1/f$ noise across different structural states, coupled with the explicit link between structural flexibility and the low-frequency spectral components, provides insights into non-equilibrium dynamics relevant to biological energy dissipation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the universal nature of 1/f noise in the power spectra of energy time-series fluctuations within solvated DNA dynamics, and how DNA topology modulates its coupling with the surrounding environment.

Main result. All analyzed energy time-series exhibit universal 1/f noise with an exponent $\beta \simeq 0.90$, but flexible structures (G-quadruplex, Hairpin) show pronounced low-frequency modes compared to the rigid Duplex.

Method. The research employs comprehensive atomistic Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations to calculate energy fluctuations ($\delta E$) and subsequently analyze their power spectral density ($S(f)$) via the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT).

Model / system. The physical system is solvated DNA modeled in three distinct conformations: G-quadruplex, Hairpin, and Duplex. The environment includes explicit water molecules and mobile counter-ions, simulated using established force fields.

Key observables. Energy fluctuations ($\delta E_{DNA\text{-}DNA}$, $\delta E_{DNA\text{-}water}$, $\delta E_{DNA\text{-}ion}$), Pearson correlation coefficients ($\rho_{ij}$), and the power spectral density $S_{ii}(f)$ exhibiting $1/f$ noise.

Important parameters / regimes. The universal decay exponent $\beta \simeq 0.90$, and the structural rigidity differences between the three DNA forms.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the system is in a stationary regime for power spectrum calculation, and that the observed noise characteristics reflect the underlying physical coupling mechanisms.

Figures summary. Figures show typical time-series and probability distributions of energy fluctuations, contour plots of bivariate distributions, and log-log plots of power spectra $S_{ii}(f)$ confirming the power-law scaling.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes energy correlations ($\rho_{ij}$) between DNA self, water, and ion energies, demonstrating strong anti-correlation between water and ion energies. Abstract

We investigate energy fluctuations of solvated DNA in three conformations: G-quadruplex, Hairpin, and Duplex. Our comprehensive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations demonstrate that the time-series of interaction energies for DNA self, DNA-water and DNA-ion show 1/f noise with a universal decay exponent $β\simeq 0.90$. We show that DNA topology and structural rigidity modulate its dynamic coupling with the surrounding hydration shell and mobile counter-ions. This coupling critically shapes the 1/f noise profile: DNA self energies in flexible structures (G-quadruplex, Hairpin) display pronounced low-frequency modes absent in the rigid Duplex. These findings reveal how the interplay between DNA topology and environmental coupling governs different stochastic behavior in biologically relevant forms of DNA.

Scaling and Condensation of Dry Active Matter Around Circular Obstacles

Summary. This work studies active matter confined by circular obstacles, finding that the resulting vortices can undergo a phase transition. Below a critical density, the vortex remains localized, but above it, the vortex forms an extensive, obstacle-stabilized condensate. The findings establish a clear link between local geometry and macroscopic phase behavior in active systems.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of obstacle-stabilized condensation in active matter provides a non-equilibrium mechanism for phase separation that is distinct from bulk MIPS, offering insights into pattern formation in complex active systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the mass and spatial extent of self-sustained vortices formed by dry active matter scale with system size, particularly analyzing the transition between localized and extensive condensed states.

Main result. The system exhibits two distinct scaling regimes: at low densities, the vortex is localized, but above a critical density, the vortex mass grows extensively with the total particle number, indicating an obstacle-stabilized condensed state.

Method. The study uses numerical simulations of Active Brownian Particles (ABPs) interacting with a fixed circular obstacle, analyzing scaling laws and comparing results to a derived kinetic model.

Model / system. The system consists of dry active matter particles modeled by an overdamped Langevin equation, interacting via repulsive forces near a circular obstacle. The analysis focuses on the resulting vortex structures and their thermodynamic limit behavior.

Key observables. Vortex mass (M), mean radius (R), maximum radius (R_max), and the scaling exponent ($\alpha$) relating vortex mass to particle number ($M \sim N^\alpha$).

Important parameters / regimes. Global area fraction ($\phi$), obstacle size ($D$), system size ($L$), and the critical density ($\phi^*_b$) separating scaling regimes.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes purely repulsive interactions and focuses on the thermodynamic limit behavior of the active system.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the density dependence of vortex mass scaling ($\alpha$ vs $\phi$), the vortex mass fraction ($f_c$ vs $\phi_t$), and the scaling of vortex radii ($R, R_{max}$) with system size ratios ($L/D$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by defining the physical model and observables, establishing the two distinct scaling regimes (low vs. high density), developing a kinetic model to explain the transition, and finally analyzing the geometric properties of the resulting condensate.

Abstract

Active Brownian particles confined to rigid substrates are known to accumulate near rigid boundaries and, under suitable conditions, undergo motility-induced phase separation (MIPS). A particularly intriguing manifestation of this behavior is the formation of self-sustained vortices around circular obstacles, which act as localized nucleation sites for particle aggregation. While several dynamical properties of such vortices have been previously characterized, their behavior in the thermodynamic limit remains largely unexplored. Here, we investigate how the mass and spatial extent of a dry active-matter vortex scale with system size. Using numerical simulations of repulsive active Brownian Particles interacting with a fixed circular obstacle, we measure the vortex mass, mean radius, and maximum radius as functions of the global area fraction, obstacle size, and system size. We find two distinct scaling regimes. At low densities, the vortex remains localized and its characteristic properties saturate as the system size increases. Above a critical density, however, the vortex mass grows extensively with the total number of particles, while its spatial dimensions scale linearly with the system size, indicating the emergence of an obstacle-stabilized condensed state.

Summary. This paper reports the largest-scale quantum machine learning experiment to date, implementing a Quantum Reservoir Computing model on a 4,500-qubit quantum annealer. Abstract

Quantum machine learning could uncover statistical structure beyond the reach of classical models, but this requires quantum models large and expressive enough to be useful and cheap enough to read out. Most approaches optimize many quantum parameters and are thus limited by expensive training loops. Here we report a quantum machine-learning model implemented on a programmable superconducting quantum annealer that processes temporal data at large scale using up to 4,500 qubits-the largest quantum machine-learning experiment performed to date. Following the quantum reservoir computing paradigm, the untrained native many-body dynamics generated by reverse annealing is directly used to process temporal data. We prove that the interactions produced during annealing are indispensable-a non-interacting reservoir retains no memory of its input. We evaluate our model experimentally on standard memory benchmarks and demonstrate that it can successfully forecast chaotic time series. These results establish quantum annealers as a scalable platform for large-scale quantum machine learning.

A Restricted Boltzmann Machine with Quantum-State Visible Units

Summary. This paper adapts the Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) to accept quantum states as input, overcoming the mathematical difficulties associated with continuous quantum variables. By deriving controlled high-dimensional limits, the authors create robust algorithms that successfully map complex quantum many-body states onto trainable feature representations. This demonstrates a powerful ML tool for analyzing quantum phase transitions.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a novel machine learning framework to analyze quantum many-body physics by mapping complex quantum states onto trainable, classical optimization objectives, which is highly relevant for understanding quantum phase transitions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of constructing a Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) where the visible input is a quantum state, rather than a classical configuration.

Main result. The authors derive two tractable, controlled high-dimensional limits—a Hopfield-type network and a Gram likelihood objective—allowing the RBM to perform quantum-phase recognition and feature extraction.

Method. The core method involves regularizing the continuous likelihood integral over the quantum state space using spherical convolution and deriving specific high-dimensional limits to make the optimization tractable.

Model / system. The model is a quantum-state visible RBM. It is benchmarked across several 1D quantum many-body systems, including the Aubry–André model, Bose–Hubbard model, and transverse-field Ising chain.

Key observables. Feature extraction capability, state recognition across quantum phase transitions, and the resulting coupling/Gram matrices ($J$ or $G$).

Important parameters / regimes. The high dimension $d$ of the visible space, and the controlled scaling factors ($\kappa_j$) used in the high-dimensional limits.

Abstract

We construct a restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) whose visible input is a quantum state rather than a classical configuration. Each hidden unit carries a trainable quantum template prepared by a parametrized circuit and converts its overlap with the input into a feature. Treating quantum states as high-dimensional continuous visible objects creates nontrivial normalization and scaling problems. We regularize the continuous likelihood and derive two controlled high-dimensional limits, yielding a Hopfield-type network with continuous Hilbert-space patterns and a data-augmented Gram likelihood. The resulting algorithms are compact and use trainable circuit-prepared templates as measurement intermediaries between quantum data and classical optimization. Abstract

On the span of the first $N$ oscillator modes, Gauss--Hermite quadrature gives an exact change of basis between mode coefficients and $N$ weighted position space samples. We implement this transform with $O(N\operatorname{polylog}(N,1/\varepsilon))$ logical gates and polylogarithmic quantum width. The operator-error bound $\varepsilon$ holds on arbitrary superpositions and includes all auxiliary registers. The construction uses signed averages on adaptive windows to convert uniform-grid samples into weighted Hermite-root samples. Their varying widths control the amplification cost, giving the near-linear bound.

Is higher-order physics different?

Authors: Pablo Villegas, Sandro Meloni
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20759v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 3/5

Summary. This paper argues that while higher-order interactions can generate novel, complex behavior in systems like oscillators or spin models, this novelty does not automatically imply a new fundamental physics. The authors demonstrate that the true long-term, universal behavior is dictated by the system's symmetries and conserved quantities at large scales, rather than the specific arity of the microscopic coupling terms.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous theoretical framework for distinguishing between necessary model complexity (for accurate prediction) and fundamental physical novelty (for new universality classes) in complex many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper critically assesses whether collective phenomena observed in higher-order interacting models prove the existence of a distinct 'higher-order physics' beyond what is predicted by standard universality principles.

Main result. The long-scale physics (universality class) is determined by the infrared ingredients (symmetries and conservation laws) surviving coarse-graining, not by the arity (order) of the microscopic interaction.

Method. The authors revisit canonical models (Ising, contagion, Kuramoto) by comparing microscopic interaction terms (arity) with their long-scale effective theories and universality classes.

Model / system. The analysis uses canonical models including Ising, SIS contagion, voter, and Kuramoto oscillators, extended with three-body or higher-order group interactions.

Key observables. Collective phenomena such as discontinuous transitions, bistability, and the resulting universality class (e.g., Directed Percolation).

Important parameters / regimes. Interaction arity (order of coupling), dimensionality, and the structure of the effective theory's operator content.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption tested is that the long-scale physics must be robust against changes in microscopic description, leading to the separation of microscopic reducibility from macroscopic universality.

Figures summary. Figures summarize the analysis of canonical models, distinguishing between microscopic reduction types and showing that group interactions generally leave the long-scale behavior unchanged.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the conflict between observed novel collective behavior and the principle of universality. It then analyzes specific models to show that while higher-order terms can be microscopically irreducible, their long-scale behavior is often captured by lower-order effective terms, concluding that the focus should be on measurable, predictive evidence.

Abstract

Group interactions are widespread, and higher-order extensions of familiar models display collective phenomena absent from their pairwise baselines, which are routinely offered as evidence of a distinct higher-order physics. We ask whether that claim survives the test that gives new a precise meaning in statistical physics: that of universality. Revisiting canonical higher-order models, we argue that what classifies collective behavior are the infrared ingredients that survive at long scales. Arity is not a universality label. Beyond universality, we examine two further questions: whether higher-order structure is a fact about the system or a choice of description, and what data can and cannot tell us about interaction order. Higher-order descriptions remain indispensable when they expose the organizing structure, provide a better mechanistic language, or improve prediction. We close with what should be measured before new phenomena can be claimed, and where higher-order structure already earns its place.
