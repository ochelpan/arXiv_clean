Amenable groups with nearly exponential sofic profile, and quantum channels that need nearly linear memory

Authors: Seth Douglas, Nidhal Mghirbi
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04271v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper connects group theory to quantum information by constructing an amenable group with an unexpectedly large sofic profile. It then uses this group structure to define a quantum channel, establishing sharp lower bounds on the quantum memory required to simulate the channel over many uses. This work reveals deep structural constraints linking algebraic complexity to quantum resource costs.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful, non-trivial link between abstract algebra (amenability, sofic properties) and fundamental resource constraints in quantum information (memory cost), suggesting deep structural limitations on quantum processes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the deep connection between the algebraic structure of amenable groups (quantified by the sofic profile) and the resource cost (memory) required for quantum channels.

Main result. It constructs an amenable group with a nearly exponential sofic profile, and derives sharp trade-offs showing that quantum channels derived from this structure require memory scaling as $\sqrt{n}$ for $n$ uses.

Method. The authors combine techniques from geometric group theory (constructing specific amenable groups) with resource theory in quantum information, analyzing memory/purity trade-offs for quantum channels.

Model / system. The primary models are a finitely presented elementary amenable group $\Gamma$ and an explicit quantum channel $\Phi$ acting on $\mathbb{C}^{873}$. The group structure is built using 'configuration lamps' related to Houghton's group moves.

Key observables. Sofic profile $\sigma_E(r)$, quantum memory size (qubits), and purity expenditure (bits).

Important parameters / regimes. The scaling exponents, such as $\exp(r^{1+o(1)})$ for the profile and $\sqrt{n}$ for the memory cost.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the channel being factorizable and lying in the closure of channels with finite maximally mixed baths.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the geometric setup involving points on three rays and the action of generators on configurations, which underpins the group construction.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the group-theoretic lower bound on the sofic profile, then constructing the specific group $\Gamma$, and finally translating these algebraic properties into sharp resource bounds for the associated quantum channel.

Abstract

How far from finite can an amenable group be? Every finite piece of an amenable group can be imitated by permutations of a finite set, and Cornulier's sofic profile counts how many points such an imitation needs at accuracy $1/r$. We build a finitely presented elementary amenable group with a finite piece whose profile is $\exp(r^{1+o(1)})$, close to the most our counting method can ever detect. The construction is a lamplighter with its lamps on configurations rather than on points. Houghton's group moves the points of three rays, finitely supported affine maps act on their binary configurations, and a copy of $S_3$ sits on every configuration. Then $N$ points carry $2^N$ lamps, yet any two lamps can be brought together by moves that each involve at most three points. The group embeds in Brin's group $3V$, so $3V$ too has a finite piece of nearly exponential profile. The group also gives an explicit quantum channel on $\mathbb C^{873}$ that is cheap to use once and expensive to use many times. A device that applies it $n$ times, releasing each output before the next input arrives, needs about $n/B$ qubits of memory when it may spend $B$ bits of purity, and exact devices achieve this up to polylogarithmic factors. With exchange at the optimal rate, the least memory is about $\sqrt n$, attained with purity of the same order. The channel is factorizable and lies in the closure of channels with finite maximally mixed baths, yet any such bath that imitates it to accuracy $u$ needs dimension $\exp(u^{-1+o(1)})$, although its minimal Stinespring dilation implements it exactly with a pure environment of dimension $130$. The engine is a one-round theorem: after a single use, the memory of any such device carries an approximate representation of the group.

An efficient Hamiltonian-based quantum algorithm for characters of the symmetric group

Summary. This paper presents an alternative, Hamiltonian-based quantum algorithm to prepare quantum states encoding characters of the symmetric group ($S_n$). It achieves better gate complexity bounds than prior QFT methods, providing an explicit algorithm for the Quantum Character Transform (QCT). This work advances the field by offering concrete, resource-aware quantum implementations for mathematically complex quantum states.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on Hamiltonian simulation techniques (Trotter/QSVT) to achieve polynomial gate complexity for a fundamental mathematical structure like group characters is highly relevant to developing practical quantum algorithms for quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing efficient quantum algorithms to prepare quantum states whose amplitudes encode the characters of the symmetric group ($S_n$).

Main result. The authors propose a Hamiltonian-based approach achieving gate complexities up to $\widetilde O(n^{2.5})$ for the full Quantum Character Transform (QCT), improving upon prior QFT-based estimates of $\widetilde O(n^3)$.

Method. The state preparation is achieved by simulating the evolution governed by a specific Hamiltonian ($H_s$) using Trotterization or Quantum Singular Value Transformation (QSVT).

Model / system. The system is based on the symmetric group $S_n$, utilizing fermionic operators and current operators ($J_s$) acting on a product state to encode character table columns.

Key observables. Character column state $|\chi^ u angle$, Quantum Character Transform (QCT), and entanglement entropy ($\Delta S_\lambda$) in CFTs.

Important parameters / regimes. The gate complexity scales depend on the cycle type $ u$, ranging from $O(n)$ to $\widetilde O(n^{2.5})$ in the worst case.

Assumptions / limitations. Gate count estimates rely on leading-order state-dependent Trotter error analysis, and the worst-case bound uses a QSVT-based Hamiltonian simulation.

Figures summary. The notes reference figures illustrating the Hamiltonian evolution (Fig. 1(b) for Trotterization and Fig. 1(c) for QSVT) used for state preparation.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the character state preparation problem, contrasting it with prior QFT methods, detailing the Hamiltonian simulation approach, deriving state-dependent error bounds, and finally presenting the full QCT algorithm.

Abstract

Consider a quantum state vector proportional to any given column of the character table of the symmetric group ($S_n$), corresponding to a superposition over the irreps weighted by the character values. It was recently argued that sampling from this state is classically hard under reasonable complexity-theoretic assumptions, while there exists an efficient quantum algorithm for preparing this character state using the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) over $S_n$ [arXiv:2501.12579]. We give an alternative, simpler algorithm to prepare this character state with a sequence of Hamiltonian evolutions, as well as an efficient implementation with reconfigurable qubits that uses only nearest-neighbor gates in 1D. The gate complexity, accounting for Hamiltonian simulation errors, depends on the choice of the column: For fixed target state error, the estimated sufficient total gate count ranges from as few as $O(n)$ gates for the $n$-cycle column, to $\widetilde O(n^{1.75})$ gates near the conjecturally classically hard parameter regime, to provably $\widetilde O(n^{2.5})$ gates in the worst case. In contrast, the QFT subroutine in the prior approach uses $\widetilde O(n^3)$ gates. Our gate count estimates come from leading-order state-dependent Trotter error analysis, and the proven worst-case bound relies on a QSVT-based Hamiltonian simulation method; numerical benchmarks of the Trotter-based protocol suggest that the estimates are conservative. Furthermore, we generalize our protocol to give an explicit algorithm for the quantum character transform (QCT) over $S_n$ using $\widetilde O(n^{2.5})$ gates and $O(n)$ qubits. An application to entanglement entropy of symmetric orbifold conformal field theories is also discussed.

Bell nonlocality does not imply violation of nonlocal games

Summary. This paper proves that nonlocal games, while useful for testing nonlocality, are not complete witnesses for Bell nonlocality. The authors show that there exist quantum correlations that are genuinely nonlocal but do not violate any standard nonlocal game. This establishes a strict hierarchy among the sets of possible correlations: Local $\subsetneq$ Game-Classical $\subsetneq$ Quantum.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a deep theoretical understanding of the limitations of operational tests (like nonlocal games) in fully characterizing fundamental physical principles like Bell nonlocality, which is relevant for understanding quantum information bounds.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether Bell nonlocality, a concept derived from local hidden variable theories, is fully captured by the constraints imposed by nonlocal games.

Main result. The authors prove that nonlocal games, as conventionally defined, are not complete witnesses of Bell nonlocality, demonstrating that quantum mechanics can exhibit nonlocal behaviors that violate no such game.

Method. The analysis uses functional representations (Bell functionals) to compare the sets of achievable correlations: the local set ($\mathcal{L}$), the game-classical set ($\mathcal{G}$), and the quantum set ($\mathcal{Q}$).

Model / system. The study is set in the context of bipartite Bell scenarios $(X, Y, A, B)$, analyzing correlations achievable by local hidden variables, quantum mechanics, and the set defined by all deterministic nonlocal games.

Key observables. The key observables are the correlation functions $p(a, b|x, y)$ and the resulting values of Bell functionals $\langle M, p angle$.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis is shown to hold for bipartite scenarios with input sizes $X, Y \geq 2$ and output sizes $A, B \geq 4$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the original definition of nonlocal games using deterministic predicates and the assumption that the set $\mathcal{G}$ is defined by the intersection of constraints derived from these games.

Figures summary. Figures schematically illustrate the relationship between the local set ($\mathcal{L}$), the game-classical set ($\mathcal{G}$), and the quantum set ($\mathcal{Q}$), showing the existence of quantum correlations in $\mathcal{G} \setminus \mathcal{L}$.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes that $\mathcal{G}$ is a polytope whose facets are nonlocal games. It then proves the strict inclusion $\mathcal{L} \subsetneq \mathcal{G}$ and, crucially, demonstrates that quantum mechanics can realize correlations in the region $\mathcal{G} \setminus \mathcal{L}$.

Abstract

Nonlocal games offer a conceptually clear way to reason about Bell nonlocality. However, we show that as originally defined, and most commonly used, i.e. with a deterministic predicate, they cannot be complete witnesses of nonlocality. Concretely, we prove the existence of nonlocal behaviours which do not violate any such nonlocal game. Furthermore, we also show that quantum mechanics can produce these game-classical nonlocal behaviours. We prove that this phenomenon exists in every bipartite scenario $(X,Y,A,B)$ with $X,Y\geq2$ and $A,B\geq4$.

Benchmarking Ansatze for Pauli Correlation Encoding in the Maximum Independent Set Problem

Authors: Cian C. Reeves, Aaron C. Kemp, Richard Padbury, Eva Lia Tarquini, Christoph Kloeffel, Vedangi Pathak, Hamed Mohammadbagherpoor, Vincent Beltrani
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03949v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work benchmarks Pauli Correlation Encoding (PCE) for solving the Maximum Independent Set problem on quantum hardware simulators. The authors compare several quantum circuit ansatze and find that ansatz selection is crucial for achieving good approximation ratios. Key findings highlight that representability constraints limit performance, and increasing the number of available qubits significantly boosts raw solution quality.

Why it may be interesting. It provides deep insight into the practical limitations of variational quantum algorithms, specifically how encoding constraints (representability) interact with hardware resource limitations (qubit count/compression) in optimization tasks.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of solving computationally hard combinatorial optimization problems, such as Maximum Independent Set (MIS), using near-term quantum hardware.

Main result. The choice of quantum ansatz is critical for solution quality, with specific ansatz families significantly outperforming others; furthermore, increasing qubit availability (reducing compression) improves raw solution quality.

Method. The study benchmarks various Pauli Correlation Encoding (PCE) ansatz designs by mapping the MIS problem to a QUBO formulation and optimizing parameters using classical optimizers.

Model / system. The model uses quantum circuits parameterized by ansatze (A, B, C, D) to estimate Pauli string expectation values, which are then used to approximate the solution to the MIS problem.

Key observables. Approximation ratio of the found MIS size relative to the exact optimum, and the feasibility of the resulting binary solution.

Important parameters / regimes. Compression ratio (related to the number of available Pauli strings/qubits), circuit repetitions, and the specific ansatz structure.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that PCE can provide a qubit-efficient alternative to full one-hot encoding, though the authors note that interdependence of correlators remains a limitation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the approximation ratios for MIS instances of varying sizes (134, 314, 569 nodes) using different ansatzes and varying levels of Pauli string compression.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the need for quantum optimization for NP-hard problems, details the PCE framework using MIS as a benchmark, compares multiple ansatz designs and compression ratios, and concludes by analyzing the role of representability constraints.

Abstract

The increasing complexity of 5G and emerging 6G wireless networks has intensified the need for scalable optimization techniques capable of addressing computationally challenging combinatorial problems. While quantum optimization offers a promising alternative, current near-term quantum hardware remains limited by qubit availability, noise, and scalability constraints. Pauli Correlation Encoding (PCE) has emerged as a qubit-efficient optimization framework that addresses these challenges through polynomial variable compression. However, the impact of compression on representability and solution recovery remains poorly understood. In this work, we investigate the hypothesis that increasing compression ratios introduce representability constraints arising from algebraic closure relationships among encoded Pauli observables. Using Maximum Independent Set benchmark problems, we evaluate multiple PCE ansatz designs and compression ratios. We find that ansatz selection is critical to both solution quality and feasibility, with two ansatz families consistently outperforming alternatives and achieving approximation ratios approaching 0.9. Furthermore, increasing circuit repetitions provides limited benefit and can degrade performance. Most notably, reducing compression by increasing the number of available qubits significantly improves raw solution quality, while post-processed performance remains comparatively stable. These findings suggest that representability effects play an important role in compressed PCE encodings and provide new insight into the relationship among compression, ansatz design, decoding, and solution quality in scalable quantum optimization.

Bounding Two-Way Average Communication Cost of Simulating Quantum Correlations

Authors: Kai-Siang Chen, Gelo Noel M. Tabia, Bo-An Tsai, Swati Kumari, Yeong-Cherng Liang

Summary. This paper develops sophisticated lower bounds on the average classical communication cost needed to simulate quantum correlations in nonlocal games. By adapting communication complexity tools and constructing an LP hierarchy, the authors provide precise quantitative measures of quantum nonlocality. This work is crucial for understanding the resource theory of quantum information.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous, quantifiable measures (communication cost) for the nonclassical resources inherent in quantum correlations, which is fundamental to understanding the limits of classical simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to determine the minimum classical communication cost required to simulate quantum correlations, particularly in the challenging setting of input-average communication cost.

Main result. They derive explicit lower bounds for the average communication cost for simulating nonlocal quantum correlations, achieving specific bounds for parallel Magic-Square-winning correlations and the CHSH game.

Method. The authors adapt communication-complexity techniques, utilizing rooted public trees and developing a Linear Programming (LP) hierarchy of lower bounds based on deterministic correlations.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on bipartite Bell scenarios involving Alice and Bob, comparing quantum correlations (from quantum measurements) against classical correlations achievable via communication protocols.

Key observables. Input-average communication cost ($ar{C}_\sigma^\mu$), winning probability in nonlocal games, and the structure of communication protocols represented by public trees.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of parallel copies ($n$), the input distribution ($\mu$), and the specific quantum correlation being simulated (e.g., CHSH).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of an optimal finite protocol and assumes the input distribution has full support for key lemmas.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates a rooted public tree structure used to model communication protocols and calculate the associated communication cost. Figure 2 shows a one-bit communication strategy winning the CHSH game.

Paper structure. The paper builds by first establishing lower bounds from nonlocal games, then developing the framework using the winning-rectangle and winning-probability bounds for parallel repetitions, and finally introducing an LP hierarchy to approach the exact input-average cost.

Abstract

Bell nonlocal correlations cannot be reproduced by local hidden-variable models without communication, making classical communication cost a natural quantitative measure of nonlocality. Although finite communication always suffices to simulate any correlation in a fixed finite Bell scenario, determining the minimum amount needed to (exactly) simulate any given nonlocal correlation remains challenging, especially in the average-cost setting. Here, we derive lower bounds on input-average communication cost from nonlocal games, allowing variable-length, fully interactive two-way protocols. When applied to parallel games, these bounds yield explicit finite quantum correlations for which the exact simulation cost exceeds any prescribed finite input-average communication budget. For $n$ parallel Magic-Square-winning correlations, we prove a lower bound of $n\log_2(3/2)$ bits and give a one-way communication protocol that attains this rate asymptotically. For the quantum correlation maximally winning the $n$-copy of the Clauser--Horne--Shimony--Holt nonlocal game, we obtain an input-average lower bound of approximately $0.04627n$ bits for exact simulation. Finally, we formulate a hierarchy of lower bounds and complementary upper bounds using deterministic correlations up to chosen communication costs. Both recover the exact input-average cost at their final levels, and if the bounds agree at lower levels, their common value yields the exact input-average cost without needing to consider all communication strategies.

Characterizing Quantum Advantage for Generalizations of the Boolean Hidden Matching Problem

Authors: Mark Bun, Joao F. Doriguello, John Kallaugher, Nadezhda Voronova
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06789v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work analyzes the communication complexity of a generalized hidden matching problem, linking the computational difficulty directly to the sign degree of the underlying Boolean function $f$. It establishes precise classical and quantum communication bounds, proving that a polynomial quantum advantage exists if and only if the sign degree $d$ is two or greater. This provides a deep theoretical understanding of quantum computational separation in communication tasks.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous characterization of quantum advantage based on a purely algebraic property (sign degree) of a Boolean function is highly relevant for understanding the limits of quantum communication protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper characterizes the quantum advantage for solving generalizations of the Boolean Hidden Matching Problem, specifically the $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition problem.

Main result. The sign degree $d$ of the function $f$ completely characterizes the communication complexity, showing that quantum advantage exists if and only if $d \ge 2$.

Method. The authors use Boolean Fourier analysis and hypercontractivity to establish rigorous classical and quantum communication lower bounds based on the sign degree $d$.

Model / system. The model is the one-way communication complexity setting where Alice holds an $n$-bit string and Bob holds disjoint blocks of indices, requiring Bob to determine if function evaluations agree or disagree with given labels.

Key observables. Classical and quantum one-way communication complexity (in bits/qubits), and the sign degree $d$ of the function $f$.

Important parameters / regimes. The sign degree $d = ext{deg}_{\pm}(f)$, and the input size $n$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the promise that the function evaluations either all agree or all disagree with the labels, and it addresses scenarios where the independence assumption breaks down.

Paper structure. The paper establishes bounds by first analyzing the problem structure, then developing new tools using Fourier analysis to prove lower bounds dependent on $d$, and finally deriving upper bounds that match these lower bounds to achieve characterization.

Abstract

We study the one-way communication complexity of the $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition problem. Here, Alice is given an $n$-bit string and Bob is given $Ω(n)$ disjoint blocks of its indices together with a string of labels. Under the promise that the evaluations of $f$ on these blocks either agree with all of the labels or disagree with all of them, Bob must determine which is the case using a single message from Alice. This problem generalizes the Boolean Hidden Matching and Hidden Hypermatching problems, which capture the special case where $f$ is the parity function. We establish classical and quantum communication bounds for $f$-Boolean Hidden Partition in terms of the sign degree $d$ of $f$, proving a conjecture of Doriguello and Montanaro (TQC 2020). Their prior work gave logarithmic communication upper bounds for classical protocols when $d \le 1$ and quantum protocols when $d \le 2$, as well as polynomial lower bounds for certain structured functions with larger sign degree. We show that for every $f$ of sign degree $d \ge 2$, the randomized classical communication complexity of this problem is $Θ(n^{1-1/d})$ while its quantum communication complexity lies between $Ω(n^{1-2/d})$ and $\bO{n^{1-1/\lceil d/2 \rceil} \log n}$. This completely characterizes the functions $f$ admitting polynomial quantum advantage as those for which $d \ge 2$, with a new infinite family of exponential separations given by the case $d = 2$.

Abstract

We give a closed-form finite-dimensional construction of quantum channels with real algebraic entries exhibiting arbitrarily large violations of minimum-output Rényi entropy additivity uniformly above any prescribed positive Rényi order, using a different finite realization based on direct products of free groups and fixed-field high-girth graphs. At the von Neumann order, a separate specialization gives an explicit channel with gap greater than $1/1024$ using $37608898$ input qubits and $8$ output qubits.

Summary. This theoretical work analyzes the resource cost of preparing multiple copies of a quantum state using collective quantum control. It proves that this cost is strictly nonadditive, meaning that coordinating the preparation of several copies can achieve the target state at a lower total cost than preparing each copy individually. This establishes a new 'many-copy geometry' for state synthesis in quantum information theory.

Why it may be interesting. The finding that collective control can yield non-additive advantages in state synthesis provides a new geometric understanding of resource costs beyond simple independent operations, which is crucial for designing efficient quantum protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the resource cost for preparing multiple copies of a quantum state (the many-copy problem) is additive when using collective quantum control.

Main result. Collective state preparation is shown to be strictly nonadditive, meaning the asymptotic cost per copy can be lower than the cost of preparing a single copy.

Method. The authors derive exact single-qubit preparation cost formulas and use entropy bounds (Rényi entropy) to establish lower bounds on the preparation rate.

Model / system. The model uses quantum state preparation governed by Pauli-product rotations $e^{i\phi P}$, where the cost is the total rotation angle $\sum |\phi_j|$. The analysis focuses on comparing single-copy costs ($C_1$) versus collective/asymptotic costs ($C_\infty$).

Abstract

Quantum state preparation is a basic primitive of quantum computation and control, but its resource cost is usually analyzed for one target state or under an independent-copy construction. Here we study the many-copy problem under weighted Pauli control, where a Pauli-product rotation $e^{iφP}$ is charged by its rotation angle $|φ|$, independently of the weight of $P$. We derive the exact single-qubit preparation cost and show that it is strictly nonadditive: a four-pulse collective protocol prepares two $T$ states at lower cost than two optimal one-copy protocols. We further identify finite regions of the Bloch sphere with collective saving, including trajectories that remain product throughout, showing that transient entanglement is not the only mechanism behind the advantage. Complementarily, a dimension-independent Rényi-entropy speed limit gives an extensive lower bound for every coherent target and an exponential fidelity penalty below a finite preparation rate. These results establish a many-copy geometry of state synthesis in which uncorrelated outputs can nevertheless benefit from collective control.

Comparing thresholds of XZZX and rotated surface codes under spatio-temporal correlated noise

Summary. This paper rigorously tests the performance of XZZX and rotated surface codes under realistic, correlated spatio-temporal noise, moving beyond simple i.i.d. error assumptions. By using a specialized hypergraph decoder, the authors demonstrate that the code's threshold is highly sensitive to the noise's geometric structure. The results highlight the necessity of accounting for correlated errors in the design of fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. It provides concrete, quantitative results on how specific geometric structures of correlated noise break the assumptions underlying standard QEC analysis, which is crucial for designing fault-tolerant quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the limitations of assuming independent and identically distributed noise when evaluating quantum error correction codes.

Main result. The XZZX code outperforms the rotated surface code under strong Z-biased correlated noise, and its performance exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on the noise bias ratio.

Method. The study employs the Minimum Weight Parity Factor (MWPF) decoder to simulate code performance under complex, spatio-temporal correlated noise models.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on comparing the error thresholds of the XZZX and rotated surface codes, which are topological quantum error-correcting codes.

Key observables. Error threshold ($p_{th}$), logical error rate, and the dependence of performance on the noise bias ratio ($\eta$).

Important parameters / regimes. Noise bias ratio ($\eta$), code distance ($d$), and the strength of spatio-temporal correlations.

Abstract

While independent and identically distributed noise is typically assumed in quantum computing research, complex error models such as correlated and spatio-temporal errors remain underexplored. In this paper, the performance of $\mathrm{XZZX}$ and rotated surface codes is evaluated under these complex noise models. Standard graph-based decoders struggle with the hyperedges generated by correlated errors on bias-tailored codes. To address this, we employ the Minimum Weight Parity Factor (MWPF) decoder, which natively processes syndrome hypergraphs without artificial edge decomposition. Our simulations show that under strong Z-biased circuit-level correlated noise ($η=100$), the threshold of the rotated code degrades to $\approx 0.36\%$, while the $\mathrm{XZZX}$ code drops to $\approx 0.70\%$. Furthermore, a distinct non-monotonic threshold dependence is observed for the $\mathrm{XZZX}$ code across different bias ratios. While the $\mathrm{XZZX}$ code effectively absorbs high-density Z-type hyperedges at extreme biases, it exhibits a threshold minimum at moderate biases ($η\approx 10$) because transverse X and Y errors actively bridge the diagonal strings, breaking the code's one-dimensional decoupling symmetry. These findings demonstrate that utilizing hypergraph-aware decoders is critical, as the performance of bias-tailored codes is highly sensitive to the geometric structure of spatio-temporal correlated noise.

Along the way, we introduce some new tools for handling quantum query lower bounds, including (a) a new multiplicative'' variant of the $γ_2$ norm (which we use in place of the multiplicative adversary method for proving the direct product theorem), and (b) a newShaltiel-free'' measure of quantum query complexity, which we show characterizes the composition behavior of distributional quantum query complexity and satisfies pleasing properties.

Abstract

We ask whether a neural network can learn a structure-preserving map from a compressed latent space to a Hilbert-space representation. Using an 8-dimensional autoencoder bottleneck on MNIST and $n$-qubit product-state targets from PCA-based angle encoding, we report four findings. Although the target angles are generated by a nonlinear sigmoid transformation of the latent projections, the resulting mapping is well approximated by a linear function over the observed latent distribution: linear regression from $z$ to the true target angles achieves $R^{2} = 0.98$, while regression to the MLP's recovered angles achieves $R^{2} = 0.91$. The learned map's primary direction is strongly aligned with the target-induced direction, with cosine similarity $0.989$, while remaining nearly orthogonal to the input's principal direction, with cosine similarity $0.002$. The map is genuinely rank-4: removing any singular direction degrades inner-product preservation by $2.5$--$5.9\times$ despite a singular-value spectrum with two dominant and two small values. The learned subspace does not coincide with the PCA basis used to construct the target, and different random seeds recover the same primary direction but diverge in higher ranks. Finally, kernel ridge regression with an RBF kernel outperforms a tuned MLP (IP error $0.0144$ vs.\ $0.0197$), suggesting that for approximately linear structure-preserving mappings, classical kernel methods may be a simpler and more effective alternative.

Abstract

Fault-tolerant quantum computation typically relies on a fixed set of logical primitives, while realizing other desired operations can require substantial resources. We introduce irrep surface codes (ISCs), two-dimensional topological quantum error-correcting codes engineered to transversally implement a prescribed set of logical gates. The engineered gates only need to satisfy two conditions: (i) they generate a finite group, which, up to global phases, is forced by the Eastin-Knill theorem, and (ii) they are irreducible, meaning that any operator commuting with the set of gates must be proportional to the identity. This accommodates arbitrary finite groups of diagonal gates and arbitrary reversible classical gates, since each can be embedded into a larger irreducible finite group of gates. It also enables logical gates outside every finite level of the Clifford hierarchy. To incorporate these operations into surface-code computations, we further develop lattice-surgery protocols to transfer the encoded information between surface codes and ISCs obeying either of two sufficient conditions. Together, these results establish a systematic method for tailoring topological codes to desired logical operations and integrating them into conventional computations.
