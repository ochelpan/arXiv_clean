Summary. This work quantifies how quantum vacuum noise, captured by the Unruh Wightman function, perturbs the geometry of a black hole. By stochastically reconstructing the geometry using the stress covariance, the authors predict measurable fluctuations in key parameters like the horizon radius and surface-gravity temperature. This provides a finite-resolution description of quantum backreaction effects on black hole structure.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on general relativity, the methodology of using quantum field correlations (Unruh state) to constrain geometric observables via stochastic reconstruction shares conceptual links with modeling noise and fluctuations in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses how quantum stress fluctuations, beyond the mean Hawking flux, perturb the geometry of a black hole.

Main result. For specified noise amplitudes, the analysis predicts relative widths of 0.0185 for the local horizon radius and 0.0483 for the surface-gravity temperature parameter.

Method. The authors connect the exterior Unruh Wightman function to a finite-band stochastic reconstruction of the Schwarzschild geometry by propagating the connected stress covariance through a density-driven radial response.

Model / system. The system is a black hole described by a Schwarzschild reference geometry, with quantum noise input derived from the complete exterior Unruh Wightman function.

Key observables. Local horizon radius fluctuations ($\delta r_h$), surface-gravity temperature parameter fluctuations ($\delta T$), and the connected stress covariance.

Important parameters / regimes. Noise amplitude ($\lambda_Q$), finite-band resolution, and the Schwarzschild geometry parameters (GM/r).

Assumptions / limitations. The calculation relies on a density-driven constraint response (a closure assumption) and uses a Gaussian reconstruction model for the fluctuations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the field-to-geometry construction, show the source spectrum, and plot the relative widths of the reconstructed radius and temperature distributions against the noise amplitude.

Paper structure. The paper builds from identifying the need to use field correlations (Unruh function) over mean flux, details the stochastic reconstruction method using covariance propagation, and concludes with quantitative results for the observable fluctuations.

Abstract

The mean Hawking flux does not determine how quantum stress fluctuations perturb a black-hole geometry. That information resides in field correlations, including vacuum contributions absent from the outgoing luminosity. Here we connect the complete exterior Unruh Wightman function to a finite-band stochastic reconstruction of Schwarzschild geometry. We retain the incoming vacuum and both outgoing frequency branches, form the connected stress covariance before angular projection, and propagate its monopole through a density-driven radial response. A shared Gaussian reconstruction then resolves how the value and derivative of the lapse control different geometric observables. For the specified covariance and noise amplitude, 2,200 realizations give relative widths of 0.0185 for the local horizon radius and 0.0483 for the surface-gravity temperature parameter, consistently across histograms and reconstruction checks. Covariance derivatives explain this unequal sensitivity. The construction separates the quantum-state input from the gravitational closure and spatial sampling assumptions, providing a reproducible finite-resolution description of how Hawking-field correlations affect horizon positions and surface-gravity statistics.

Summary. This work reports the observation of Floquet dressing in the bulk metallic bands of 2H-NbSe2 using time-resolved ARPES. By analyzing polarization-dependent sideband replicas, the authors demonstrate that the observed signal is best explained by a coherent interference model combining initial-state and final-state dressing effects. This extends the utility of Floquet engineering to bulk metallic conductors.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to observe and model coherent optical dressing in bulk metals, rather than just surface states, opens a new avenue for controlling metallic transport and collective electronic phases using light.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to extend the control of electronic states via Floquet dressing from surface states to the itinerant bulk bands of metals.

Main result. The authors successfully identify Floquet dressing in the bulk metallic bands of 2H-NbSe2, showing that the coherent Floquet-Volkov interference model best describes the observed momentum-space weight redistribution.

Method. The research combines time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (tr-ARPES) experiments with detailed theoretical modeling of coherent Floquet-Volkov interference.

Model / system. The physical system is 2H-NbSe2, a layered multiband metal. The theoretical framework models the dressing effect using both initial-state (Bloch) and final-state (Volkov) approximations, calculating the total intensity via coherent interference.

Key observables. Photon-shifted replicas in the unoccupied state window, polarization-dependent redistribution of replica weight, and momentum distribution curves (MDCs).

Important parameters / regimes. Pump photon energy ($\hbar\omega_{ ext{pump}} = 1.2$ eV), probe energy (18 eV), and polarization angles ($\phi$).

Assumptions / limitations. The initial-state contribution must be separated from final-state photoemission effects, and the analysis relies on comparing coherent interference against incoherent sums.

Figures summary. Figures demonstrate time-delay dependence separating field-synchronous signals, constant-energy contours showing replica structure, and MDC comparisons validating the coherent interference model over incoherent sums.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the feasibility of the measurement using tr-ARPES, then using polarization dependence and MDC analysis to distinguish between initial-state and final-state dressing, culminating in a detailed theoretical comparison between coherent and incoherent interference models.

Abstract

Floquet dressing offers a route to controlling electronic states through periodic optical driving. Its realization in solids has centred primarily on topological-insulator surface states, semiconductors and two-dimensional Dirac semimetals. Extending this control to bulk metals would enable manipulation of the itinerant states underlying metallic transport and collective order. Yet strong screening and rapid scattering have long cast doubt on its feasibility in bulk metals, while photoelectron dressing complicates its identification. Here we identify Floquet dressing of the overlapping, bulk-derived metallic bands of 2H-NbSe2 using time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy. Photon-shifted replicas in a sparse unoccupied-state window retain the parent-band dispersions and appear only during pump--probe overlap. Rotating the pump's linear polarization at fixed probe polarization redistributes replica weight between momentum-space branches. With a common parameter set, a model accounting for the optical fields inside and outside the solid captures this redistribution through coherent Floquet--Volkov interference more closely than either pathway alone or their incoherent intensity sum. These findings extend Floquet dressing to the itinerant bulk bands of a multiband metal without relying on the observation of energy gaps opened by hybridization between parent bands and their photon-dressed replicas, providing a basis for exploring how coherent optical dressing couples to metallic transport and collective electronic phases.

Summary. This paper develops a rigorous theory quantifying when the energy convergence achieved by variational quantum algorithms fails to guarantee the accuracy of measured observables. It derives bounds showing that the observable error can be amplified relative to the energy error, a mechanism tied to the observable's static susceptibility. The findings are validated across diverse physical models, providing essential diagnostic tools for quantum simulation practitioners.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides crucial theoretical diagnostics for practitioners using variational quantum algorithms (VQE, DMRG) on real hardware or complex models, warning them that energy convergence alone is insufficient for guaranteeing observable accuracy.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper formalizes and quantifies the failure of the assumption that energy convergence implies observable convergence when using variational principles in quantum simulations.

Main result. The observable error can be significantly larger than the energy error, a phenomenon called non-linear error amplification, which is quantified by the static susceptibility of the observable.

Method. The authors derive rigorous upper and lower bounds for the 'certification radius' of observables by analyzing the state decomposition into ground and excited subspaces.

Model / system. The analysis is general but is demonstrated on specific systems including the transverse-field Ising chain (VQE), two weakly coupled hydrogen chains (DMRG), and Lithium Fluoride (KQD).

Key observables. Energy error ($\epsilon$), observable error ($\delta_O$), static susceptibility ($\chi_O$), and the expectation value of the observable $\langle O angle$.

Important parameters / regimes. The energy error $\epsilon$, the spectral gap $\Delta$, and the static susceptibility $\chi_O$ are critical parameters governing the error bounds.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on decomposing the state and bounding the resulting population and coherence terms, with specific bounds derived assuming non-zero gaps.

Figures summary. The notes detail simulations on the Ising chain, H5 chains, and LiF, showing how different ansatzes or symmetry constraints (e.g., parity-preserving vs. full) affect the observable error.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing general theoretical bounds (Theorem 1 and Theorem 2) relating $\delta_O$ to $\chi_O$, and then validates these findings by applying the theory to three distinct numerical examples: VQE on Ising, DMRG on H5 chains, and KQD on LiF.

Abstract

Algorithms which prepare states via the variational principle assume that closeness in energy implies closeness in state; we formalise how this assumption can fail by showing that a ground-state energy estimate converged to within $ε$ of the true value is compatible with observable expectation values in error by up to $\sqrt{2εχ_O}$ at leading order, where $χ_O$ is the static susceptibility of the observable. As a result, the observable error can be orders of magnitude larger than the energy error, a mechanism we call non-linear error amplification, and because a variational algorithm cannot guarantee the correctness of these properties from energy alone, energy does not necessarily certify an observable. In light of our findings, we examine three algorithms: the variational quantum eigensolver on the transverse-field Ising chain, the density-matrix renormalisation group on a pair of weakly coupled hydrogen chains, and Krylov quantum diagonalisation on lithium fluoride. In each case we demonstrate that a well-converged energy can leave observables uncertified.

Summary. This paper reports extensive experiments showing that photon counts from various light sources exhibit a universal $1/f^2$ spectral signature at low frequencies. After ruling out instrumental and source-specific noise, the authors propose that the fluctuations follow a doubly stochastic Poisson process, suggesting a fundamental physical mechanism beyond simple classical noise sources.

Why it may be interesting. The robust, universal nature of the $1/f^2$ noise, independent of the light source physics, suggests a fundamental, non-trivial underlying physical process that could relate to complex stochastic processes in quantum optics or condensed matter noise sources.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the universal origin of a $1/f^2$ spectral signature observed in photon-count fluctuations at baseband across diverse optical sources.

Main result. The observed $1/f^2$ spectrum is robust across different sources and cannot be explained by standard technical noise or source/detector fluctuations. The underlying process is statistically modeled as a doubly stochastic Poisson process.

Method. The authors performed long-term photon-counting experiments, analyzing the resulting periodograms and scalograms to characterize the low-frequency spectral density.

Model / system. The experiments utilized five classes of light sources (LEDs, SLEDs, LDs, incandescent, betaluminescent) and employed photon-counting detectors over periods up to 11.6 days. The analysis compares these measurements against theoretical models like Brownian motion and gravitational wave backgrounds.

Key observables. Photon-count spectra exhibiting a consistent $1/f^2$ form over the frequency range $1 imes 10^{-6} \le f \le 5 imes 10^{-4}$ Hz.

Important parameters / regimes. Timescale range $33$ min $\le T_f \le 11.6$ d; spectral slope of $f^{-2}$; comparison to GWB spectral slopes ($f^{-7/3}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The fluctuations are assumed to follow a doubly stochastic Poisson process with a slowly varying random intensity, and the analysis compares the results against standard models like GWB.

Figures summary. Figures display averaged periodograms and scalograms showing the characteristic $1/f^2$ or $T^2$ decay for various sources, and compare these to source current/voltage periodograms.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the empirical observation of the $1/f^2$ spectrum across multiple sources, rules out common noise sources, statistically characterizes the process, and finally compares the spectral signature to astrophysical models like the Gravitational Wave Background.

Abstract

A series of 11.6-d-duration photon-counting experiments, employing a broad variety of light sources with different statistical properties and optical spectra, were carried out over a 1.5-yr period. All of the photon-count spectra at baseband followed a common $1/f^2$ form over the frequency range $1 \times 10^{-6} \leqslant f \leqslant 5 \times 10^{-4}$ Hz, corresponding to a timescale range $33$ min $ \leqslant T_f \leqslant 11.6$ d, where $T_f \equiv 1/f$. The lower and upper timescale limits were established by the photodetector noise floor and the duration of the individual experiments, respectively. Unlike ordinary Brownian motion, all of the measured photon-count sample paths exhibited irregular long-timescale fluctuations, with durations ranging from hours to days. It has been established that the photon-count spectra cannot plausibly be ascribed to fluctuations of the current, voltage, or temperature of the source or the detector, nor to technical sources of noise associated with the optical system or local environment. Although the physical origin of the photon-count fluctuations remains unresolved, several heterodox hypotheses are set forth. From a statistical point-of-view, the photon counts appear to follow a doubly stochastic Poisson process with a slowly varying random intensity. Analogous experiments that rely on ionizing radiation and direct-conversion solid-state radiation detectors are proposed.

Abstract

Abstract

Closure phase enables imaging with ground-based interferometric arrays by canceling telescope-dependent phase errors due to atmospheric fluctuations. It can be obtained by measuring fringe phases on individual baselines forming a triangle and summing them. We show that closure phases can be measured more accurately with fewer photons by performing joint measurements on multiple photons. We first establish that at least three photons from the same atmospheric temporal phase-stability window are required for closure-phase information to survive the unknown telescope-dependent phase noise, and show that joint three-photon measurements already provide an advantage over single-photon measurements in the minimal three-telescope setting. We then turn to large arrays and consider estimating all bispectra---complex quantities whose phases are the closure phases---under a global mean-square error criterion. Although an $m$-telescope array contains $Θ(m^2)$ independent triangle bispectra, we show that physical constraints on the visibilities make the total information content grow only nearly linearly with $m$ at fixed accuracy. Exploiting this structure, we construct an efficient protocol using at most three-photon joint measurements with photon cost $\tilde{O}(m)$, whereas any sequential single-photon protocol requires $Ω(m^2)$ photons. When the number of detected photons per temporal stability window scales linearly with the array size, this allows the required exposure time to remain independent of $m$ up to logarithmic factors. We further analyze the complementary high-precision regime using multiparameter quantum metrology and find a similar advantage for collective measurements. Overall, our results show that quantum information processing techniques can substantially improve the sensitivity and robustness of optical interferometry.

Abstract

Self-propelled particles that only repel one another can still separate into a dense and a dilute phase, a process known as motility-induced phase separation (MIPS). In a clean system the domains coarsen like any conserved mixture: domains grow as $\ell(t)\sim t^{1/3}$. Living and synthetic swimmers, however, move through rough, porous, or patterned surroundings. Using large-scale simulations of active Brownian particles, we ask how frozen heterogeneity changes MIPS coarsening. We introduce disorder in two ways, as a quenched random force field acting on particle positions and as a quenched random torque field acting on their orientations. Both destroy Lifshitz-Slyozov growth. A transient power law with a disorder-dependent exponent gives way to activated dynamics in which the effective dynamic exponent grows without bound and domains grow at most logarithmically. The crossover obeys the scaling form known from the random-field Ising model, and the domain morphology depends on disorder strength, so superuniversality fails. The two kinds of disorder act through different mechanisms. Random forces trap particles in the sinks of a random drift field once the drift beats self-propulsion. Random torques act as a sign-random, quenched chirality that erodes persistence and lowers the local Péclet number toward its critical value. Our results show that quenched disorder is a relevant perturbation for the kinetics of active phase separation and connect MIPS coarsening to the physics of pinned interfaces in disordered magnets.

Abstract

Abstract
