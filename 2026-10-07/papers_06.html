Improved Adaptive Estimation of Quantum Partition Functions with Heisenberg Scaling

Authors: Yufei Wang, Daniel Stilck França, Samuel Slezak
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06197v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper presents quantum algorithms to estimate the log partition function of an $n$-qubit quantum Hamiltonian with Heisenberg scaling. By employing a recursive-doubling identity over a cooling schedule, the authors significantly improve query complexity by utilizing a coherent-temperature access model. This advances the field's ability to simulate equilibrium thermodynamics for quantum many-body systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a quantum computational tool to calculate fundamental thermodynamic quantities (free energy) for complex quantum many-body systems, which is crucial for simulating quantum materials and phase transitions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of estimating the log partition function, $f(eta) = \ln ext{Tr}(e^{-eta H})$, for an $n$-qubit quantum Hamiltonian $H$. The goal is to achieve Heisenberg scaling in the required quantum query complexity.

Main result. The authors achieve an expected query complexity of $ ilde{O}(n/\epsilon)$ using a coherent-temperature access model, which is shown to be optimal up to polylogarithmic factors for block-encoding queries.

Method. The estimation is achieved by decomposing the log partition function into a sum over a slowly-varying cooling schedule, utilizing recursive-doubling identities involving quantum overlaps and reweighting expectations.

Model / system. The system is an $n$-qubit quantum system governed by a Hamiltonian $H$, where the estimation targets the free energy encoded in the partition function $Z(eta)$. The analysis considers block-encoding representations of $H$ and the use of thermofield-double states.

Key observables. The primary observable is the log partition function, $f(eta)$. Other key quantities include the thermal state $ ho_H^eta$ and the overlaps between purified Gibbs states.

Important parameters / regimes. The key parameters are the additive error $\epsilon$, the system size $n$, the maximum energy scale $h=O(n)$, and the inverse temperature $eta$. The scaling goal is Heisenberg scaling, $e^{O(1/\epsilon)}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the Hamiltonian $H$ is bounded ($0 \preceq H \preceq hI$) and relies on the ability to prepare thermofield-double states and perform controlled operations, particularly in the coherent access model.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem of partition function estimation, develops the theoretical framework using recursive-doubling identities over cooling schedules, and compares the query complexity bounds achieved by fixed-temperature versus coherent-temperature access models, culminating in a proof of optimality for the latter.

Abstract

We give quantum algorithms that estimate the log partition function of an $n$-qubit quantum Hamiltonian to additive error $ε$ with Heisenberg scaling, while minimizing calls to thermal-state purification preparation. The input is a block encoding of $H$, unitaries preparing thermofield-double states at chosen inverse temperatures, with their inverses and controlled versions, and an adaptive slowly-varying cooling schedule. The system-size bounds below assume $0\preceq H\preceq hI$ with $h=O(n)$ and block-encoding normalization $α=Θ(h)$. For constant inverse temperature, we show that such a schedule of length $O(\sqrt n)$ always exists and can be generated from overlap estimates with $\widetilde O(\sqrt n)$ further preparations. Given such a schedule and classically chosen temperatures, we estimate the log partition function with $\widetilde O(n^{5/4}/ε)$ expected calls to state preparation and to the block encoding, improving by $n^{1/4}$ on non-adaptive strategies. To achieve our result, we use a recursive-doubling identity that expresses each schedule increment as a weighted sum of logarithms of thermofield-double overlaps and of short imaginary-time steps implemented by quantum singular value transformation combined with previously developed variance reduction techniques. If thermofield doubles can be prepared controlled on a temperature register, a quantum inverse-binomial log estimator combined with quantum mean estimation reduces both query counts to $\widetilde O(n/ε)$. We show that the latter scaling is optimal in block-encoding queries up to polylogarithmic factors and discuss end-to-end complexities for one-dimensional partition-function estimation.

Onset of Melting in Finite Ion Crystals: The Role of Structural Isomerization

Authors: Boris V. Pashinsky, Boris Blinov
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.05624v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Frenkel-Kontorova 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical study explores how finite, trapped ion crystals undergo melting by examining the role of structural isomerization. Using molecular dynamics simulations, the authors show that the melting process is gradual and highly dependent on which metastable structural configuration the crystal occupies. This clarifies that structural rearrangements dictate the pathway of the phase transition in these confined quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed, microscopic picture of phase transitions in confined quantum systems, directly relevant to understanding the thermal stability and dynamics of trapped ion arrays, which are key platforms in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the microscopic mechanism of melting in finite two-dimensional ion crystals, specifically focusing on how structural isomerization influences the melting pathways.

Main result. Melting is a gradual, thermally-driven crossover characterized by the accumulation of radial and angular disorder, and the final melting behavior strongly depends on the metastable structural isomer the crystal initially occupies.

Method. The study employs molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and Metropolis-Hastings equilibrium sampling to analyze thermal fluctuations and structural rearrangements.

Model / system. The system is a finite two-dimensional ion crystal confined in an anisotropic harmonic trap, governed by Coulomb interactions. The analysis considers crystal sizes from N=4 to N=101 ions.

Key observables. Melting probability (P_melt), radial fluctuations, angular disorder, Lindemann parameters, and the energy landscape associated with structural isomers.

Important parameters / regimes. Ion number (N), trap anisotropy ($\gamma$), and temperature (T).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis treats the melting process as a finite-size crossover rather than a sharp thermodynamic phase transition, and it relies on classical molecular dynamics simulations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate melting phase diagrams (P_melt vs T, $\gamma$), the dependence of the melting transition width on N ($W(N) \propto N^{-0.52}$), and comparisons of thermal stabilities between different structural isomers for a fixed N.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the melting transition by first establishing the dependence on N and $\gamma$, then introducing the concept of structural isomerism, and finally using detailed time-resolved diagnostics (radial/angular fluctuations) to distinguish the melting mechanism from simple soft-mode instabilities.

Abstract

We theoretically investigate the microscopic mechanism of melting in finite two-dimensional ion crystals confined in anisotropic traps. Building on our previous studies of melting probability as a function of temperature and anisotropy, we extend the analysis to crystal sizes ranging from $N=4$ to $N=101$ ions and examine how structural isomerization modifies the melting pathways. Using molecular dynamics simulations together with Metropolis-Hastings equilibrium sampling, we analyze radial fluctuations, angular disorder, Lindemann parameters, and isomer-dependent energy landscapes in these Coulomb crystals. We find that crystals with identical particle number and trap anisotropy can exhibit substantially different melting behavior depending on the metastable structural isomer they occupy. Contrary to a simple soft-mode instability picture, the observed melting process is characterized by a gradual thermally-driven accumulation of radial and angular disorder. Structural isomerization reshapes the fluctuation channels through which delocalization develops, leading to nonuniform melting probabilities across temperature and anisotropy space. These results provide a unified microscopic picture of melting in finite ion crystals and clarify the role of structural rearrangements in finite-size phase transitions.

Summary. This theoretical paper proposes using quantum interferometry with two massive particles to detect quantum gravitational effects via entanglement. The authors demonstrate that the geometric design of the interferometer, particularly incorporating vertical path contributions, is crucial for maximizing the measurable entanglement. By optimizing the geometry, they significantly improve the prospects for experimentally testing quantum gravity.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, testable quantum optics proposal to probe quantum gravity effects, linking fundamental constants and geometry to measurable quantum correlations, which is highly relevant for quantum sensing and open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of finding empirical evidence for quantum gravity by analyzing gravitationally induced quantum entanglement.

Main result. Geometric optimization of the interferometer setup, specifically tuning the vertical path length ratio (b/l), can substantially enhance the measurable quantum entanglement generated by gravity.

Method. The analysis uses a two-qubit framework, calculating entanglement using Concurrence and testing experimental feasibility via the violation of the CHSH Bell inequality.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the physical motivation (quantum gravity) to developing the theoretical framework using exact solutions for the phase shift, quantifying entanglement via Concurrence and Bell inequalities, and finally deriving optimal geometric parameters.

Abstract

The quest for a quantum theory of gravity remains a central challenge in modern physics, driven by the goal of unifying general relativity and quantum mechanics. Direct experimental access to quantum gravitational effects is notoriously difficult due to the inaccessibility of the Planck scale. Recent proposals, however, suggest that gravitationally induced quantum entanglement between two massive particles, realized through advanced quantum interferometric techniques, could provide empirical evidence for the quantum nature of gravity. We demonstrate that the geometrical configuration of an interferometer plays a decisive role in entanglement generation. Our analysis reveals that geometric optimization can substantially enhance the gravitationally induced phase shift, thereby improving the prospects for experimental detection of gravity-mediated entanglement. Moreover, we show that standard approximations employed in prior proposals may severely limit predictive accuracy and compromise experimental feasibility.

Structural Conditions for Distributed Quantum Advantage

Authors: Sabina Drăgoi, María Gragera Garcés, Lirandë Pira
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04393v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 3/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper establishes rigorous structural criteria—affordable classical knitting, preserved computational hardness, and stable gradients—required for distributed quantum advantage. By analyzing these criteria and testing them on toric-code patches on real hardware, the authors identify finite local-depth circuits as a viable path forward for modular quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly addresses the practical limitations of scaling quantum computation by providing concrete, testable structural requirements for achieving advantage across modular hardware architectures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the structural conditions necessary to achieve a 'distributed quantum advantage' when running quantum computations across multiple, physically separated processors.

Main result. The authors identify Finite Local-Depth Circuits (FLDCs) with bounded interfaces as a promising architecture where the three necessary conditions—affordable knitting, classical hardness, and resolvable gradients—can coexist.

Method. The methodology involves formulating three necessary screening criteria (Cut, Sim, Train) and applying them to various circuit families, supplemented by hardware experiments on toric-code patches.

Model / system. The model is quantum computation distributed across multiple quantum chips, utilizing 'circuit cutting' to estimate expectation values from smaller subcircuits. The physical realization is tested on an IBM Nighthawk processor using field-perturbed toric-code patches.

Key observables. Knitting cost ($\Gamma_{cut}$), gradient variance ($ ext{Var}[\partial_\mu C]$), and contact correlation across the cut interface.

Important parameters / regimes. Bounded interface size, local depth ($\chi$), and the circuit size ($n$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the required hardness criterion ($S_1$) holds beyond the tested sizes, and the hardware results are limited by the one-parameter approximation used for signal correction.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate circuit cutting processes, the decay of gradient variance with causal distance, and the comparison of raw vs. rescaled device energies showing signal retention.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the three necessary criteria for distributed advantage (Cut, Sim, Train). It then analyzes these criteria across circuit families, proposing FLDCs as a solution. Finally, it validates this framework through both theoretical analysis of gradient decay and experimental testing on superconducting qubits.

Abstract

Circuit cutting runs a quantum computation on processors smaller than the circuit, at the price of classical knitting whose cost grows exponentially with the number of cut gates. Keeping this cost affordable is necessary but not sufficient for an advantage, since knitting classically easy subcircuits is itself classically easy. We formulate three requirements for distributed quantum advantage under a stated budget: affordable knitting, classical difficulty that survives the cut, and, for variational circuits, resolvable gradients. We prove that under stated assumptions knitting stays affordable when subcircuits grow behind a bounded interface and becomes exponentially expensive when fixed-width subcircuits multiply. We turn the requirements into screening criteria and apply them to eighteen circuit families. We identify finite local-depth circuits with bounded interfaces as a setting in which the requirements can coexist, with hardness established only in the worst case. As a classically verifiable proof of principle, we knit one gate joining two field-perturbed toric-code patches on an IBM Nighthawk processor for up to 142 spins, and confirm that the reconstruction can at least resolve the contact correlation beyond independent execution for up to 98 qubits.

Superconducting landscape of the strongly correlated bilayer Hubbard model

Summary. This work maps the superconducting phase diagram of the bilayer Hubbard model, showing that optimal superconductivity occurs at intermediate interlayer coupling and doping. The authors demonstrate that strong correlations suppress $T_c$ near half-filling by reducing low-energy spectral weight, suggesting that modifying the Fermi surface geometry via longer-range hopping can enhance pairing.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed study of how correlation-induced spectral weight suppression limits $T_c$ provides a rich theoretical framework relevant to understanding pairing mechanisms in high-temperature superconductors, which is a core area of condensed matter theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how repulsive interactions drive superconductivity in a coupled, strongly correlated bilayer Hubbard model, and how external parameters control this superconducting state.

Main result. Superconductivity is optimized at intermediate interlayer hopping and moderate doping, exhibiting an $s_{\pm}$-wave gap; however, strong self-energy effects suppress $T_c$ near half-filling.

Method. The research employs advanced theoretical techniques, primarily the Dynamical Vertex Approximation (D$\Gamma$A) and comparison with the Fluctuation Exchange (FLEX) approximation, to solve the linearized gap equation.

Model / system. The system is the strongly correlated bilayer Hubbard model on a square lattice, described by a Hamiltonian including onsite repulsion ($U$) and interlayer hopping ($t_\perp$). The physics is analyzed by treating layer degrees of freedom via pseudo momentum $k_z$.

Key observables. Superconducting transition temperature ($T_c$), superconducting eigenvalue ($\lambda_{SC}$), low-energy spectral weight, and the real/imaginary parts of the self-energy ($\Sigma$).

Important parameters / regimes. Filling ($n$), interlayer hopping ($t_\perp$), and interaction strength ($U/t$). Optimal regimes are found at intermediate $t_\perp$ and moderate doping.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on approximations like D$\Gamma$A and FLEX, which are used to capture strong correlations and nonlocal fluctuations. A key assumption is that the superconducting pairing is governed by the interplay between these correlation effects.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the superconducting phase diagram ($T_c$ vs. filling), momentum-space maps showing spectral weight suppression near half-filling, and comparisons of $\lambda_{SC}$ across different theoretical approximations and temperature regimes.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the superconducting landscape by first establishing the optimal doping/hopping regime, then detailing the mechanism of $T_c$ suppression near half-filling due to self-energy effects, and finally proposing that longer-range hoppings can mitigate this suppression.

Abstract

Coupling two strongly correlated layers opens a distinct route to repulsion-driven superconductivity. Yet the same interlayer coupling makes the optimization of superconductivity highly nontrivial. Using the dynamical vertex approximation, we determine the superconducting landscape in the enlarged parameter space spanned by filling and interlayer hopping. We identify an optimal regime at intermediate interlayer hopping and moderate doping away from half filling, where the system exhibits high superconducting transition temperatures ($T_{\rm c}$) with an interband $s_{\pm}$-wave gap structure. Toward half filling, however, strong self-energy effects can suppress the superconducting tendency by reducing the low-energy spectral weight. We further show that a Fermi-surface distortion induced by longer-range hoppings can weaken this suppression and enhance $T_{\rm c}$. Our results establish how filling, interlayer hopping, and Fermi-surface geometry jointly optimize the superconducting regime in the strongly correlated bilayer Hubbard model.

Symmetry resolved entanglement in Schwinger pair production

Authors: Wajid Joyia, S. M. Al-Marzoug, Amaria Javed
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04513v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 3/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper investigates how entanglement is structured during Schwinger pair production in a constant electric field by resolving entanglement according to the conserved electric charge. Using symmetry-resolved entropy and negativity, the authors show that the field primarily redistributes existing entanglement across charge sectors rather than creating it uniformly. This technique provides detailed information about the location and movement of quantum correlations in charge space.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a sophisticated tool (symmetry resolution) to dissect entanglement in non-perturbative quantum field processes, offering insights into how fundamental symmetries constrain quantum correlations in extreme physical environments.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study focuses on characterizing the charge-resolved structure of pairwise mode entanglement generated by the Schwinger effect in quantum electrodynamics.

Main result. Symmetry resolution reveals that the electric field primarily redistributes entanglement across different charge sectors rather than creating it globally, and the vacuum pair negativity is shown to transport across charge imbalance sectors.

Method. The authors employ symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy ($S_Q$) for pure states and charge imbalance resolved negativity ($N(q)$) for mixed states, utilizing the $U(1)$ charge conservation symmetry.

Model / system. The system is Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) subjected to a constant, uniform electric field ($E_0$). The analysis models pair production from the vacuum, considering reduced density matrices for various mode pairings (particle-particle, antiparticle-antiparticle, particle-antiparticle).

Key observables. Symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy ($S_Q$), charge imbalance resolved negativity ($N(q)$), and sector probabilities ($p_Q$).

Important parameters / regimes. The pair production probability $|eta|^2 = e^{-\pi\mu}$, where $\mu$ depends on the field strength $E_0$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is restricted to the case of a constant electric field; the results are derived using the $U(1)$ charge conservation symmetry.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the total symmetry-resolved entanglement entropy and sector probabilities for different pair types (e.g., vacuum pair $ ho_{k,-k}$), showing field dependence peaking at critical values.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes entanglement measures for different reduced states ($ ho_{p,q}$, $ ho_{-p,-q}$, $ ho_{p,-q}$, $ ho_{k,-k}$), comparing entropy and negativity, and demonstrating how charge resolution refines the understanding of field-induced entanglement dynamics.

Abstract

We study the charge resolved structure of the pairwise mode entanglement produced by the Schwinger effect. Using the $U(1)$ electric charge of quantum electrodynamics, we decompose the entanglement of the reduced density matrices of momentum modes into individual charge sectors, applying the symmetry resolved entanglement entropy to the pure bipartition reductions and the charge imbalance resolved negativity to the mixed particle-antiparticle pair. For a constant electric field we obtain closed form results for every mode pairing, with all field dependence controlled by the single pair production probability $|β|^2=e^{-πμ}$. For the particle, particle and antiparticle antiparticle pairs the entanglement entropy within each charge sector is field independent, so that the field acts only by redistributing statistical weight among the sectors; for the mixed particle antiparticle pair the populated sector additionally carries a field dependent entropy. The vacuum created pair is the richest case: its resolved negativity is distributed over three imbalance sectors and is transported from one sector to the next as the field increases, the sectors contributing equally only at the critical field. The entanglement across a fixed momentum bipartition, by contrast, is frozen at its initial value because pair creation conserves the sector charge. Symmetry resolution thus refines the total entanglement measures into a sector resolved picture, exposing where in charge space the entanglement of the Schwinger mechanism resides and how the electric field moves it, information beyond that contained in the total measures or in particle number distributions.

A Cheat-Sensitive Primitive for Destination-Private Quantum Routing

Authors: S. F. E. Oliviero, S. Onofri, V. Giovannetti
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04359v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a minimal two-call quantum routing primitive designed to test for eavesdropping on the destination address in quantum networks. By combining a direct request with a coherent 'trap' call, the protocol achieves a cheat-sensitive test that detects reading attacks. The work establishes strong privacy guarantees in the perfect-pass limit, advancing the theory of secure quantum communication.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on quantum networking primitives, the rigorous analysis of information leakage and privacy guarantees in a controlled quantum channel setting provides foundational theoretical tools applicable to understanding information flow in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a minimal quantum routing primitive that allows a user to establish an entangled link while simultaneously testing whether an intermediate provider has read the destination address.

Main result. The protocol is shown to be 'cheat-sensitive,' detecting explicit destination-reading attacks with non-zero probability, and achieving destination privacy in the 'perfect-pass limit.'

Method. The methodology adapts the hidden-order mechanism from Quantum Private Queries, utilizing a two-call structure involving a direct request and a coherent superposition 'trap' call.

Model / system. The model is a star-network quantum routing setup involving a user (Alice), a provider (Bob), and multiple clients. It relies on shared entangled singlets and private matching qubits held by the clients.

Key observables. Detection probability of an attack, Bob's residual state, and the state's dependence on the destination address $j$.

Important parameters / regimes. The protocol is limited to a two-call primitive, and the analysis characterizes the 'perfect-pass limit' where the residual state is independent of the destination.

Assumptions / limitations. The claim is deliberately limited; it does not provide a quantitative information-disturbance tradeoff or composable security. The perfect-pass limit assumes ideal initial singlets and exact direct functionality.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the star-network implementation, detailing the roles of Alice, Bob, the clients, the singlets, and the private matching qubit $Q(j)$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by defining the two-call protocol, analyzing the state evolution after favorable Bell outcomes, deriving the detection probability for attacks, and finally proving the strong privacy guarantee by showing the residual state is destination-independent in the perfect-pass limit.

Abstract

Quantum networks will rely on intermediate nodes to distribute entanglement, but the same nodes may learn which users are being connected. We introduce a minimal two-call routing primitive in which a user, Alice, establishes an entangled link with one of several clients while testing whether the provider, Bob, has disturbed a coherently encoded request. The construction adapts the hidden-order mechanism of quantum private queries: one call contains a direct request, the other a coherent superposition of the desired and null addresses, and Alice keeps their order secret. A private matching qubit held by the selected client restores the coherent test state after a favorable Bell-measurement outcome, so that Alice can test the returned query without any further operation by Bob. The claim is deliberately limited. The protocol shows that a routing request can carry a physical cheating test, and it detects an explicit destination-reading attack with nonzero probability. It does not yet provide a quantitative information-disturbance tradeoff, composable security, or certification of an unmeasured output against arbitrary attacks. We also characterize the exact perfect-pass limit, in which Bob's residual state, averaged over the private matching states, is independent of the destination.

Approximate cloning of structured pure states

Authors: Yaroslav Herasymenko, Junqiao Lin, Philip Verduyn Lunel, Dmitry Grinko
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06723v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work tackles the quantum state cloning problem by analyzing the minimum number of copies needed when the state is restricted to structured families like Gaussian states. It develops a unifying mathematical framework to show that for these structured systems, the required resources scale polynomially with system size, offering significant theoretical improvements over general quantum state cloning bounds.

Why it may be interesting. The results provide concrete, polynomial scaling laws for cloning structured quantum states, which is crucial for understanding the resource requirements in quantum simulation and quantum information processing involving structured many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the sample complexity required for approximate quantum state cloning when the input state is restricted to a structured family, contrasting this with the exponentially large complexity for general states.

Main result. For structured families like fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states, the required sample complexity scales polynomially (or linearly) with the number of modes ($n$), showing significant improvements over unstructured cloning bounds.

Method. The authors construct optimal cloning channels using a single representation-theoretic framework and employ controlled saddle-point evaluations of the frame potential to derive scaling laws.

Model / system. The analysis covers various structured pure states fundamental to many-body physics, including $n$-mode fermionic Gaussian/Slater states and $n$-mode bosonic Gaussian states.

Key observables. Cloning fidelity (or trace-distance error $\epsilon$) and the minimum sample complexity $N_{\min,S}(\epsilon)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Number of modes ($n$), error tolerance ($\epsilon$), and the dimension of the state manifold ($\dim S$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the input state belonging to a specific, structured family (e.g., Gaussian states) and uses approximations like the saddle-point method for high $N$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general problem of sample complexity for cloning, then developing a unified representation-theoretic framework, and finally applying this framework to derive specific, improved scaling laws for fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states.

Abstract

The no-cloning theorem is a cornerstone result in quantum mechanics that forbids copying of general quantum information. More quantitatively, even given $N$ copies of an unknown state, any channel producing an $(N+1)$-copy state incurs a nonzero trace-distance error on some inputs. Turning this around, one can ask about the sample complexity of approximate $N\rightarrow N+1$ cloning: for a desired small error $ε$, what $N$ suffices? For arbitrary pure states on a Hilbert space $H$, the answer is $N\simeq \dim H/ε$ - astronomically large for most $H$ of interest. What if the input is promised to lie in a structured family? We examine a range of families fundamental to many-body physics and quantum information: $n$-mode fermionic Gaussian and Slater states, $n$-mode bosonic Gaussian states, and qudit phase states. For fermions, we construct optimal cloning channels, reducing the complexity from exponential to polynomial in $n$. For bosonic Gaussian states, we construct an explicit cloner that certifies polynomial complexity, without any constraint on the energy of the state; this channel, however, is not optimal in general. The fermionic and bosonic results follow from a single representation-theoretic framework, which generalizes previous work by Werner and by Chiribella and Yang; it also yields the cloners' unitary implementation. Furthermore, we explain why for many families the cloning complexity is linear in the dimension of the family manifold, via a controlled saddle-point evaluation of the frame potential at high $N$. The same calculation accounts for the analogous scaling of tomography complexity. The above framework does not cover phase states; for these we give an independent construction of a channel with optimal cloning fidelity. Our work complements recent results on cloning stabilizer states.

Certifying Measurement Incompatibility under Bounded Classical Communication

Authors: Oxana Shaya
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06722v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work develops a powerful tool, 'union maximal leakage,' to certify measurement incompatibility in quantum communication settings, even when classical communication is involved. By comparing this leakage to classical communication bounds, the authors establish necessary and sufficient conditions for quantum advantages like entanglement and private randomness. It provides a rigorous information-theoretic framework for testing the non-classical nature of quantum correlations.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on information theory, the rigorous quantification of incompatibility and private randomness extraction using information-theoretic tools is foundational for understanding the limits of quantum communication protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses how to certify measurement incompatibility in quantum protocols when the receiver's outcomes are influenced by bounded classical communication from a sender.

Main result. A violation of the union maximal leakage bound implies measurement incompatibility, which in turn certifies quantum advantages like entanglement or private randomness extraction.

Method. The authors introduce and utilize the 'union maximal leakage' ($\Lambda_{\cup}$), calculated via a linear program, to quantify the minimum classical information needed to explain observed quantum probabilities.

Model / system. The model involves a sender, a shared quantum resource, a classical message, and a receiver performing measurements. The analysis is general, focusing on the statistical correlations $p(b|x,y)$ rather than specific Hamiltonians.

Key observables. Union maximal leakage ($\Lambda_{\cup}(\mathbf{p})$), conditional entropy $H(B | \dots)$, and the total-variation distance ($\varepsilon$).

Important parameters / regimes. The bound on classical communication ($k$ bits) and the structure of the shared resource (e.g., nonsignalling vs. quantum).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on bounding the leakage based on the assumption of classical simulation or the availability of a shared nonsignalling resource.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the general communication setup: sender ($x$) $ o$ shared resource $ o$ message $m o$ receiver ($y, b$).

Paper structure. The paper develops by first defining the leakage measure ($\Lambda_{\cup}$), establishing bounds relating compatibility to this leakage, and then extending these bounds to certify nonlocality and private randomness extraction in sequential protocols.

Abstract

Measurement incompatibility enables quantum advantages in communication and state discrimination. It is also necessary for Bell nonlocality, which is used in device-independent protocols for quantum key distribution and private randomness generation. Classical communication can influence the receiver's outcome and must therefore be accounted for when certifying measurement incompatibility. We introduce union maximal leakage, which quantifies the information needed for a common classical explanation of all observed probabilities and is computed from them by a linear program. If a message of at most $k$ bits precedes the receiver's independent measurement choice, compatible measurements for each message give union maximal leakage at most $k$. This bound holds with any input-independent nonsignalling shared resource. In quantum theory, a violation also implies that the initially shared state is entangled. Conversely, union maximal leakage determines the minimum classical communication needed to reproduce the observations with compatible measurements and arbitrary nonsignalling assistance. For a fixed shared quantum state and receiver measurements, a violation is possible exactly when, for some message value, the receiver's measurements are Bell-nonlocal against two-outcome sender measurements on that state. For binary receiver outcomes, we show that a violation also certifies randomness private from the sender, even when the receiver's measurement depends on the sender's message and the sender retains quantum side information. We quantify this privacy through an explicit conditional entropy bound and establish composably secure randomness extraction in sequential protocols with device memory.

Summary. This paper develops a theoretical framework to study quasi-solitons in the 1D Bose gas at any interaction strength using the Lieb-Liniger model. By selectively superposing multi-hole eigenstates, the authors show how to create robust, localized excitations whose dynamics are governed by unitary quantum mechanics. This advances understanding of non-perturbative quantum behavior in strongly correlated quantum gases.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous quantum mechanical treatment of classical-like excitations (solitons) in a strongly interacting quantum gas, extending beyond the standard Gross-Pitaevskii mean-field approximation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study focuses on constructing and analyzing long-lived, spatially localized, soliton-like excitations (quasi-solitons) within the one-dimensional Bose gas.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that by using a selective superposition of multi-hole eigenstates, they can generate such quasi-solitons with long lifetimes, even in the strongly interacting regime where perturbative methods fail.

Method. The methodology involves constructing time-evolving wavefunctions as superpositions of Bethe Ansatz eigenstates and calculating the time evolution of the density profile using specific matrix elements.

Model / system. The system is the 1D Bose gas interacting via a contact repulsion, modeled by the Lieb-Liniger Hamiltonian. The analysis utilizes the exact solvability provided by the Bethe Ansatz.

Key observables. Density profile $\rho(x, t)$, and lifetime quantified by time power series $\Delta\rho(t)$ and depletion integral $R(t)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Arbitrary contact repulsion strength ($c$), number of holes ($n$), and system size/density ($N/L$).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is the applicability of the Bethe Ansatz framework to build the required quantum states, allowing the study beyond the semi-classical or weak-coupling limits.

Figures summary. Figures compare analytical and numerical results for density matrix elements and the 1-hole density profile in the Tonks-Girardeau (TG) regime, confirming theoretical predictions.

Paper structure. The paper builds from the general Hamiltonian formulation to the construction of wavepackets using multi-hole states. It then details the calculation of density matrix elements and concludes by verifying the expected fixed-point density in the TG limit.

Abstract

Harnessing integrability at arbitrary interaction strength, we build long-lived and spatially localized soliton-like excitations in the 1D Bose gas with contact repulsion (Lieb-Liniger model). We show how these density profiles, built from selective superpositions of multi-hole eigenstates, display long lifetimes tunable through parameters like dispersion width and number of holes. We further illustrate a scaling protocol that in principle enables unbounded increase in lifetimes in the thermodynamic limit. Our results showcase classical-like excitations originating from unitary quantum dynamics, away from the semi-classical regime, and in regions where interactions cannot be treated perturbatively.

Strong metamagnetic switching and anisotropic magnetotransport in the distorted kagome magnet Tb6Ti4Al43

Authors: Lipika, Sneh, Shobha Singh, Ralf Koban, Walter Schnelle, Rie Umetsu, Kaustuv Manna
Type: experiment · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06732v1

Summary. This work comprehensively characterizes the magnetic, electrical, and thermal properties of the distorted kagome magnet Tb6Ti4Al43. Key findings include observing field-induced metamagnetic switching and quantifying a large intrinsic contribution to the anomalous Hall effect via Berry curvature. The results establish the material as a promising platform for multifunctional spintronic applications.

Why it may be interesting. The observation of large intrinsic Berry curvature contributions to transport properties in a magnetically ordered, geometrically frustrated system like a kagome magnet is highly relevant to topological condensed matter physics, which often overlaps with AMO/quantum simulation concepts.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the complex interplay between spin, charge, and lattice degrees of freedom in a distorted kagome magnet to understand its field-tunable multifunctional behavior.

Main result. The material exhibits strong magnetic anisotropy, undergoing metamagnetic switching along the c-axis and showing evidence of intrinsic Berry curvature effects contributing significantly to the anomalous Hall conductivity.

Method. The research employed a combination of magnetic measurements (magnetization, specific heat), electrical transport measurements (resistivity, anomalous Hall effect), and structural characterization (XRD) on single crystals.

Model / system. The physical system is single-crystalline Tb6Ti4Al43, which features Tb atoms forming a distorted kagome lattice embedded within a Ti-Al framework, leading to complex magnetic interactions.

Key observables. Metamagnetic switching, spin-flop transition, anomalous magnetoresistance, anomalous Hall conductivity ($\sigma_{yx}$), and magnetic ordering temperature ($T_N \sim 19 ext{ K}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Low temperatures (2 K), magnetic fields applied along different crystal axes ($H \parallel c$ vs $H \parallel ab$), and the Curie-Weiss temperature ($ heta \sim 29 ext{ K}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The anomalous Hall effect is attributed to intrinsic Berry curvature effects arising from the system's momentum-space topology, despite the presence of extrinsic scattering mechanisms.

Figures summary. Figures detail atomic structure and crystal orientation (Fig. 1); magnetization vs. T and H (Figs. 2, 5); specific heat measurements (Fig. 3); and the separation of anomalous Hall contributions into skew-scattering and intrinsic quadratic terms (Fig. 8).

Paper structure. The paper follows a structure of characterization (structure, magnetism, thermodynamics) followed by detailed transport measurements, culminating in the separation and analysis of the anomalous Hall effect contributions.

Abstract

We report a comprehensive investigation of the magnetic, electrical, and thermodynamic properties of single crystalline Tb6Ti4Al43, in which Tb atoms form a distorted kagome lattice embedded within a complex Ti-Al framework. Tb6Ti4Al43 exhibits pronounced magnetic anisotropy and antiferromagnetic ordering at approximately 19 K. For magnetic field applied along the c-axis, a strong metamagnetic switching is observed, accompanied by a clear asymmetric hysteresis at low temperatures, while for field applied along the ab plane, a spin-flop transition is observed. The effect of field induced magnetic states is strongly reflected in magnetotransport measurements, giving rise to pronounced anomalies in magnetoresistance and an anomalous Hall contribution. A significant intrinsic contribution to the anomalous Hall conductivity is observed, highlighting the Berry curvature effects arising from the system's momentum-space topology. Our results highlight the interplay of spin, charge, and lattice degrees of freedom in Tb6Ti4Al43 and establish this distorted kagome antiferromagnet as a promising platform for field-tunable multifunctional behaviour, including electrically readable magnetic switching, spin manipulation, and magneto-mechanical functionalities.
