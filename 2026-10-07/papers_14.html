Origin of the inhomogeneous linewidth in a solid state $^{229}$Th:CaF$_2$ nuclear clock

Authors: Kai Li, Tian Ooi, Jack F. Doyle, Konstantin Herb, Emil Pellett, Martin Pimon, Thorsten Schumm, Steven M. Girvin, Leo Radzihovsky, Jun Ye
Type: both · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04205v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper characterizes the source of inhomogeneous broadening in solid-state $^{229} ext{Th}$ nuclear clocks embedded in $ ext{CaF}_2$. By measuring linewidths across varying conditions, the authors determine that the broadening is dominated by static, random variations in the local Electric-Field Gradient (EFG). The study provides both experimental evidence and DFT simulations confirming that this EFG disorder scales linearly with dopant concentration, offering crucial guidance for material engineering.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it tackles the fundamental limitations of solid-state quantum sensors by pinpointing the microscopic source of decoherence (EFG disorder) and providing a quantitative model for its engineering mitigation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the origin of the large inhomogeneous linewidth observed in solid-state $^{229} ext{Th}: ext{CaF}_2$ nuclear clocks, which significantly exceeds the natural linewidth.

Main result. The dominant source of broadening is identified as variations in the local Electric-Field Gradient (EFG), which increases linearly with the thorium dopant concentration.

Method. The research combines systematic experimental measurements of linewidths versus temperature and concentration with theoretical modeling using first-principle DFT calculations.

Model / system. The system is the $^{229} ext{Th}$ nuclear transition in a $ ext{CaF}_2$ crystal host, governed by interactions involving the isomeric shift and the local EFG. The broadening is modeled using the statistical distribution of EFG variations.

Key observables. Linewidth ($\Gamma$) as a function of temperature, thorium concentration, and quadrupole state; frequency shifts related to EFG components ($V_{zz}, V_{yy}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Linear dependence of linewidth on Th concentration; $1/r^3$ scaling for long-range dopant perturbations; EFG ratios ($\Gamma_{yy}/\Gamma_{zz} \sim 1.6$).

Assumptions / limitations. The dominant broadening mechanism is attributed to static, long-range, dopant-induced variations in the local EFG, which are assumed to follow a specific statistical distribution.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dependence of linewidths on various parameters and show schematic representations of how the surrounding charge distribution influences both the isomeric shift and the local EFG.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from observing the linewidth discrepancy to systematically measuring its dependence on external parameters (T, concentration), identifying EFG disorder as the cause, and finally providing theoretical explanations and computational verification using DFT.

Abstract

Narrow linewidth (high-Q) quantum discriminators are essential for realizing solid-state $^{229}$Th nuclear clocks, yet the observed transition linewidths remain at the tens-to-hundreds-of-kilohertz level, far exceeding the sub-Hertz linewidth set by the radiative lifetime of the nuclear isomer. Here, we investigate the origin of this broadening in $^{229}$Th:CaF$_2$ through systematic measurements of the linewidth as functions of temperature, thorium concentration, and quadrupole state, accompanied by theoretical analysis and first-principle simulations. We find that the linewidth is independent of temperature, increases linearly with thorium concentration, and depends strongly on the quadrupole state. Data analysis identifies variations of the local electric-field gradient (EFG) as the dominant source of inhomogeneous broadening and reveals that the EFG disorder increases linearly with thorium concentration. The observed Lorentzian line shapes and linewidth linear concentration dependence are explained naturally from the $1/r^3$ scaling of a random distribution of long-range dopant-induced perturbations. Complementary atomistic and \textit{ab initio} density-functional-theory calculations reproduce these characteristic features and provide insights into the microscopic defect configurations responsible for the broadening. These results establish the dominant broadening mechanism in solid-state $^{229}$Th and provide guidance for engineering nuclear-clock materials with reduced inhomogeneous broadening.

Pairwise detection, chiral conversion: Resource theory of quantum multistates

Authors: Yuan-Hong Tao, Yu-Xin Wu, Bing Xie, Lin Zhang
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04930v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops a resource-conversion theory for quantum multistates, showing that while pairwise overlaps (second-order invariants) are sufficient for detection, exact transformation requires higher-order invariants (third-order invariants) due to chirality. The analysis rigorously defines the necessary and sufficient conditions for such conversions, revealing structural rigidity in higher dimensions.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep mathematical constraints on how quantum correlations (coherence) can be transformed, linking fundamental algebraic invariants (Bargmann invariants) directly to operational feasibility (complete positivity) in resource theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the exact resource-conversion theory for quantum multistates under common commutativity-preserving operations (CPOs). It investigates whether pairwise state overlaps are sufficient to govern the deterministic transformation between coherent ensembles.

Main result. For qubits, detectability relies on second-order invariants, but exact conversion requires third-order invariants, establishing an order separation ($k_{ ext{detect}}=2 < k_{ ext{convert}}=3$). Furthermore, the structure is rigid for higher dimensions ($d \ge 3$).

Method. The authors use resource theory concepts, analyzing the complete positivity (CP) of the candidate transformation channel ($\Phi_T$) derived from pairwise overlaps. They employ Bargmann invariants ($\Delta_k$) to characterize the state geometry and the transformation's orientation (chirality).

Model / system. The system under study is a quantum multistate $ ho = ( ho_1, \dots, ho_n)$ in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, with a specific focus on qubits ($d=2$). The transformations are constrained to Common Commutativity-Preserving Operations (CPOs).

Key observables. Pairwise overlaps (second-order Bargmann invariants, $\Delta_2$), third-order Bargmann invariants ($\Delta_3$), singular values of the transformation matrix $T$, and the chirality sign $\eta$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensionality $d$ (especially $d=2$ vs $d \ge 3$), the number of states $n$, and the degree of the invariant ($k=2$ vs $k=3$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of a candidate channel $\Phi_T$ fixed by the ratio of Bloch matrices ($T = S R^{-1}$) and relies on the definition of CPOs.

Figures summary. Figure 1 contrasts detection (using $\Delta_2$) versus conversion (requiring $\Delta_3$). Figure 2 illustrates the singular-value simplex, partitioning it into regions (I, II, $\mathcal{C}_{ ext{chir}}$) based on CP conditions.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the resource-conversion theory by first analyzing the qubit case ($d=2$), demonstrating the necessity of third-order invariants due to chirality. It then generalizes to higher dimensions ($d \ge 3$), showing a rigid structure governed by lower-degree invariants.

Abstract

Basis-independent coherence of a quantum multistate is fixed by whether its constituent states commute, and for qubits it is detected by pairwise state overlaps alone. What governs its deterministic transformation has been open. We give the exact resource-conversion theory of quantum multistates under common commutativity-preserving operations (CPOs). For qubits, pairwise Bargmann invariants determine the singular values of the unique candidate channel, while a single third-order Bargmann phase determines its orientation. Complete positivity then yields a necessary and sufficient criterion for exact conversion. The criterion identifies a chirality-sensitive shell $\mathcal C_{\rm chir}$, within which targets with identical pairwise geometry can differ in convertibility solely by the sign of their cubic invariant. This yields an order separation, $k_{\rm detect}=2<3=k_{\rm convert}$, for qubit multistates of every size $n\geqslant3$. The chirality-sensitive shell occupies exactly one third of the singular-value simplex, while mirror reflection obstructs two thirds of the admissible orientation-preserving transformations by volume. For $d\geqslant3$, by contrast, the structure is rigid: every resourceful conversion is realized by either an isotropic depolarizing or a transpose-depolarizing channel. Convertibility is then decided by centered Bargmann invariants of degree at most $d^2$.

Performance Analysis of Multiparty GHZ-State Quantum Conference Key Agreement over Optical Fiber Channel

Authors: Dipanjan Bera, Ramniwas Meena, Neel Kanth Kundu
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.05448v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work rigorously analyzes the performance of multipartite quantum key distribution using GHZ states over noisy optical fibers. By modeling realistic channel impairments like loss and dark counts, the authors derive error rates and calculate the finite-key secret key rate. The results quantify the unavoidable degradation of secure key rates as the network size or distance increases.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a detailed, physically grounded analysis of key distribution security limits, which is crucial for designing practical quantum communication networks.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the performance limits of Quantum Conference Key Agreement (QCKA) using GHZ states when transmitted over realistic optical fiber channels.

Main result. The achievable secret key rate (SKR) decreases with both increased fiber distance and the number of participating parties, although optimizing parameter estimation can extend the secure range.

Method. The authors derive closed-form expressions for error rates by modeling the channel as an erasure-Pauli channel with dark counts, and then integrate these into a finite-key SKR framework optimized via Brent's method.

Model / system. The system is an N-party QCKA protocol utilizing N-qubit GHZ states distributed over optical fibers. The channel incorporates fiber loss, polarization decoherence, and detector dark counts.

Key observables. Local bit-error rate ($Q_{AB_i}$), global phase-error rate ($Q_X$), and the finite-key Secret Key Rate (SKR).

Important parameters / regimes. Fiber transmissivity ($\eta$), dark-count probability ($p_{dc}$), number of parties ($N$), and fiber distance ($d$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the dephasing-dominated regime for the fiber channel and models the channel as an extension of the bipartite erasure-Pauli channel.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the system model, plot error rates ($Q_{ABi}$ and $Q_X$) versus distance for varying $N$, and compare the finite-key SKR against the asymptotic limit for different $N$.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical model, derives error rate formulas based on the channel impairments, integrates these into the finite-key SKR framework, and concludes with numerical results showing the scaling of SKR with distance and $N$.

Abstract

Quantum conference key agreement (QCKA) enables multiple spatially separated users to establish a common key with composable information-theoretic security by exploiting multipartite quantum correlations. Greenberger--Horne--Zeilinger (GHZ) states are a natural resource for this task, but their distribution over optical fiber is degraded by transmission loss, polarization decoherence, and detector imperfections. We investigate the finite-key performance of an $N$-party GHZ-based QCKA system using the $N$-BB84 protocol, where each optical link is modeled by extending the bipartite erasure--Pauli channel to incorporate detector dark counts. We characterize the impact of this quantum channel on the shared GHZ state and derive closed-form expressions for the error rates. The analytical results reveal that the local bit-error rate depends only on transmissivity and dark counts, while the global phase-error rate compounds over all $N-1$ links and therefore degrades faster with both distance and the number of parties. These error rates are integrated into a finite-key secret-key-rate (SKR) framework, in which we optimize the parameter-estimation probability via Brent's derivative-free method. Numerical results show that increasing fiber distance and network size reduce the achievable SKR. Furthermore, the optimized parameter-estimation probability consistently increases the SKR and extends the maximum secure distance relative to a fixed-allocation baseline, with comparison to the asymptotic limit quantifying the residual finite-key penalty.

Summary. This paper constructs a discrete analogue of geometric quantization applicable to finite fields. It shows that quantum error-correcting codes can be understood as the quantization of classical constraints defined on a symplectic vector space. This establishes a rigorous, physical interpretation for the mathematical relationship between classical coding theory and quantum stabilizer codes.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a deep mathematical link between abstract algebraic structures (symplectic geometry over finite fields) and the physical realization of quantum error correction codes, which is foundational to quantum computation theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a discrete analogue of geometric quantization over finite fields, providing a physical interpretation for the correspondence between classical and quantum error-correcting codes.

Main result. The quantization procedure successfully maps classical constraints (defined by isotropic subspaces in a symplectic vector space over $\mathbb{F}_d$) to quantum stabilizer constraints, demonstrating that quantum codes are fundamentally quantized codes.

Method. The authors adapt geometric quantization techniques, originally for continuous symplectic manifolds, to the discrete setting of finite fields using displacement operators and polarization analogues.

Model / system. The model involves symplectic vector spaces $(V, \omega)$ over finite fields $\mathbb{F}_d$, relating classical linear codes (defined by constraints) to quantum stabilizer codes on $d$-dimensional qudits.

Key observables. Stabilizer constraints, logical Pauli operators, and the resulting code space dimension $[[n, k]]_d$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension $d$ of the qudit (often an odd prime), and the structure of the symplectic form $\omega$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on establishing a discrete analogue of the Segal-Bargmann representation and assuming the compatibility of quantization with symplectic reduction.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first reviewing continuous geometric quantization, then developing the discrete analogue over finite fields, and finally applying this framework to explicitly derive the structure of stabilizer codes from classical coding theory constraints.

Abstract

We present a discrete analogue of geometric quantization over finite fields which, when applied to certain classical error-correcting codes, yields quantum error-correcting codes. This provides a physical interpretation for a previously known quantum-classical correspondence in coding theory.

Quantum codes in the Lee metric

Authors: Jinkang Guo, Yiqiu Han, Aranya Chakraborty, Shubham P. Jain, Tianhao Liu, Victor V. Albert, Andrew Lucas
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.04834v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a quantum coding theory framework for qudits designed to combat discrete small-shift noise, utilizing the Lee metric instead of the standard Hamming metric. It develops mathematical tools to analyze stabilizer codes under this metric, showing potential advantages in qubit count and establishing fundamental bounds on achievable code distances. The work advances the theory of fault-tolerant quantum computation by incorporating realistic noise models.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly tackles advanced topics in quantum error correction by incorporating a physically motivated, non-uniform error model (the Lee metric) into the theory of qudit codes, which is highly relevant to understanding realistic noise in quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a quantum coding framework to address noise modeled by discrete small-shift Pauli errors (X and Z shifts) in qudit systems, moving beyond standard assumptions of equally likely errors.

Main result. It establishes that the joint Lee metric can yield qubit codes with fewer physical qubits than Hamming metric codes, and it proves an obstruction showing the Lee distance for qudit LDPC codes is bounded by $O(n)$.

Method. The authors develop a formalism for stabilizer codes over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ using joint and separate Lee metrics, analyzing error spreading under Clifford gates and employing techniques like the Gray map for qubitization.

Model / system. The system involves qudits (systems with $q$ levels) subjected to noise modeled by low-weight X- and Z-type Pauli shifts. The theoretical framework is built around generalizing classical Lee-metric coding theory to the quantum domain.

Key observables. Lee distance, code distance, error spread ($ ext{w}_B(U)$), and memory times for classical codes.

Important parameters / regimes. The qudit dimension $q$, the system size $n$, and the temperature $T$ (for classical dynamics).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the noise structure is governed by the Lee metric, which weights errors based on the total size of X and Z shifts, rather than assuming all Pauli shifts are equally likely.

Paper structure. The paper systematically introduces the Lee metric for qudits, develops the formalism for stabilizer codes using joint/separate metrics, analyzes specific constructions (e.g., qubitization, LDPC codes), and concludes with discussions on classical analogues and open questions.

Abstract

We introduce a quantum coding framework for discrete small-shift noise, in which errors on qudits are modeled as low-weight $X$- and $Z$-type Pauli shifts, analogous to small phase-space displacements in continuous-variable systems. This structure is approximately respected by nuclear-spin noise and captured by the Lee metric, motivating a quantum extension of classical Lee-metric coding theory. We develop such a formalism for stabilizer codes over $\mathbb{Z}_q$ for arbitrary $q$, using joint and separate Lee metrics for the $X$- and $Z$-components of errors. For qubits, the joint metric counts $Y$ errors twice and can yield codes that detect and correct $X$ and $Z$ errors with fewer physical qubits than codes designed for the conventional Hamming metric. We discuss Lee-weight spreading under Clifford gates and qubitize codes over $\mathbb{Z}_4$ via the Gray map, finding codes with two-fold transversal non-qubit-Clifford gates. For quantum CSS Lee-LDPC codes on $n$ qudits, we prove that the Lee distance cannot exceed $O(n)$, uniformly in $q$, demonstrating an unexpected obstruction to using the large internal Hilbert space of a large-$q$ qudit to make high-Lee-distance codes. Under local Metropolis dynamics, certain classical $q$-ary ``helical repetition codes'' have exponentially long memory times at fixed temperature for sufficiently large $q$ (that grows with system length), which can be understood as spontaneous symmetry breaking at finite temperature, even in local one-dimensional models. Hypergraph products of these helical repetition codes provide local two-dimensional quantum codes that inherit self-correction for $Z$ errors, but self-correction for $X$ errors remains an open question.

Quantum data loading from the learned shared structure of real signals

Summary. This paper introduces a quantum-native loader (QNL) designed to efficiently prepare quantum states from classical data by exploiting the shared underlying structure of real-world signals. By learning a low-dimensional representation, QNL requires far fewer input parameters than existing methods, demonstrating superior scalability and resource efficiency for quantum data encoding tasks.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on data loading rather than Hamiltonian simulation, the concept of learning a low-dimensional manifold to parameterize complex quantum operations is highly relevant for efficient quantum circuit design and resource estimation in quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge addressed is the high cost of preparing quantum states from classical data, which can negate any potential quantum speed-up.

Main result. The proposed quantum-native loader (QNL) significantly outperforms established structured loading methods by requiring a constant, small number of input parameters regardless of the signal size, while maintaining high fidelity.

Method. The method learns a low-dimensional, shared structure (manifold) from the entire dataset, allowing it to prepare any new signal using a single, fixed quantum circuit topology parameterized by a few inferred coordinates.

Model / system. The system involves encoding real-world time-series signals (like ECG) into quantum states using a fixed circuit structure derived from a learned shared basis, contrasting with per-sample circuit tailoring.

Key observables. State-preparation error ($ ext{Estate}$), the number of required input coordinates/coefficients ($r$), and the two-qubit gate count (CX cost) needed to achieve target fidelities.

Important parameters / regimes. Target fidelities (e.g., 99.50%), signal dimension ($D$), and the scaling exponent ($\alpha$) describing resource growth with dimension.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the source of the real signals occupies a low-dimensional family that is simultaneously statistical and physical, allowing for effective structure learning.

Figures summary. Figures compare the loading pipeline, showing QNL vs. shared Walsh support. They also compare error rates and resource requirements (like CX ratio) across varying sample sizes and fidelities.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the QNL approach, details the offline learning (fitting an affine decoder) and online inference process, and rigorously compares its performance against state-of-the-art structured loaders across multiple datasets and scaling regimes.

Abstract

Preparing quantum states from classical data can cost more than the computation they serve; most loaders tailor a circuit to each input. Here we show that the signals of a real dataset share structure that can be learned once and reused. Our quantum-native loader learns a low-dimensional description of a dataset and prepares every signal with one fixed circuit set by a few numbers. Across seven views of five public datasets it meets the targets of the strongest structured loader at equal gate cost with several times fewer numbers per signal. These numbers can be inferred from a random subset: in a preregistered blind replication the subset needed to come within ten per cent of full-signal accuracy stayed constant within a prespecified margin as signals grew sixteenfold, whereas the structured loader needed ever more. It declines what it cannot represent, covering fewer cases than that baseline and no electrocardiogram.

Quantum information processing under maximal distance measures

Authors: Bartosz Regula, Ludovico Lami, Mario Berta, Marco Tomamichel
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.06842v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work explores the consequences of using 'maximal' error metrics instead of standard ones in quantum information theory. It demonstrates that while some quantum tasks remain robust to this change, others adopt new rate functions based on the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy. Crucially, this shift invalidates several established reversibility theorems in the field.

Why it may be interesting. The findings redefine the fundamental quantitative measures of resource costs in quantum information, suggesting that standard theorems relying on simple divergence measures may fail when considering the most general operational constraints.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how changing the error metrics used in quantum information processing tasks, from standard minimal extensions to maximal extensions of classical divergences, affects task performance.

Main result. While some tasks (like distillation) are unaffected, others (like channel simulation) are governed by the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy, leading to the breakdown of known reversibility theorems.

Method. The authors compare minimal and maximal extensions of classical divergences, utilizing asymptotic i.i.d. limits to calculate task rates and characterizing the variational forms of these maximal divergences.

Model / system. The analysis applies to general quantum information processing tasks, including channel coding, quantum resource distillation, channel simulation, and quantum resource dilution, using quantum states and quantum channels as the core model.

Key observables. Asymptotic i.i.d. rates for various tasks, the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy, and various maximal divergences ($bT, bP, D_{\max}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The choice of error metric (minimal vs. maximal extension) and the specific type of quantum resource transformation being analyzed.

Assumptions / limitations. The maximal extensions are defined by optimizing over all classical distributions and quantum channels; the analysis often requires restricting to Resource-Non-Generating (RNG) operations in resource theories.

Figures summary. Table I compares asymptotic i.i.d. rates for three task types under standard versus maximal distance measures.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the framework by comparing minimal and maximal extensions of divergences. It then analyzes specific tasks (distillation vs. dilution) to show the dependence on the entropy measure. Finally, it extends this to general resource theories and analyzes additivity properties of information measures.

Abstract

The measures of distance frequently used in quantum information, such as trace distance or fidelity-based purified distance, are the smallest extensions of the corresponding distance measures in classical information theory. However, this way of extending classical distances is not unique, and a whole spectrum of other extensions can be defined. Here we study the question of how quantum information processing tasks are affected by a change of the error metrics to the maximal extensions of the classical distance measures, imposing more demanding error requirements on operational tasks. We find that the performance of some tasks, such as channel coding or quantum resource distillation, is not affected whatsoever by this change. However, for other tasks such as channel simulation and quantum resource dilution, we show that the rates are now given not by functions of the standard (Umegaki) quantum relative entropy, but ones based on the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy. Our results connect the Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy with operational tasks and reveal that known reversibility results in quantum information, including the reverse Shannon theorem and the asymptotic reversibility of quantum resource transformations, no longer hold under maximal distance measures.

Quantum minimum description of density matrices

Summary. This paper calculates the quantum minimum description length for compressing density matrices when only the spectrum is known, not the eigenbasis. It establishes both the achievable rate and the fundamental lower bound using advanced tools from representation theory and information theory. This provides a precise measure of the coding overhead required for quantum state estimation and compression.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides rigorous, quantitative bounds on the fundamental limits of quantum data compression, which underpins resource theories and quantum communication protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the quantum minimum description length (QMDL) for compressing many copies of a density matrix when the spectrum is known but the eigenbasis is unknown, extending Schumacher compression.

Main result. The optimal memory cost is explicitly determined by a formula involving the dimension and eigenvalue differences, quantifying the coding overhead above standard entropy.

Method. The analysis uses generalizations of Werner's cloning map for irreducible representations and relies on a quantitative form of Koashi–Imoto incompressibility for the converse bound.

Model / system. The system involves density matrices ($ ho$) in dimension $d$, analyzed in the context of compressing multiple copies ($ ho^{\otimes n}$) where the state is defined by its spectrum $\mathbf{x}$ and an unknown unitary transformation $U(g)$.

Key observables. Quantum Minimum Description Length (QMDL), achievable memory size $|M_n|$, and the trace-distance error $\delta_n$.

Important parameters / regimes. Fixed dimension $d$ and a fixed spectrum $\mathbf{x}$ with distinct positive eigenvalues.

Assumptions / limitations. The results assume a spectral-gap condition for the converse bound and concern the asymptotic behavior for fixed parameters.

Figures summary. No specific figure summaries were provided, but the text references mathematical equations and theorems detailing the cost and achievability.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by first establishing the optimal cost (Theorem 1) using generalized cloning maps for achievability, and then deriving the matching converse using incompressibility bounds.

Abstract

We study a variant of Schumacher compression. The task asks for the minimal memory cost for compressing many copies of a density matrix with known spectrum and unknown eigenbasis, without preserving its purification. For fixed dimension and distinct positive eigenvalues, we obtain the cost through its additive constant. Achievability uses a generalization of Werner's cloning map to $\mathrm{GL}(d,\mathbb C)$ irreducible representations, with finite trace-distance bounds controlled by highest-weight differences and row gaps. The matching converse follows from a quantitative form of Koashi--Imoto incompressibility for irreducible group orbits under a spectral-gap assumption. We also identify the relation between this quantum minimum description length and universal lossless coding overhead, and its connection to free entropy is explained in a companion letter. We provide a Lean certificate for our proofs.

Quantum Query Complexity and Span Programs from Pre-Geometry

Authors: Justin Roy Cox, Neil Epstein, Zhirui Hu, Michael Jarret, Thomas De Mastri

Summary. This theoretical paper introduces a novel matroidal framework to analyze quantum query algorithms using span programs. It successfully separates the inherent query dependence from the algorithmic structure, leading to rigorous proofs that equate the optimal query cost to the general adversary bound for binary queries. This provides deep mathematical insight into the fundamental limits of quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. While highly abstract, the rigorous mathematical connection between combinatorial optimization (matroids) and the fundamental limits of quantum computation (query complexity) provides powerful new tools for analyzing quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a general framework using matroids to design and analyze quantum query algorithms, aiming to relate the optimal query cost to the general adversary bound.

Main result. For separating binary query sets, the global matroid-span-program optimum is shown to equal the quantum adversary bound, and explicit asymptotic bounds are derived for complex query problems.

Method. The core method involves developing a matroidal organization that separates query dependence (source matroid) from program structure dependence (program matroid), allowing for compositional evaluation using Seymour decomposition.

Model / system. The study focuses on quantum query models, specifically using 'span programs' as a general algorithmic framework. The mathematical machinery heavily relies on Matroid Theory to characterize the constraints imposed by the query problem.

Key observables. Quantum query complexity ($ ext{Adv}_{\pm}Q(f)$), minimum span-program complexity ($ ext{MSP}(f, \mathfrak{M}_f)$), and various derived asymptotic exponents ($\alpha$).

Important parameters / regimes. The input size $N$, the error parameter $\epsilon$, and the structure of the query function $f$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis builds upon the existence of source and program matroids, and for certain cases, assumes the applicability of the partition-based quantum query model.

Figures summary. The notes reference equations detailing the equivalence between the partition-based query model and the standard response-oracle model, and the steps for deriving the adversary lower bound.

Paper structure. The paper is highly theoretical, structured around applying Matroid Theory to Quantum Query Models. It moves from defining the general span program framework to establishing the source/program matroid separation, and finally deriving concrete bounds and asymptotic results for specific query types.

Abstract

Span programs provide a general method for designing quantum query algorithms, and for Boolean coordinate queries their optimum cost agrees with the general adversary bound. We develop a matroidal organization of this optimization that separates dependence data determined by the query problem from the dependence structure chosen by a span program. The resulting source matroid records query dependence, target determination, and separation data, while a program matroid records dependencies among the target and response-labeled input elements of a candidate program. When a query problem admits a source matroid, we use its structure to organize adversary upper and lower bounds. For separating binary query sets in this setting, the global matroid-span-program optimum equals $\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}_{\mathcal Q}(f)$. For a fixed regular program matroid, projective uniqueness reduces optimization over real representations to positive element weights. We express positive and negative costs as weighted basis ratios and derive exact $1$-, $2$-, and $3$-sum reductions, giving a compositional evaluation of the fixed-weight objective along a Seymour decomposition. For pointed $R_{10}$ closure, exact certificates give $3.9087553<\mathrm{Adv}^{\pm}_{\mathcal Q_R}(f_R)<3.9301$. The natural $R_{10}$ program has optimized cost $5$, while another regular program has cost at most $\sqrt{87/5}$. Recursive self-composition yields a family on $N$ input bits with randomized query complexity $Ω(N^{0.7324867\ldots})$, an explicit quantum algorithm using $O(N^{0.6500178\ldots})$ queries, and $Q(F)=Θ(N^α)$ for $0.6204277\ldots<α<0.6229063\ldots$.

Abstract

We present ToF-PAX-RIXS, a compact instrument that combines time-of-flight momentum-microscopy angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) with resonant inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS). The latter is based on photoelectron spectrometry for analysis of X-rays (PAX). The two techniques are implemented with a single multimode electron-optical column, allowing measurements to be performed sequentially on the same sample under identical experimental conditions. In ARPES mode, the instrument records the photoemission intensity as a function of kinetic energy and two in-plane momentum components. In PAX-RIXS mode, scattered soft X-ray photons are absorbed in a thin metallic converter, and the generated photoelectrons are analyzed in energy and position of emission on the converter. The electron kinetic energy retains the spectral information of the scattered photons, while the position is related to the scattering angle and therefore to the momentum transfer, enabling simultaneous access to a finite momentum-transfer interval. We describe the mechanical design, converter assembly, scattering geometry, and multimode electron optics of the instrument. Its PAX-RIXS performance is benchmarked at the Cu $L_3$ edge using CaCuO$_2$, with Au, Ag, and Pt converters. The measured spectra reproduce the principal features obtained with a conventional grating RIXS spectrometer, and deconvolution yields an effective reconstructed energy resolution better than $120$~meV under the present conditions. Finally, combined ARPES and PAX-RIXS measurements on the same Ca-doped YBa$_2$Cu$_3$O$_{6.45}$ sample demonstrate the capability of the instrument to correlate electronic structure and collective excitations within a common compact platform.

Summary. This work defines and proves the existence of 'topology-independent quantum advantage' in communication networks. Abstract

Quantum communication can outperform classical communication in a variety of information-processing tasks, but such advantages are typically established for a fixed communication network. Here we introduce topology-independent quantum advantage, in which a quantum protocol outperforms every classical protocol subject to the same communication constraint, irrespective of how the classical communication resource is distributed among the parties or which communication topology is employed, even when the parties may share unlimited classical randomness. We first demonstrate this phenomenon through a simple three-party equality task: two senders receive ternary classical inputs, while an input-free receiver determines whether the inputs are equal. We then formulate a general framework for topology-independent communication and completely characterize the corresponding classical correlation polytope in the minimal three-party scenario. Its facet inequalities reveal several instances of topology-independent quantum advantage. Remarkably, one of these inequalities enables the semi-device-independent certification of a nonunitary quantum channel, while another certifies a sequential quantum network topology without any prior assumption about the network configuration. Finally, we extend the framework to four-party networks, characterize the relevant classical communication structures, and identify a communication task exhibiting quantum advantages against all admissible classical topologies. Our results establish topology independence as a genuine and robust form of quantum advantage in communication networks.
