Training Variational Quantum Algorithms Is NP-Hard, Even Locally

Authors: Dax Enshan Koh, Triscia Mundo, Iosif Sakos, Antonios Varvitsiotis
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.05128v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

Summary. This paper proves that training Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) is computationally hard, showing that even finding a local minimum of the objective function is strongly NP-hard. The authors achieve this by reducing the problem from the known hard Stable Set problem to the minimization of a trigonometric polynomial, which is then mapped directly onto a VQA training objective. This establishes a fundamental worst-case computational barrier for variational quantum optimization.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a fundamental theoretical barrier, suggesting that even local optimization in VQAs is computationally intractable in the worst case, which is crucial for understanding the limits of near-term quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the computational hardness of training Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs), specifically focusing on the difficulty of finding local minima of the training objective function.

Main result. It proves that finding a local minimum of a VQA training objective, or even approximating a point near it, is strongly NP-hard in the worst case.

Method. The proof uses a polynomial-time reduction from the Stable Set problem, transforming the hard combinatorial optimization problem into the minimization of a Hermitian trigonometric polynomial, which is then mapped to the VQA objective function.

Model / system. The model is the VQA, which trains parameterized quantum circuits by minimizing an objective function $F( heta) = \langle 0|U( heta)^\dagger H U( heta)|0 angle$. The hardness is shown even for simple circuits using single-qubit rotations and commuting observables.

Key observables. The training objective function $F( heta)$, the location of local minimizers $ heta^*$, and the $\ell_p$-distance to these minimizers.

Important parameters / regimes. The circuit parameters $ heta \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the distance metric $\ell_p$ ($p \ge 1$), and the size of the graph $G$ used in the reduction.

Assumptions / limitations. The hardness result is established for the worst-case scenario, and the proof relies on a dichotomy related to the stable set size $\alpha(G)$ versus a parameter $k$.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the separation of possible local minimizers in the parameter space for the hard trigonometric polynomial instance, and Figure 2 shows the mapping between local minimizers under the trigonometric substitution $T( heta)$.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the computational barrier by first reducing the Stable Set problem to minimizing a Hermitian trigonometric polynomial, and then constructing an explicit quantum circuit whose VQA training objective reproduces this hard instance.

Abstract

Variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) generally rely on classical optimization to train parameterized quantum circuits. This training seeks to minimize an objective function, and its efficiency is central to the practical success of these algorithms. However, globally minimizing such training objectives over the circuit parameters is known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-hard, limiting the prospect of general guarantees for efficient training. In this Letter, we prove that even the weaker task of finding a local minimum of such VQA training objectives is strongly $\mathsf{NP}$-hard, including when the objective admits efficient classical evaluation. Moreover, we show that this hardness persists even for the task of finding a parameter vector within $\ell_p$-distance strictly less than $π/2$ of some local minimizer, for every $p\geq 1$. Our central technical result is that approximating a local minimizer of a Hermitian trigonometric polynomial is strongly $\mathsf{NP}$-hard. By explicitly constructing quantum circuits whose training objectives reproduce these hard instances, we obtain a polynomial-time reduction to VQA training. Our results establish a fundamental computational barrier to variational quantum training: even reaching the vicinity of a local minimum remains hard in the worst case.

Universal Improvement of Channel Fidelity via Entanglement Assistance

Summary. This paper introduces a universal, entanglement-assisted protocol to boost the worst-case fidelity of quantum channels when multiple noisy channels are available. By constructing a specific superinstrument, the authors demonstrate that the output channel can surpass the fidelity of any individual input channel. This establishes a powerful theoretical tool for quantum communication resource enhancement.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides fundamental theoretical tools for understanding how shared quantum resources (like entanglement) can overcome inherent noise limitations in communication protocols, which is central to quantum networking.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of finding a universal method to improve the worst-case fidelity of quantum communication channels when multiple, potentially non-identical channels are used.

Main result. It proves that an entanglement-assisted encoding and decoding strategy can universally enhance the worst-case fidelity of quantum channels beyond the fidelity of any single given channel.

Method. The authors employ resource theory concepts, constructing an entanglement-assisted superinstrument ($\Theta$) acting on the tensor product of the input channels to achieve the enhancement.

Model / system. The system involves quantum communication using $n$ nonidentical quantum channels $\{N_i\}$. The analysis is framed using superchannels and resource theories, particularly focusing on fidelity measures.

Key observables. Worst-case fidelity ($F(N)$) and entanglement fidelity ($ ext{Fe}( ext{N})$) are the primary observables used to quantify channel quality.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of channels ($n$), the initial entanglement fidelities of the channels, and the critical fidelity threshold ($F_c$) are important parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. The protocol requires the input channels to satisfy mild assumptions on their initial entanglement fidelities, and the analysis often relies on the ability to perform channel twirling.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the physical decomposition of superchannels and superinstruments, and show the general task of achieving universal channel fidelity improvement.

Paper structure. The paper develops the problem by defining the goal of universal fidelity improvement, constructs the entanglement-assisted protocol, proves its optimality for specific channels (like depolarizing ones), and then generalizes the findings to classical channels and general resource theories.

Abstract

Communication using quantum channels generally requires encoding and decoding on many identical uses of a quantum channel. In practical settings, decoherence may severely limit our ability to do so, potentially rendering each channel use nonidentical. Given $n$ nonidentical channels, we present an entanglement-assisted encoding and decoding strategy that yields a channel with higher worst-case fidelity than all of the $n$ given channels. The strategy is universal, in the sense that the improvement holds regardless of what channels are given, as long as they satisfy mild assumptions on their initial entanglement fidelities. Our protocol is optimal when the given channels are depolarizing channels, and is essentially the unique one that is universal. This idea can also be extended to classical channels, where shared randomness between the sender and the receiver allows universal enhancement of the probability of correct transmission. We then consider general quantum resource theories, providing a necessary condition and a sufficient condition for the possibility of such universal improvement of quantum resources.

Summary. This work establishes a deep mathematical correspondence between the zeta functions describing quantum walks on finite paths and continuum spectral determinants. By analyzing the factorization of the discrete zeta function in various limits, the authors show that the discrete structure acts as a 'pre-geometric skeleton' whose continuum limit is governed by the massive Dirac operator. This links quantum walk dynamics to fundamental results in spectral geometry.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between discrete spectral identities (quantum walks) and continuous spectral theory (Dirac operators) via zeta regularization is a powerful tool for understanding spectral properties in quantum systems, linking lattice physics to continuum field theory.

Main problem. The paper investigates the 'Walk/Zeta correspondence' by establishing a relationship between the zeta functions of quantum walks on finite path graphs and continuum trace formulas.

Main result. The discrete zeta function factorizes into components indexed by the infinite dihedral group, and in the continuum limit, this product matches the Gelfand–Yaglom determinant of a massive Dirac operator.

Method. The authors use a generalized reflection principle and a local Konno–Sato correspondence to derive exact discrete trace formulas, which are then analyzed in various scaling limits.

Model / system. The model is a quantum walk (Szegedy walk) and a classical random walk on a finite path graph ($P_N$) with reflecting boundaries and arbitrary spatial asymmetry. The analysis compares these discrete systems to their infinite-line counterparts.

Key observables. Zeta functions ($\det(I - uP(N))^{-1}$), the boundary factor, and the local factors indexed by the dihedral group.

Important parameters / regimes. Asymmetry ($p eq q$), the size of the graph ($N$), and scaling parameters ($s, M$) used in the continuum limit.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of the generalized reflection principle and the local Konno–Sato correspondence to establish the factorization.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the discrete factorization of the zeta function, then analyzing the convergence of this factorization in the thermodynamic, diffusive, and ballistic limits, culminating in the identification of the continuum limit with the massive Dirac operator determinant.

Abstract

We establish a local Konno-Sato correspondence for reflected random and Szegedy walks on a finite path graph. A generalized reflection principle, using explicit involutions rather than point reflections in the asymmetric case, yields exact zeta factorizations into a boundary factor and local factors indexed by the infinite dihedral group. After symmetrization, the classical and quantum local factors correspond for each group element. The identity contribution determines the normalized thermodynamic limits. In the symmetric case, diffusive and ballistic scalings recover the Gaussian Poisson summation formula and the Dirac comb identity, respectively. Under a double scaling of the asymmetry and spectral parameter, the quantum factorization yields a massive Dirac-type limit: nonidentity even words produce the periodic-orbit factor, the identity produces the free-field factor, and odd words together with the boundary produce a finite boundary correction.

The work serves as a cautionary feasibility study on the current resource overhead of QNLP simulations.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the need to measure simulator cost in QNLP, details the experimental setup (VQC vs. classical baselines on SST-2), presents the quantitative results on cost scaling and accuracy comparison, and concludes with limitations.

Abstract

Near-term quantum natural language processing (QNLP) experiments often run on classical simulators, so simulator cost is part of the field's practical compute burden, yet accuracy tables do not show it. We measure that cost for a variational quantum classifier (VQC) on binary SST-2 sentiment classification, using PennyLane's state-vector simulator over a controlled grid of 27 configurations: three balanced training-set sizes (N = 200, 500, 1000), three qubit counts (4, 6, 8), and three circuit depths. Each VQC is compared with logistic regression on the same PCA-reduced input; full TF-IDF logistic regression gives an uncompressed reference. The VQC beats its matched baseline in 6 of 27 single-seed comparisons. After reruns at two further seeds, only 1 of these 6 keeps a positive mean advantage larger than its paired seed-to-seed variability, and paired tests on the fixed validation set do not establish it. VQC training is 886-21,127 times slower in measured wall-clock time than the matched classical fit (median 3,158 times); going from 4 to 8 qubits roughly doubles simulator time, and within the tested range per-step cost is well approximated by a linear function of the parameter count. CodeCarbon energy and CO2 estimates are secondary: they imply an almost constant power of about 41 W, so they add little beyond runtime, and we do not build an energy ratio from them. The study is narrow (one dataset, representation, ansatz, simulator, and CPU environment) and is a reproducible feasibility measurement, not a general verdict on QNLP.

Summary. This paper investigates how quantum circuits lose access to high-level synthesis optimizations when their representation changes across different compilers or formats. The authors demonstrate that merely ensuring the computation remains mathematically equivalent is insufficient, as this loss can lead to significantly suboptimal gate counts. They introduce 'Verified,' a novel mechanism that tracks and restores these lost synthesis choices, improving compiler robustness.

Why it may be interesting. It highlights a critical, practical gap in quantum software stacks: the gap between mathematical equivalence and compiler optimization potential, which is vital for designing efficient quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Quantum compilers can lose the ability to apply high-level synthesis optimizations when quantum circuits undergo representation changes (like serialization or format conversion).

Main result. Functional equivalence is insufficient; the loss of synthesis availability can significantly increase circuit costs, and a new 'Verified' mechanism is proposed to restore these lost synthesis choices.

Method. The authors conducted controlled experiments and repository analyses across major quantum SDKs (Qiskit, TKET, Cirq) by simulating various representation change routes.

Model / system. The study focuses on abstract quantum circuits and the compilation process itself, using high-level operations like QFT and MCX gates as test cases.

Key observables. Synthesis availability, two-qubit gate count, circuit depth, and the success rate of operation record reconstruction.

Important parameters / regimes. The specific quantum compilers (Qiskit, TKET, Cirq) and the type of representation change (OpenQASM 3 round trip, QPY serialization, etc.).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that preserving functional equivalence is not enough; the compiler must also preserve the potential for high-level synthesis.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how direct OpenQASM 3 round trips lose synthesis availability, while the proposed 'Verified' mechanism successfully restores it, often showing a reduction in gate count compared to unverified routes.

Paper structure. The paper identifies the problem of synthesis loss during representation changes, analyzes this loss across multiple compiler toolchains and routes, quantifies the resulting circuit cost increases, and finally introduces and validates the 'Verified' recovery mechanism.

Abstract

Quantum compilers synthesize high-level operations, such as the quantum Fourier transform and multi-controlled X gates, into gate-level circuits. Across compilation stages, a circuit may be serialized, exchanged as OpenQASM, converted between compilers, or lowered to gates. These representation changes can preserve computation while removing the high-level operation itself, leaving the receiving compiler unable to apply a requested synthesis method. We call this loss synthesis availability. We study synthesis availability in Qiskit, TKET, and Cirq through controlled experiments, repository analysis, circuits captured from project tests, and a Munich Quantum Toolkit pipeline. Synthesis availability survives serialization when deserialization restores the high-level operation and survives compiler conversion only when the receiving compiler supports that operation. It does not survive the evaluated OpenQASM routes. After a direct OpenQASM 3 round trip, requested synthesis has no effect in all 360 delivered conditions even though compilation succeeds. In the MQT pipeline, OpenQASM 2 interchange increases the two-qubit gate count by 37.2 percent for an eight-qubit Grover circuit. We also introduce Verified, which records high-level operations before a representation change and reconstructs them only when a check confirms that the record still matches the delivered circuit. Verified restores requested synthesis in all 830 recovery runs, with median verification times from 2.0 to 638 ms per operation family. The check rejects every stale record but also some valid records. These results show that preserving functional equivalence alone is insufficient when later synthesis choices depend on retained high-level structure.

Summary. This paper rigorously classifies when the Clifford hierarchy of quantum gates, $C_k(n)$, belongs to the semi-Clifford or generalized semi-Clifford gate sets. Using advanced group theory and symplectic geometry, the authors derive necessary and sufficient conditions based on the hierarchy level $k$ and qubit number $n$. The work provides deep structural insights into the mathematical boundaries of quantum gate sets.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the mathematical conditions under which the Clifford hierarchy of quantum gates, $C_k(n)$, is contained within the sets of semi-Clifford ($ ext{SC}(n)$) or generalized semi-Clifford ($ ext{GSC}(n)$) gates.

Main result. It establishes precise conditions for $ ext{SC}(n)$ membership (e.g., $k\le2$, $n\le2$, or $k=3, n\le6$) and provides structural characterizations and counterexamples showing when $C_k(n)$ fails to be $ ext{GSC}(n)$ for large $k$ and $n$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first characterizing $ ext{SC}(n)$ membership, then tackling the more general $ ext{GSC}(n)$ membership using conductor arguments, and finally constructing explicit counterexamples (like $U_\xi$) to show when the hierarchy escapes the $ ext{GSC}$ group.

Abstract

We study semi-Clifford and generalized semi-Clifford structures in the Clifford hierarchy as a function of hierarchy level $k$ and qubit number $n$. We show that $\mathcal{C}_k(n)$ is semi-Clifford if and only if $k\le2$, $n\le2$, or $k=3$ and $n\le6$. We also establish that $\mathcal{C}_k(n)$ is generalized semi-Clifford when $k\le4$ or $n\le3$ and construct counterexamples when $k,n \geq 5$. For $n=4$ and $k\ge5$, we show that every gate defined over $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ up to a scalar is generalized semi-Clifford, reduce the general case to a single level, and propose a strategy to prove our conjecture that $\mathcal{C}_k(n)$ is generalized semi-Clifford if and only if $k\le4$ or $n\le4$. Lastly, we go beyond this structure by introducing $n_c$-generalized semi-Clifford gates, which we show are exactly the gates of the form $\sum_x|π(x)\rangle\langle x|\otimes V_x$ up to Clifford gates on both sides, where $π$ permutes the computational basis states of $n_c$ control qubits. We conjecture that for $k\ge3$ every gate in $\mathcal{C}_k(n)$ has this form for some $n_c\ge1$ with every $V_x\in\mathcal{C}_{k-3}(n-n_c)$, refining the conjecture of Zeng, Chen, and Chuang.

Summary. This paper introduces WLPA, a novel, efficient framework for optimally allocating scarce quantum-safe resources like QKD across large networks. Abstract

Network operators migrating distributed systems to post-quantum and quantum-safe cryptography face a resource-management problem: quantum key distribution (QKD) gives the strongest guarantee available but is hardware-scarce and costly, so only a fraction of a deployment's links can receive it. Default practice -- ranking links by traffic or centrality and upgrading the top scorers -- optimizes each link in isolation, while a distributed job's real security is set by whichever active link ends up weakest. We present WLPA (Weakest-Link Posture Assignment), an implementation-ready decision framework and algorithm for allocating scarce quantum-safe postures under hardware, latency, and compliance constraints. WLPA computes the optimal weakest-link posture in closed form and returns an assignment in O(|E| log |E|) time -- 125 ms for a 20,000-link network on a laptop -- with a breach-aware refinement matching an exact integer program under common conditions. Our central contribution is a dichotomy telling operators exactly when existing per-link heuristics already match this optimum and when they systematically fail: under constant upgrade cost and uniform traffic-driven risk the two coincide exactly; once costs vary or an adversary concentrates on weak targets, heuristics leave the weakest link unprotected, unboundedly. We validate WLPA across 19 experiments: four microservice topologies, a real enterprise network (SNAP email-Eu-core), a post-quantum latency benchmark, a Qiskit BB84 simulation and a real-hardware BB84 run on IBM's ibm_marrakesh processor, checks against real production traffic traces, a CVE-calibrated correlated-compromise study, Bayesian uncertainty quantification, and seven baseline methods. A concrete-security reduction grounds the framework as theoretical foundation, not primary contribution.

Abstract

For two decades, the origin of Chirality-Induced Spin Selectivity (CISS), the spin polarization of electrons by nonmagnetic chiral systems without magnetic fields, has remained unsettled. Here, we establish a single exact rule defining the fundamental conditions for the effect: in nonmagnetic, time-reversal-invariant conductors, all measurable CISS signals are time-reversal odd and vanish near equilibrium, proving that structural chirality alone is insufficient. By evaluating leading theoretical mechanisms across four levels of nonequilibrium transport, from coherent classical driving to fully non-Markovian quantum baths, we verify this selection rule to machine precision. We show that coherent chiral vibrations generate substantial collinear polarization (up to 10 percent across 0.5-6 THz) that reverses with handedness, whereas incoherent vibrations yield under 1 percent, and non-Markovian bath memory further suppresses the signal. The chiral geometry first makes the electronic motion chiral by accumulating orbital angular momentum; spin-orbit coupling (SOC) then converts that into spin. CISS therefore acts as both a spin polarizer and a spin filter, the latter an order of magnitude weaker. The polarization grows with molecular length and then saturates, matching trends reported for DNA and peptides. Reversing the drive converts decaying spin into a handedness-locked charge-current pulse (inverse-CISS). We find that once the system is driven, the polarization magnitude scales with an effective spin-orbit coupling: making heavy atoms and curved light-atom backbones indistinguishable at equivalent effective SOC. This enables us to chart how geometry, driving field, and length separate those routes.

Abstract

Yet, in planar thin films, individual vortices are difficult to manipulate, often requiring intricate nanopatterning or demanding scanning-probe instrumentation. Here, we show that shaping a superconducting thin film into a three-dimensional curved geometry enables controllable vortex trapping under a spatially uniform applied magnetic field.
