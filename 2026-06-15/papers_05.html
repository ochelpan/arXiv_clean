A coupled-oscillator model for the formation of planetary rings

Summary. This paper models planetary ring formation by treating particle interactions as discrete collisions within a coupled oscillator system. It demonstrates that the resulting collective behavior maps onto the Kuramoto model, predicting a phase transition that dictates whether the material aggregates into moons or forms stable rings. The work provides scaling laws connecting orbital mechanics parameters to the effective coupling strength.

Why it may be interesting. While applied to astrophysics, the core mathematical framework—modeling collective behavior via a phase transition analogous to the Kuramoto model using collision dynamics—is highly relevant to non-equilibrium statistical mechanics and many-body synchronization problems in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to explain the physical dichotomy between the formation of compact satellite bodies versus extended planetary ring systems in protoplanetary disks.

Main result. The system dynamics are shown to be mathematically equivalent to a variant of the Kuramoto model, exhibiting a phase transition that governs whether the final state is aggregated (moon-like) or dispersed (ring-like).

Method. The authors develop a collision-based framework where particle interactions and energy dissipation drive the evolution, comparing this discrete process to continuous mean-field models.

Model / system. The model consists of N identical particles on coplanar circular orbits around a central mass M. Interactions are modeled as pairwise collisions that conserve angular momentum while allowing for energy dissipation ($E_0\Delta t$).

Key observables. Order Parameter (R), final orbital radii, and the phase transition point between synchronous/incoherent states.

Important parameters / regimes. Particle number (N), interaction range (d), standard deviation of radii ($\sigma_r$), and dissipation rate ($E_0$).

Assumptions / limitations. Particles are restricted to perfect coplanar circular orbits, and the energy loss mechanism is assumed constant per collision event.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate simulation snapshots showing ordered states (order parameter $\approx 0.75$), phase transition curves ($N$ vs $R$), and scaling laws for the effective coupling strength ($K_{pw}$) in weak versus strong interaction regimes.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the physical problem to deriving collision mechanics, establishing the analogy with the Kuramoto model, determining scaling laws for the coupling constant based on interaction regimes (weak/strong), and finally simulating the resulting structure formation.

Abstract

We study the dichotomy between compact satellite and ring formation in proto-planetary disks. Specifically, we examine the behavior of a model system of $N$ identical particles locked into circular, gravitationally-bound orbits around a central body. We treat interactions as dominated by inter-particle collisions, and extract an effective two-particle interaction function based on both theory and simulations. We then demonstrate that the expected dynamics are equivalent to a variant of the Kuramoto model, which undergoes a phase transition as parameters vary. This offers a novel potential explanation for the transition between formation of rings versus moons.

Summary. This work systematically studies spin current enhancement in ferromagnetic bilayers by modeling the influence of interlayer exchange coupling ($J_{ex}$). Main result. Model / system. The system is an epitaxial $ ext{Fe}{85} ext{Co}}/ ext{Ni{80} ext{Fe}$ bilayer grown on MgO[100] substrates. The dynamics are modeled using a bilayer Hamiltonian incorporating Zeeman, shape, cubic/uniaxial anisotropy, and the interlayer exchange coupling term ($J_{ex}$).

Abstract

We present a detailed study on how the strength of the interlayer magnetic coupling on Fe$_{85}$Co$_{15}$/Ni$_{80}$Fe$_{20}$ bilayers modifies the spin wave behavior of this system. A series of Fe$_{85}$Co$_{15}$/Ni$_{80}$Fe$_{20}$ bilayers deposited on MgO[100] substrates were grown by magnetron sputtering. Magnetic characterization of the samples was performed using a vibrating sample magnetometer and magneto-optical Kerr effect. The in-plane hysteresis loops reveal a cubic magnetic anisotropy of magnetocrystalline origin, with easy and hard axis along the [100] and [110] Fe-Co crystallographic directions, respectively. Ferromagnetic resonance measurements were performed to analyze the in-plane angular dependence of the resonance field, and also the resonance field at several frequencies was determined along the hard axis. By using a bilayer model in the frame of the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert magnetization equation of motion, the magnetization precession components were calculated, as well as the dependence of precession area on the Fe-Co layer thickness and the ferromagnetic interlayer coupling. We observe a maximum in the area of the ellipsoid generated by the magnetization precession of the permalloy layer at a certain exchange constant, showing that this effect could be used to maximize the injected spin currents, which could be tuned by changing the interlayer exchange constant in bilayer systems, the saturation magnetization of the materials, or the excitation frequency.

Summary. This work analyzes how thermal feedback controls pattern formation in chemical reaction-diffusion systems by dynamically coupling temperature evolution to reaction rates. It demonstrates that while temperature universally enhances instability and tunes spatial scales, the resulting global behavior—especially near boundaries—is critically dependent on whether the system is modeled as isothermal or thermally coupled.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical pattern formation, the methodology of coupling a non-equilibrium thermodynamic variable (T) into reaction kinetics via exponential dependencies is highly relevant to open quantum systems where environmental coupling dictates system dynamics or energy dissipation pathways.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how coupling temperature dynamics into reaction-diffusion systems modifies pattern formation, moving beyond traditional isothermal assumptions.

Main result. Thermal feedback acts as a general control mechanism, enhancing instability growth rates and shifting pattern selection toward shorter wavelengths. The global dynamic response (stationary vs. nonstationary) is highly dependent on the specific model kinetics and boundary conditions.

Method. The analysis employs linear stability analysis (LSA) around steady states for coupled reaction-diffusion equations, supplemented by numerical simulations to examine nonlinear boundary effects.

Model / system. The system involves activator-inhibitor kinetics ($u, v$) coupled to a dynamically evolving temperature field ($T$). The coupling is modeled using Arrhenius-type dependencies for reaction rates and heat exchange governed by Newton's law of cooling at the boundaries.

Key observables. Instability growth rates ($ ext{Re}(\lambda)$), pattern selection wavelengths, and global dynamic behavior (stationary vs. nonstationary).

Important parameters / regimes. Boundary conditions (Neumann vs. Dirichlet for T), temperature ($T_{ss}$), and kinetic parameters defining the Arrhenius factors.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that temperature evolves dynamically and is coupled to reactions, contrasting with previous isothermal assumptions; linear stability analysis is performed around a steady state.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate bifurcation diagrams showing instability regions in parameter space, dispersion relations demonstrating wavelength shifts with temperature, and simulated spatial patterns comparing zero-flux (Neumann) versus fixed-temperature (Dirichlet) boundary conditions for both CDIMA and Schnakenberg models.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general coupled equations, performing linear stability analysis to show kinetic renormalization effects, and then contrasting the nonlinear outcomes using two canonical models (CDIMA and Schnakenberg) under different thermal boundary constraints.

Abstract

Pattern formation in reaction-diffusion systems is traditionally analyzed under isothermal assumptions, overlooking the dynamical role of temperature in systems where reactions generate and dissipate heat. Here, we investigate non-isothermal reaction-diffusion dynamics by coupling activator-inhibitor kinetics to a dynamically evolving temperature field that modulates reaction rates through Arrhenius-type dependencies. This coupling introduces an additional feedback mechanism that influences stability and pattern selection. Through analytical and numerical analysis of the Cholrine dioxide-Iodine-Malonic acid (CDIMA) and Schnakenberg models, we demonstrate that thermal feedback modifies dispersion relations by enhancing instability growth rates and shifting pattern selection toward shorter wavelengths. Beyond these intrinsic effects, we identify a boundary-mediated mechanism in which thermal constraints qualitatively alter global dynamics. In particular, fixed-temperature boundaries induce nonstationary behavior in the CDIMA system, whereas the Schnakenberg model exhibits robust stationary patterns. These results establish thermal-kinetic coupling as a general mechanism for controlling pattern formation and highlight the role of boundary-mediated heat exchange as a tunable parameter for spatiotemporal organization.

Abstract

Focused helium ion beam (He-FIB) irradiation with 30 keV ions is a key tool for nanoscale patterning and defect engineering in high transition temperature (Tc) cuprate superconducting devices, yet its usable fluence window is constrained by the competing requirements of reliable superconductivity suppression and minimal structural degradation. In this work, we provide a comprehensive dataset on the effects of large-area 30 keV He+ ion exposure on the electric transport and superconducting properties of epitaxial YBa2Cu3O7 (YBCO) thin films. X-ray diffraction shows a fluence-driven loss of crystalline order accompanied by an out-of-plane lattice expansion and an orthorhombic-to-tetragonal transition, culminating at predominant amorphization at the highest fluence of 1 x 10^16 cm^-2. Raman spectra exhibit increasing disorder while lacking signatures of oxygen depletion, indicating that irradiation mainly generates oxygen-related Frenkel defects rather than changing the carrier concentration. Consistently, with increasing fluence, the normal-state resistivity ρ_N(T) at temperature T above Tc increases strongly, while d ρ_N/d T remains nearly unchanged at moderate fluence. The suppression of Tc is accurately described by Abrikosov-Gor'kov pair breaking and reaches complete quenching of superconductivity at 4.5 x 10^15 cm^-2. The anisotropic upper critical fields decrease approximately exponentially with increasing fluence, the vortex activation energy is reduced, and the anisotropy drops, in contrast to oxygen-depleted YBCO. Hall-angle analysis confirms a nearly constant carrier density but a systematic increase in defect scattering and reduced mobility, consistent with a crossover toward the dirty limit at high fluence. These results establish quantitative fluence thresholds and a practical operational window for He-FIB nanopatterning of YBCO quantum circuits.

Summary. This work investigates the symmetry of critical current in nanoscale superconductors using both experiments and advanced theoretical modeling. It establishes that while many multi-link systems obey $I$-$B$ symmetry, hybrid junctions can exhibit clear violations due to polarity-dependent selection among competing vortex states. This has implications for designing reliable superconducting electronic components.

Why it may be interesting. The study bridges fundamental superconducting symmetries with device physics, offering insights into how microscopic details (like junction type or polarity) can break macroscopic symmetries in quantum transport phenomena. This relates to understanding non-equilibrium dynamics in mesoscopic circuits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the symmetry properties of the critical current function ($I_c$) in superconducting nanodevices, specifically focusing on the $I$-$B$ inversion symmetry: $I_{c,+}(B) = -I_{c,-}(-B)$. The goal is to understand when this fundamental symmetry is preserved or broken.

Main result. The $I$-$B$ symmetry is generally obeyed in multi-nanowire SQUID models, but clear violations are observed in hybrid junctions (SIS–ML), suggesting that polarity-dependent selection of metastable vorticity states causes the asymmetry.

Method. The research combines low-temperature transport measurements on various nanodevices with theoretical modeling using effective potential energy landscapes derived from Meissner phase equations and Josephson junction physics.

Model / system. The systems include superconducting weak-link devices (nanowires, SQUIDs) modeled by combining different Current-Phase Relations (CPR), such as sinusoidal (SIS junctions) and linear (metallic links). The analysis focuses on the energy landscape governing vortex configurations.

Assumptions / limitations. For theoretical modeling, approximations include neglecting vector potential contributions along the length of each nanowire (short wire approximation) and assuming specific CPR forms.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate SEM images of tested devices; key figures compare measured $ ext{Ic}_+(B)$ vs. $- ext{Ic}_-(- ext{B})$ to demonstrate symmetry preservation or type 1/type 2 breaking in hybrid junctions, alongside simulated energy landscapes showing state transitions.

Abstract

We study a variety of nano-scale superconducting devices containing more than one weak link. Even in devices with multiple weak links the IB symmetry is usually obeyed, which means that if we reverse both the bias current direction and the magnetic field direction at the same time, the superconducting response, namely the critical current, remains unchanged. We also provide a detailed analysis of the situations in which such symmetry is violated.

Abstract

Wafer-level 3D integration of superconducting interconnects requires low-thermal budget bonding processes compatible with superconducting materials. Rapid native oxide formation on tantalum (Ta) surfaces limits low-temperature, low-pressure direct Ta-Ta thermocompression bonding. In this study, we develop an oxidation-resistant bonding process using an ultrathin Au passivation layer to suppress oxide formation during bonding. Abstract

Low-dimensional transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs) provide an ideal platform for studying the emergence of charge density wave (CDW) and superconductivity. The discovery of emergent CDW order in 1T $\mathrm{ZrTe_2}$ monolayer raises an important question: does this instability persist when one $\mathrm{Te}$ chalcogen layer is substituted by $\mathrm{Se}$? In the present work, we investigate the CDW (2$\times$2$\times$1) and superconducting instability in 1T $\mathrm{ZrSeTe}$ Janus monolayer using first-principles calculations. It originates primarily from the robust coupling between the soft phonon mode at $\mathrm{M}$ point and the electronic bands predominantly derived from $\mathrm{Zr}$ $\mathit{d}$ and $\mathrm{Te}$ $\mathit{p}$ orbitals crossing the Fermi level. Spin-orbit coupling (SOC) further modifies the electronic states and reduces the superconducting transition temperature.

Abstract

Abstract

We investigate the optical response of Kekulé-Y graphene under uniaxial strain and carrier doping. Using a low-energy effective Hamiltonian, we show that strain reshapes the low-energy electronic structure of the Kekulé-Y phase and induces Van Hove singularities at energies well below those of pristine graphene. Within the Kubo formalism, we calculate the optical conductivity and identify multiple anisotropic interband features, with a pronounced resonance arising from strain-induced Van Hove singularities. The pronounced resonance is strongly anisotropic and robust against moderate thermal broadening and disorder, providing a clear optical signature of Kekulé-Y ordering. We further derive analytical expressions for the low-energy optical conductivity and the Drude weight, providing a detailed characterization of the strain- and doping-dependent optical response. Our results establish strain engineering as an effective route for controlling valley-dependent optical properties in Kekulé-Y graphene, originating from the Kekulé-induced coupling of the Dirac valleys, and suggest feasible optical probes for the experimental identification of the Kekulé-Y phase.
