Spectral Fingerprints of Gauge Theories on a Quantum Computer

Authors: Graham Van Goffrier, Debasish Banerjee, Bipasha Chakraborty, Emilie Huffman
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27457v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 3/5 · quantum measurements 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper presents a quantum algorithm, Maximally Mixed State (MMS) spectral sampling, to extract the full density of states from a quantum Hamiltonian by measuring its time evolution. The technique is demonstrated on a non-Abelian $ ext{SO}(3)$ gauge theory, showing its feasibility on near-term quantum hardware. It provides a powerful, unbiased tool for probing diverse physical regimes, from ground state physics to thermalization, using quantum signal processing.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the computational bottleneck of characterizing complex quantum Hamiltonians by proposing a time-series quantum algorithm that bypasses the need for full eigenstate preparation, making it highly relevant for simulating strongly correlated systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to extract the full spectral distribution of a quantum many-body Hamiltonian, which contains crucial information about ground state properties, thermalization, and quantum criticality.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that the Maximally Mixed State (MMS) spectral sampling protocol can be successfully applied to an interacting lattice gauge theory, showing feasibility on near-term superconducting quantum hardware.

Method. The core method is MMS spectral sampling, which measures the characteristic function $ ext{Tr}[ ho_{ ext{MMS}} e^{-iHt}]$ via controlled quantum evolution circuits, followed by a Fourier transform to yield the density of states.

Model / system. The technique is applied to a $(1+1)$d non-Abelian $ ext{SO}(3)$ gauge theory (the $ ext{SO}(3)$ Quantum Link Model coupled to staggered fermions). The general framework applies to any system whose Hamiltonian $H$ can be mapped to a quantum circuit.

Key observables. Density of States ($\varrho_H(E)$), low-lying energies (for quantum criticality), and mid-spectrum features (for thermalization behavior).

Important parameters / regimes. The spectral resolution is limited by the total evolution time $T$; the circuit depth was demonstrated to be manageable (e.g., 78 two-qubit gates deep).

Assumptions / limitations. The output is a finite-resolution spectral distribution, not an exact listing of all eigenvalues. The method relies on the availability of mid-circuit measurement and reset for qubit optimization.

Figures summary. Figure 14 illustrates the Pauli-frame-optimized controlled-Trotter step for the $ ext{SO}(3)$ QLM, and Figure 15 compares the effect of using a Hann window versus a rectangular window in the post-processing Fourier transform.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the MMS protocol for spectral sampling, details the necessary quantum circuit compilation (including Pauli-frame optimizations), applies this technique to the $ ext{SO}(3)$ QLM, and discusses the interpretation of the resulting spectral fingerprints for various physical phenomena.

Abstract

Maximally mixed state spectral sampling is an unbiased quantum algorithm that allows for extraction of a finite-resolution spectral distribution from a Hamiltonian over potentially the entire allowed range of energies. We show how it may be focused on any desired area of the spectrum in order to learn about the full \textit{fingerprint} of the model of interest: from its ground state phenomena such as quantum criticality, obtained from the lowest lying energies, to its thermalization behavior, obtained from the mid-spectrum. We demonstrate this technique specifically on a $(1+1)d$ non-Abelian $SO(3)$ gauge theory, providing a comprehensive analysis of the steps necessary for performing this algorithm, as well as what is possible in the near-term with superconducting quantum hardware, performing simulations with circuits that are $78$ two-qubit gates deep. We show how this algorithm is able to take advantage of emerging dynamical circuit capabilities in near-term hardware to roughly halve the number required qubits, as well as how quantum readout error mitigation is trivial for this method. Along the way, we propose a novel strategy for compiling the controlled-time evolutions needed for spectral sampling by means of Pauli-frame optimizations. We illustrate two physical applications of quantum spectral sampling -- disordered many-body transitions, and mid-spectrum densities of states -- and what postprocessing steps they require beyond the Fourier outputs of the algorithm.

Cavity-assisted homodyne detection with a single photodiode

Authors: Jonas Junker
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26938v1

Summary. This paper introduces cavity-assisted homodyne detection as an advanced technique for measuring quantum quadratures at high frequencies. By coupling the signal and local oscillator through a resonant cavity, it overcomes the need for perfectly matched photodetection channels required by standard methods. The resulting setup offers high signal efficiency and superior suppression of technical noise sidebands using only a single photodiode.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical framework for implementing high-bandwidth quantum measurements using cavity QED principles, which is highly relevant for open quantum systems and quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Measuring quadrature components of high-frequency squeezed states is challenging, especially at gigahertz frequencies, due to limitations in standard detection methods.

Main result. Cavity-assisted homodyne detection achieves near-unity signal-transfer efficiency at moderate LO power while effectively suppressing technical LO noise sidebands.

Method. The authors derive quantum input-output relations for a traveling-wave cavity where the signal and LO interact, leading to a single-photodiode readout.

Model / system. The system uses an impedance-matched traveling-wave ring cavity where the quantum signal enters one port and the LO enters a separate port. The detection relies on the LO carrier beating with off-resonant signal sidebands reflected from the cavity.

Key observables. The photocurrent $\delta \hat{I}(\Omega)$ and the signal-transfer efficiency $\eta_{ ext{cav}}(\Omega)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Cavity linewidth ($\kappa$), Free Spectral Range (FSR), and the ratio of LO power to signal power.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the detected LO carrier amplitude dominates the residual signal carrier amplitude, and the cavity is impedance-matched.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the concept of signal and LO entering separate ports of the cavity, resulting in the detected field. Figure 2 shows the partitioning of the unit transfer coefficients ($\eta_{ ext{cav}}$, $|t|^2$, $|l|^2$).

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the limitations of BHD, then proposes the cavity-assisted setup, deriving the input-output relations. Subsequent sections analyze the noise suppression and compare the single-photodiode readout against alternative methods like beam-splitter readouts.

Abstract

High-frequency squeezed states are important for quantum metrology and information processing, but quadrature measurements at gigahertz frequencies remain challenging. Balanced homodyne detection (BHD), the standard approach, requires closely matched complex transfer functions in both photodetection channels to suppress local-oscillator (LO) noise and maintain a well-defined readout quadrature. This matching becomes increasingly difficult at high bandwidths. Strongly asymmetric single-photodiode homodyne detection avoids this requirement, but achieving high signal efficiency and shot-noise clearance typically requires hundreds of milliwatts of LO power and does not suppress technical LO sidebands. I propose cavity-assisted homodyne detection, in which the quantum field and LO enter separate ports of an impedance-matched traveling-wave cavity. The resonant LO carrier is transmitted to the photodetector, where it beats with off-resonant signal sidebands reflected from the cavity. At the same time, technical LO sidebands outside the cavity linewidth are suppressed. I derive the quantum input-output relations and linearized photocurrent including intracavity loss, and show that near-unity signal-transfer efficiency can be achieved at moderate incident LO power. The cavity linewidth and free spectral range determine the usable detection band.

How quantum is quantum geometry?

Authors: Ady Stern, Felix von Oppen
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26269v1

Summary. This paper explores the quantum geometric tensor by studying a classical model of a precessing magnetic moment. It demonstrates that several key quantum phenomena, such as anomalous velocity and emergent mass, have direct classical analogues arising from the interplay between fast and slow degrees of freedom. This suggests that the quantum nature of these effects might be less fundamental than previously thought for certain observables.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful theoretical tool for distinguishing between genuinely quantum effects and those that can be captured by semi-classical or classical descriptions, which is crucial for interpreting experimental results in condensed matter.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the physical phenomena associated with 'quantum geometry' (like anomalous Hall effects or superfluid weights) fundamentally require quantum mechanics by analyzing a purely classical analogue.

Main result. Most key observables associated with quantum geometry, including anomalous velocity, orbital magnetic moment, and emergent inertial mass, can be reproduced by analyzing the dynamics of a classical particle system.

Method. The analysis uses Hamiltonian classical mechanics, employing the adiabatic approximation to separate fast precession dynamics from slow particle motion, and calculating time-averaged quantities.

Model / system. The model is a point particle carrying a classical magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ that precesses in a momentum-dependent magnetic field $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{p})$. The analysis is generalized to include two interacting particles coupled by a spring potential.

Key observables. Anomalous velocity, position spread (metric), orbital magnetic moment, and emergent inertial mass/Drude weight.

Important parameters / regimes. The magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\ell}$, the coupling constant $k$, and the characteristic precession frequency $B$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the adiabatic approximation (fast precession relative to slow dynamics) and the ability to average over fast oscillations.

Figures summary. Not specified.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the classical analog of the quantum geometric tensor using a single particle. It then extends the analysis to two interacting particles, deriving an effective kinetic energy and showing how emergent mass arises from the interaction averaged over fast oscillations.

Abstract

The quantum geometric tensor - the Berry curvature together with the quantum metric - now underlies a long list of observables, from the anomalous Hall effect to the superfluid weight of a flat band. We ask which of these observables actually require quantum mechanics. To answer this question, we study a purely classical system: a point particle carrying a classical magnetic moment $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ that precesses in a momentum-dependent magnetic field $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{p})$. Within Hamiltonian classical mechanics, the component of $\boldsymbol{\ell}$ along the field reproduces the Berry-curvature phenomena, while its precessing transverse component reproduces the quantum-metric phenomena. The particle acquires a position spread whose second moment is the metric, an orbital magnetic moment, and - most strikingly - an inertial mass generated by a position-dependent force, and with it a nonzero Drude weight in a system that is nominally dispersionless.

Isotropic Nanoscale Quantum Sensor at Room-Temperature

Summary. This paper introduces an isotropic nanoscale quantum sensor based on the $ ext{NV}^0$ center in diamond, overcoming the major limitation of conventional sensors whose sensitivity is restricted by crystal orientation. By utilizing a spin-pair mechanism, the sensor's response dynamically aligns with the external magnetic field, enabling nanotesla sensitivity and alignment-free magnetometry at room temperature.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to AMO physics and quantum optics as it demonstrates a robust, solid-state quantum sensor operating at ambient conditions with long coherence times, pushing the boundaries of practical quantum sensing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Conventional color-center quantum sensors suffer from signal loss in off-axis magnetic fields because their spin quantization axes are fixed to the host lattice, limiting real-world applicability.

Main result. The use of the neutrally charged nitrogen-vacancy center ($ ext{NV}^0$) enables an isotropic magnetometer whose spin pair dynamically aligns with the external field, achieving nanotesla sensitivity at room temperature.

Method. The system uses Spin-to-Charge (SC)-ODMR spectroscopy to read out the spin state of the $ ext{NV}^0$-dark spin pair, which exhibits field-direction independence.

Model / system. The platform utilizes the $ ext{NV}^0$ center in diamond, specifically exploiting a spin-pair mechanism with an optically inactive dark spin. This pair's quantization axis dynamically aligns with the external magnetic field, providing an isotropic sensing mechanism.

Key observables. Resonance frequency of the central $|+1/2 angle \leftrightarrow |−1/2 angle$ transition, signal contrast, and coherence time ($T_2$).

Important parameters / regimes. Room temperature operation, nanotesla sensitivity, microsecond coherence time ($T_2 \approx 3 \mu ext{s}$), and alignment-free operation.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the $ ext{NV}^0$-dark spin pair's spin selection rules result in a quantization axis that is rotationally invariant with respect to the external field.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dynamic alignment of the $ ext{NV}^0$-dark spin pair, the dual-band SC-ODMR readout mechanism, and field mapping demonstrating isotropic response across various orientations.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the limitation of fixed-axis sensors to proposing the $ ext{NV}^0$-dark spin pair mechanism. It details spectroscopic measurements (SC-ODMR), validates the isotropic nature through field mapping, and confirms long coherence times using quantum control techniques.

Abstract

Color-center based quantum sensors provide nanoscale resolution under ambient conditions, yet their applicability remains limited. Because the quantization axes are locked to the host lattice, conventional color centers suffer severe signal loss in off-axis magnetic fields. To address this, we report an isotropic magnetometer enabled by the neutrally charged nitrogen-vacancy center (NV0) in diamond. Here, spin-to-charge dynamics yield an NV0-dark spin pair whose quantization axis dynamically aligns with the external field. Read out through NV charge-state-selective fluorescence, this system exhibits microsecond room-temperature coherence and nanotesla sensitivity for arbitrary field directions. We demonstrate alignment-free mapping of steep field gradients and single paramagnetic micro-targets, alongside isotropic readout from randomly oriented nanodiamonds. Resolving longstanding orientation constraints, this platform unlocks unrestricted nanoscale magnetometry across life sciences and quantum materials.

Vortex lattices in coupled one-dimensional Bose-Einstein condensates with a synthetic magnetic field

Summary. This paper theoretically examines how vortex lattices form in coupled 1D Bose-Einstein condensates subjected to a synthetic magnetic field. By analyzing the mean-field ground states, the authors show that the resulting vortex structure is critically governed by whether the system boundaries are periodic or open. The findings provide crucial insights into controlling quantum fluid behavior in engineered atomic potentials.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the interplay between topology (synthetic fields), geometry (finite size/boundary conditions), and superfluidity in ultracold atomic systems, which is a major frontier in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the mean-field ground states of coupled one-dimensional Bose-Einstein condensates subjected to a synthetic magnetic field.

Main result. The system's vortex structure is highly dependent on boundary conditions, favoring Abrikosov-like lattices under periodic boundary conditions and confining vortices centrally under open boundary conditions.

Method. The analysis employs mean-field theory, solving coupled Gross-Pitaevskii equations using numerical methods like imaginary time evolution and variational ansatz.

Model / system. The model is a coupled-wire system of 1D BECs, characterized by one continuous and one discrete spatial direction, allowing interpolation between few-leg ladders and extended vortex lattices.

Key observables. Density ($ ho_\lambda$), phase ($ heta_\lambda$), longitudinal and inter-wire currents ($j_{\parallel}, j_{\perp}$), and the emergence of vortex patterns.

Important parameters / regimes. Synthetic magnetic flux ($\phi$), inter-wire hopping strength ($J$), and the boundary conditions (Periodic vs. Open) in the discrete direction.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on mean-field approximations, treating the system as a bosonic condensate described by coupled Gross-Pitaevskii equations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the band structure for $N$ wires with open boundary conditions, showing how energy minima merge as coupling increases.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first analyzing the non-interacting model, then developing the mean-field coupled-wire model, and finally comparing the resulting vortex states and ground state evolution under different boundary conditions.

Abstract

We investigate the mean-field ground states of coupled one-dimensional Bose-Einstein condensates subject to a synthetic magnetic field. The resulting interacting coupled-wire model has one continuous and one discrete spatial direction, providing a controlled way to interpolate between the physics of few-leg ladders and extended vortex lattices. For two wires, we study the vortex-like, biased-density, and Meissner-like states, exploring how the finite longitudinal size of the system modifies the transitions between them. Increasing the number of wires, the ground state evolves towards an extended vortex lattice. We find numerically that periodic boundary conditions in the discrete direction can favour staggered arrays of like-signed vortices resembling an Abrikosov lattice, while open boundaries in small finite-size systems confine the vortices into rows near the centre of the synthetic direction. Our results explore how finite size effects and boundary conditions govern the emergence and spatial organisation of vortices in continuous-discrete quantum fluids, with relevance to future experiments in tunnel-coupled atomic wires or with synthetic dimensions.

Summary. This paper proposes using a scale-shared neural network architecture to perform real-space renormalization group calculations for 2D site percolation. By forcing the network to learn scale-dependent transformations, the model accurately predicts critical observables and exhibits finite-size scaling behavior, demonstrating the power of integrating physical symmetries into machine learning models.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit incorporation of RG principles into a neural network architecture provides a powerful, data-driven tool for analyzing critical phenomena, which shares conceptual links with how quantum critical points are studied in many-body physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing a data-driven, real-space renormalization group (RG) approach using machine learning to handle nonlocal observables in scale-invariant systems.

Main result. The model successfully predicts critical percolation observables, showing that the learned latent representation must exhibit critical fluctuations consistent with RG theory to achieve accurate finite-size scaling.

Method. A novel, scale-shared encoder-decoder neural architecture is trained to recursively apply coarse-graining and fine-graining rules across multiple scales.

Model / system. The study analyzes two-dimensional site percolation on a square lattice, a classic model for studying critical phenomena and phase transitions.

Key observables. Percolation probability ($\Pi$), order parameter ($P_\infty$), and the finite-size scaling variable $x = (p - p_c)L^{1/ u}$.

Important parameters / regimes. The critical probability $p_c$ and the correlation length exponent $ u$ are key parameters tested against theoretical predictions.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the RG structure provides a necessary inductive bias that allows the network to extrapolate accurately to much larger systems than those used for training.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the model's prediction of the spanning cluster mask at large sizes, the collapse of predicted observables onto finite-size scaling functions, and the schematic of the scale-shared RG-inspired architecture.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem of data-driven RG for nonlocal observables, details the scale-shared encoder-decoder architecture (encoder for coarse-graining, decoder for reconstruction), trains the model using specific losses, and validates its performance by demonstrating adherence to known finite-size scaling laws.

Abstract

Machine learning offers a possible route to data-driven real-space renormalization when the relevant observables are nonlocal and difficult to prescribe explicitly. We explore this idea for two-dimensional site percolation developping a supervised, scale-shared neural architecture. The model recursively applies the same learned coarse-graining rule across scales, producing a latent field from which the crossing probability is predicted, while a corresponding fine-graining decoder reconstructs the largest-cluster mask. Trained only on small lattices, the model extrapolates to substantially larger systems, recovers the spanning cluster with high fidelity, and produces observables obeying the expected finite-size scaling near the critical point. We observe that to get such performance it is key that the learned latent representation exhibits critical fluctuations and scale-dependent flows consistent with the renormalization-group structure of percolation.

The Role of Geometric Analysis in Interferometer Design and Optimization for Gravitational Quantum Entanglement

Authors: Alireza Maleki
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26420v1

Summary. This paper investigates using quantum entanglement in massive particle interferometers as a potential probe for quantum gravity effects. The authors rigorously show that simplified models of the interferometer geometry fail to accurately predict the induced entanglement. They provide exact solutions demonstrating that the full geometric details are crucial for correctly assessing the potential for gravity-induced quantum correlations.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it applies advanced quantum measurement techniques (interferometry) to fundamental physics (quantum gravity), providing concrete, calculable predictions for future quantum sensing experiments that might involve matter waves.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to probe the quantum nature of gravity by detecting gravitationally induced quantum entanglement between two massive particles using interferometry.

Main result. The analysis demonstrates that neglecting the full geometry of the interferometer, particularly the vertical path segments, leads to incorrect predictions for the induced entanglement, which can vanish entirely in specific configurations.

Method. The authors derive exact analytical solutions for the gravitational phase shifts and quantify entanglement using the concurrence measure, comparing these to simplified approximations.

Model / system. The physical system involves two massive particles passing through interferometer geometries (Stern-Gerlach and Mach-Zehnder setups). The evolution is governed by gravitational phase shifts derived from the interaction with the gravitational potential.

Key observables. Concurrence (C) quantifying entanglement, and the accumulated phase difference ($\Delta\phi$).

Important parameters / regimes. Particle masses ($m_1, m_2$), separation distance ($d$), and geometric ratios like $b/l$ and $\Delta x/l$.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption being tested is the validity of approximations that neglect phase contributions from the vertical segments of the particle trajectories.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental setups (Fig. 1, Fig. 5) and show the dependence of the rescaled induced phase ($\Delta\phi/\kappa$) on geometric parameters like deviation angle ($ heta$), horizontal separation ($\Delta x/l$), and vertical arm ratio ($b/l$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by establishing the physical problem, deriving simplified models, and then presenting exact solutions that incorporate full geometry. The structure emphasizes comparing the results of the simplified versus the exact treatments to highlight the necessity of full geometric consideration.

Abstract

The pursuit of a quantum theory of gravity, aiming to unify general relativity and quantum mechanics, remains one of the most enduring challenges in physics. Because of the extreme energy scales associated with the Planck regime, direct experimental evidence for quantum gravity remains elusive. However, recent proposals suggest that quantum entanglement between two massive particles may provide a pathway to probe the quantum nature of gravity. In this study, we examine the interferometer geometries proposed in these works, with particular attention to the commonly used approximation that neglects phase contributions from the vertical segments of the particle trajectories. Our analysis shows that this approximation can lead to incorrect predictions and, in certain parameter regimes, to null results where entanglement would otherwise be expected. We derive exact solutions that incorporate the full particle trajectories and demonstrate that the vertical arms can significantly affect the accumulated phase. Crucially, we identify configurations in which the induced entanglement vanishes entirely, a feature missed by simplified treatments. These findings show that accounting for the full interferometer geometry is not merely a refinement, but is essential for accurately assessing gravity-induced entanglement.

Dynamics of local quantum information in random unitary circuits

Authors: Ratul Thakur, Sthitadhi Roy
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27440v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5 · scars & prethermalization 1/5

Summary. This work develops a novel framework, the information lattice, to track how local quantum information spreads across different length scales in chaotic quantum systems. By analyzing random unitary circuits, the authors find that this information flow exhibits universal scaling characterized by a Tracy-Widom distribution. This establishes a powerful, fine-grained diagnostic tool for understanding quantum scrambling.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, length-scale-resolved measure of information flow that bridges microscopic quantum dynamics with macroscopic, hydrodynamic-like descriptions of scrambling and thermalization.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the limitation of standard, coarse-grained diagnostics (like bipartite entanglement) in characterizing the fine-grained spatiotemporal flow of local quantum information in out-of-equilibrium many-body systems.

Main result. The dynamics of local information are found to follow a universal scaling law, described by a Tracy-Widom distribution that grows ballistically in time ($\propto t$) with a $\sim t^{1/3}$ broadening, regardless of whether the system is Haar-random or Clifford.

Method. The authors develop a theory using an 'information lattice' to track information content at every length scale and location, mapping the dynamics onto an exact classical stochastic process for the infinite-dimensional limit.

Model / system. The study uses random unitary circuits as minimal models for quantum chaos, specifically examining Haar-random circuits (infinite local Hilbert space) and random Clifford circuits acting on qubits.

Key observables. Local information distribution across length scales, the dynamics of the information lattice, and the resulting Tracy-Widom distribution.

Important parameters / regimes. Time ($t$), local Hilbert-space dimension ($q$), and the specific type of random unitary circuit (Haar vs. Clifford).

Assumptions / limitations. Standard diagnostics are too coarse-grained; the local information dynamics can be mapped onto a classical stochastic process, and the results are assumed to be universal across different circuit types.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the information lattice structure and the ballistic growth of information flow ($\propto t$) with $\sim t^{1/3}$ fluctuations.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the information lattice framework to resolve local information flow. It then derives the dynamics for Haar-random circuits, mapping it to a classical stochastic process. Finally, it confirms the universal scaling behavior using random Clifford circuits via a phenomenological Markov process.

Abstract

The physics of information scrambling in quantum many-body systems is intimately related to thermalisation and emergence of chaos. However, its standard characterisation through bipartite entanglement or operator growth remains inherently coarse-grained, obscuring the spatiotemporal anatomy of how quantum information flows between different regions of the system and across various length scales. In this work, we develop a theory for the dynamics of local information in local random unitary circuits which resolves the fine-grained microscopic structures of local information flow in such systems. Using the framework of information lattice which systematises the information content at each length scale within each subsystem, we identify a distribution of length scales at which information resides and obtain the dynamics of the distribution. For Haar-random circuits with infinite local Hilbert-space dimensions, we map this dynamics onto an exact classical stochastic process which reveals that this distribution is described by a Tracy-Widom form which moves ballistically in time ($ \propto t$) toward larger scales, accompanied by a $\sim t^{1/3}$ broadening. We also find the same qualitative behaviour for random Clifford circuits acting on qubits. The similar scaling behaviour in two rather different settings hints strongly towards the universality of our results. In the case of Clifford circuits, we develop a phenomenological Markov process for the dynamics of the stabiliser generators in a specific gauge which confirms the Tracy-Widom distribution and the $t^{1/3}$ scaling of the fluctuations. Ultimately, our results establish the universal properties of the dynamics of local quantum information in a length scale-resolved fashion and provide a possible route towards bridging exact microscopic theories for information dynamics with emergent hydrodynamic descriptions of information flow.

Emptiness formation in the Lieb-Liniger gas: hydrodynamic instantons and a conjectured rate function

Authors: Boris A. Khanikati, Alexander G. Abanov
Type: theory · Category: quantum gases · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26640v1

Summary. This theoretical paper calculates the rate function governing the probability of finding a macroscopic empty region in a 1D Lieb-Liniger Bose gas. By combining advanced techniques from quantum field theory and hydrodynamics, the authors derive a conjectured rate function that successfully reproduces known limits and matches high-precision numerical simulations. This provides a powerful, unified description of rare quantum fluctuations in strongly interacting quantum gases.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep insights into non-equilibrium quantum dynamics and rare events in interacting quantum gases, connecting field-theoretic methods (Fredholm determinants) to macroscopic observables like vacuum decay rates.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the emptiness formation probability (EFP) in the ground state of the repulsive one-dimensional Lieb-Liniger Bose gas.

Main result. The authors derive a conjectured rate function $f(\gamma_0)$ for the EFP, which is shown to match known weak-coupling, strong-coupling, and numerical results for the Lieb-Liniger model.

Method. The analysis combines techniques from Fredholm determinants, dual-field representations, and variational minimization of the Euclidean hydrodynamic action.

Model / system. The system is the repulsive 1D Bose gas described by the Lieb-Liniger model, characterized by a repulsive delta-function interaction potential. The physics is analyzed in the ground state context.

Key observables. The Emptiness Formation Probability $P(R)$ for a macroscopic empty interval of length $2R$, and its asymptotic rate function $f(\gamma_0)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensionless interaction strength $\gamma_0 = c/ ho_0$, and the empty interval length $R$.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is the validity of a 'double-scaled determinant hypothesis' for the asymptotic evaluation, which the authors note remains unproven.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the numerical emptiness instantons, which are characterized by astroid-like vacuum regions, confirming the theoretical predictions.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from the initial Fredholm determinant representation of the EFP to a proposed double-scaled determinant hypothesis. It then relates this to the minimization of the hydrodynamic action, culminating in the derivation and verification of the rate function $f(\gamma_0)$ across different coupling regimes.

Abstract

We study the emptiness formation probability (EFP) in the ground state of the repulsive one-dimensional Lieb-Liniger Bose gas. For a macroscopic empty interval of length $2R$, its leading asymptotic behavior is described by a rate function $f(γ_0)$, defined by $-\log P(R)\sim(ρ_0R)^2f(γ_0)$, where $ρ_0$ is the mean density and $γ_0$ is the dimensionless interaction strength. We propose a parameter-free integral equation for $f(γ_0)$. Starting from the exact dual-field Fredholm-determinant representation of the EFP, we show how the conjectured kernel arises formally and identify the uniform asymptotic statement that remains to be proven for a rigorous derivation. The conjecture reproduces the Tonks-Girardeau and weak-coupling limits, as well as the first correction obtained independently in both limits. It also agrees at the few-percent level with numerical minimization of the Lieb-Liniger hydrodynamic action over more than four orders of magnitude in coupling. The numerical calculation yields the corresponding emptiness instantons and their astroid-like vacuum regions.

In every regime, the DSF is a broad continuum dominated by a bright band at its lower edge, with the intensity at the one-triplon energy $ω\simeq J_{AB}$ suppressed. We identify the spectrum as a deconfined two-spinon continuum of kink and antikink domain walls between the two degenerate singlet coverings. Closed-form spinon dispersions fix the continuum edges and track the dominant band across the zone in all three regimes, while an explicit finite-separation two-kink calculation in a constrained Hilbert space reproduces the measured intensity distribution. Our results provide a microscopic picture of fractionalized excitations in the dimerized sawtooth chain and are relevant to the recently discovered Ti$^{3+}$ kagome fluorides, where strongly anisotropic exchange interactions can generate sawtooth-chain building blocks.

Abstract

Pair-density waves (PDWs) are unconventional superconducting states beyond the BCS paradigm, but identifying them unambiguously in microscopic lattice models is generally challenging due to various competing orders. In particular, an exact realization of PDW in interacting models with topological bands remains elusive. Here we construct an interacting model on a twisted bilayer checkerboard lattice (TBCB) featuring topological flat bands, and rigorously show that an exact PDW ground state can be induced by quantum geometric nesting (QGN), owing to its momentum-space nonsymmorphic symmetry. We further prove that the superfluid stiffness of any nondegenerate QGN superconductor (including the PDW studied here and uniform superconductor) obeys the universal relation $D_s=2N_{\rm flat}ν(1-ν)m_{\rm pair}^{-1}$, where $N_{\rm flat}$, $ν$, and $m_{\rm pair}$ are the number of flat bands, flat band filling, and the two-particle effective mass, respectively. Our results reveal a rich interplay among PDW order, quantum geometry, and band topology.

Abstract

Quantum estimation theory serves as one of fundamental backbones of quantum Shannon information theory and was mathematically systematized by pioneers of quantum information science. Although its applied research saw limited progress for a long period, the 21st century has witnessed active discussions and numerous fruitful proposals regarding its applications. Notably, the contributions of Monras and Paris represent one of the most remarkable achievements addressing the realization problem of estimation bounds. This paper aims to demonstrate a practical direction for extending their original ideas. Next-generation quantum information mechanisms urgently demand guarantees of quantum advantage unattainable by classical theory, alongside strict real-time processing without delays. By leveraging the concept of generalized heterodyne detection proposed nearly half a century ago, we demonstrate that a scheme based on their results can effectively meet the stringent requirements of near-future information technologies.
