Information-theoretic formulation of the Traveling Salesman Problem

Authors: Enrico Maria Fenoaltea, Riccardo Piombo, Aurelio Patelli
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26339v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper reformulates the Traveling Salesman Problem using an information-theoretic maximum-entropy framework. It overcomes the challenge of enforcing global cycle constraints by introducing a global penalty term that 'tilts' the probability distribution. The resulting mean-field algorithm provides a computationally tractable method that yields near-optimal solutions for the TSP.

Why it may be interesting. While the problem is classical optimization, the methodology—reducing a hard combinatorial problem by coupling local statistical ensembles with global constraints via an effective field—is highly relevant to modeling complex many-body systems with hard constraints (e.g., lattice gauge theories or constrained quantum states).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the difficulty of formulating the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) within a probabilistic, information-theoretic framework due to the coexistence of local and hard global constraints.

Main result. The authors propose a method that successfully incorporates the global Hamiltonian cycle constraint by tilting a maximum-entropy ensemble, leading to an algorithm that converges to near-optimal tours in polynomial time.

Method. They employ the maximum-entropy principle on the probability distribution over graphs, deriving a mean-field approximation that couples local assignment constraints with a global cycle penalty term.

Model / system. The model is the TSP, viewed as finding the minimum-cost Hamiltonian cycle on a set of cities. The mathematical framework involves defining a probability distribution $P(G)$ over graphs $G$ using Lagrangian multipliers.

Key observables. The probability matrix $P_{ij}$ (edge occupancy), the global connectivity penalty $N_{ ext{conn}}(G)$, and the resulting candidate tour cost.

Important parameters / regimes. The parameters $eta$ (controlling energetic cost) and $\mu$ (controlling the global connectivity penalty) are crucial for tuning the solution regime.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation relies on a mean-field approximation, which neglects loop-induced correlations, simplifying the intractable global constraint into a manageable effective field.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically shows the transition from the assignment polytope (local constraints) to the Hamiltonian cycle region ($\Gamma$) by varying the penalty parameter $\mu$. Figure 3 illustrates the solution space in the $(eta, \mu)$ plane.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the information-theoretic formulation using maximum entropy. It then derives the mean-field approximation, which reduces the problem to solving self-consistent equations for edge occupancies. Finally, it details the numerical procedure for finding the tour and compares its performance against state-of-the-art heuristics.

Abstract

The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) asks for the shortest route to visit a set of cities exactly once. It combines a simple local rule - each city must be visited once - with a hard, global constraint- all cities must be traversed within a single cycle. We cast the problem within a probabilistic, information-theoretic framework. The coexistence of local and global constraints is precisely what makes the problem difficult to address in this framework: the local rule can be enforced through vertex-level constraints, whereas the global constraint cannot be captured by independent edge probabilities. We show that this obstacle can be overcome by defining a maximum-entropy probability distribution over graphs, in which edge costs and degree constraints generate an assignment-like ensemble, and a global term, describing the hard constraint, tilts this ensemble toward Hamiltonian cycles. To make the construction tractable, we derive a mean-field approximation in terms of edge occupancies and implement a differentiable cycle penalty that suppresses sub-tours. This leads to a self-consistent numerical procedure whose output is not only a candidate tour but also a probability matrix encoding competing edges and degenerate solutions. We test the method on synthetic ensembles and on TSPLIB instances. The algorithm converges to connected tours in polynomial time, matching the best-known solution in the majority of instances and remaining within a small relative gap otherwise. Beyond its competitive performance, the proposed framework offers a general approach for handling hard constraints while reducing hard combinatorial optimization problems to simpler ones.

Summary. This paper proposes a novel framework for ghost imaging recognition that jointly optimizes the physical speckle patterns and the parameters of a quantum circuit. By integrating a speckle consistency regularization term, the method overcomes the limitations of traditional quantum machine learning approaches at low sampling rates. The results show significant accuracy improvements over classical and existing quantum methods, validating its potential for practical quantum-enhanced imaging.

Why it may be interesting. It directly applies quantum computing concepts (variational circuits, entanglement structures) to a quantum optics imaging problem, demonstrating a practical pathway for quantum enhancement in measurement science.

Main problem. The core challenge is achieving high-fidelity image recognition using ghost imaging, particularly when data sampling rates are extremely low.

Main result. The proposed joint optimization framework significantly improves recognition accuracy (e.g., 90.1% on MNIST at 1.5625% sampling) compared to classical or traditional quantum machine learning baselines.

Method. The method jointly optimizes the classical speckle patterns (optical coding) and the parameters of a parameterized quantum circuit (quantum feature extraction) via a regularization mechanism.

Model / system. The system uses Ghost Imaging (GI) combined with a hardware-efficient variational quantum circuit. The quantum circuit employs a star-shaped entanglement structure and processes input bucket signals encoded via an angle embedding.

Important parameters / regimes. Ultra-low sampling rates (e.g., 1.5625%), quantum circuit parameters (Theta), and the regularization strength ($\lambda$).

Abstract

Ghost imaging enables nonlocal image reconstruction and exhibits strong robustness against interference, but achieving high-fidelity recognition at ultra-low sampling rates remains challenging. Quantum machine learning offers a novel approach for efficient feature extraction on noisy medium-scale quantum devices; however, existing methods generally suffer from low recognition accuracy and weak noise resistance. This paper proposes a ghost imaging recognition method based on the simultaneous optimization of speckle patterns and quantum network parameters. By leveraging the mathematical equivalence between classical convolution and speckle-object dot product operations in ghost imaging, a speckle consistency regularization mechanism is introduced to achieve end-to-end joint optimization of optical coding and quantum feature extractors. A parallel 8-qubit quantum circuit employing block coding and a star-shaped entanglement structure is designed to extract higher-order features from bucket signals. Simulation results on the MNIST and Fashion-MNIST datasets show that at an ultra-low sampling rate of 1.5625%, the proposed framework achieves recognition accuracies of 90.1% and 81.7%, respectively, representing a 2.6% improvement over classical convolutional neural networks and a maximum improvement of 14.2% over traditional hybrid quantum machine learning models. This method also exhibits strong robustness to quantum noise and has been validated on a real optical ghost imaging system, achieving an average recognition accuracy of 84.8%. These results confirm that the joint optimization of speckle patterns and quantum network parameters provides a reliable and practical solution for low-sampling ghost imaging recognition.

Summary. This work investigates the critical phenomena in systems of randomly coupled, extended scalar fields, treating the system as a generalization of a spin glass. Using replica theory and large-N limits, the authors derive scaling laws and determine the upper critical dimension for the transition. The results provide deep insights into how disorder governs phase transitions in extended quantum or classical field theories.

Why it may be interesting. The techniques used—replica methods and analyzing critical exponents in random media—are foundational to understanding complex many-body systems, which share mathematical parallels with quantum many-body physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper characterizes the critical behavior and universality classes of systems composed of a large number of spatially extended, randomly coupled scalar fields.

Main result. The analysis determines an upper critical dimension ($d_u$) for the system, finding $d_u=1$ for the Wigner case, and shows that the heat capacity exhibits a discontinuity at the critical point for $d>1$.

Method. The study employs the replica formalism combined with the saddle point method to evaluate the quenched-averaged free energy and analyze the stability of the replica-symmetric solution.

Model / system. The model consists of $N$ scalar fields coupled by a quenched random interaction, analogous to a spin glass where spins are promoted to fields. A specific, exactly solvable variant is the Spherical Model.

Key observables. Free energy density ($eta f$), critical heat capacity ($C( au)$), structure factor ($S(k)$), and the two-point correlation function.

Important parameters / regimes. Large number of fields ($N o \infty$), system size ($L o \infty$), and the reduced temperature parameter ($ au$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies heavily on the large-$N$ limit and the saddle point approximation applied to the replicated partition function.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general Gaussian theory, then analyzing the nonlinear case using the replica formalism, and finally examining the stability of the saddle point solution via the Hessian matrix.

Abstract

We study an assembly of $N$ scalar fields coupled via a quenched random interaction, equivalent to a spin glass whose spins are promoted to $d$-dimensional fields. When $N$ is large, the random coupling matrix produces a family of Gaussian universality classes whose critical exponents are determined by the behavior of the eigenvalue density near the spectral edge. For a Wigner matrix, the correlation length diverges at criticality but the susceptibility remains finite, and one-loop replica analysis supports an upper critical dimension of unity. In an exactly solvable spherical variant, the heat capacity jumps at the transition in $d>1$, and correlations remain pinned to their critical form throughout the glass phase, giving generic scale invariance despite the absence of a Goldstone mode.

Metallogenic quantum criticality: Fermi surface nucleation at transitions between gapped phases

Authors: Zhengyan Darius Shi
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27453v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical work introduces Metallogenic Quantum Critical Points (MGQCPs), where a Fermi surface unexpectedly emerges at a quantum phase transition between two gapped insulating states. By constructing effective field theories for these transitions, the authors show that the critical point exhibits non-Fermi liquid behavior, challenging established paradigms of quantum criticality.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of MGQCPs provides a powerful, non-standard framework for understanding critical points in strongly correlated electron systems, particularly those involving topological insulators and moire heterostructures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates a radical failure of standard quantum critical point theory, where an entire gapless Fermi surface can emerge at a quantum critical point separating two fully gapped insulating phases.

Main result. These exotic transitions, called Metallogenic Quantum Critical Points (MGQCPs), are characterized by a critical Fermi surface and exhibit non-Fermi liquid spectral function signatures, despite the neighboring phases being gapped.

Method. The authors construct controlled effective field theories by combining analyses of fermionic composite fermion fluctuations with critical bosonic field theories (like QED3) using parton constructions and mean-field theory.

Model / system. The minimal model is a quantum Hall bilayer system transitioning between two distinct Fractional Chern Insulators (FCIs). The physics is described by coupling fermionic partons to background gauge fields arising from the bosonic transition.

Key observables. Fermi surface existence, spectral function non-analyticity, resistivity scaling laws, and topological invariants (Hall conductivities, central charges).

Important parameters / regimes. Filling fractions ($ u_i$), background flux ($B$), and the nature of the Coulomb interaction (screened vs. unscreened).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on mean-field approximations for the bosonic phases and the use of parton constructions to decouple the low-energy physics.

Figures summary. Figures schematically illustrate the phase diagram showing the emergence of an NFL metal at the FCI transition, and diagrams detail the underlying QED3 sector and the role of gauge fluctuations.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by establishing the general principle, constructing the effective Lagrangian by coupling fermionic and bosonic sectors, analyzing the stability of pairing instabilities via RG flow, and finally confirming the MGQCP nature through specific FCI transitions.

Abstract

Continuous quantum phase transitions in interacting many-body systems exhibit universal phenomena that are largely independent of microscopic details. An organizing principle distilled from canonical examples such as the magnetic transition in Ising models is that the low-energy physics of transitions between gapped phases is governed by scale-invariant quantum field theories with gapless degrees of freedom at isolated points in momentum space. In this paper, we show that this principle can fail in a radical way: an entire gapless Fermi surface can emerge at a quantum critical point separating two fully gapped insulators. These exotic phase transitions are dubbed \textit{metallogenic quantum critical points} (MGQCP). We construct a controlled family of effective field theories for MGQCPs between distinct fractional Chern insulators and propose a periodically modulated quantum Hall bilayer as a minimal microscopic setting. The critical theory exhibits an unusual mixture of physical signatures: while transport and compressibility follow scaling laws compatible with a conformal field theory, the electron spectral function develops a power-law non-analyticity at the Fermi momentum, characteristic of non-Fermi-liquid metals. We discuss how the concept of MGQCP may shed new light on critical phenomena in moire materials with tunable Chern bands. More generally, our results point to a broader class of quantum critical phenomena in which extended manifolds of gapless excitations emerge in momentum space despite being absent in both neighboring phases.

Summary. This paper explores quantum sensing using three novel qutrit models derived from the OAT Hamiltonian. By analyzing the Quantum Fisher Information, the authors show these systems achieve near-Heisenberg scaling and, crucially, enable simultaneous, compatible estimation of multiple parameters—a feat difficult in qubit systems. This advances the field by demonstrating robust, high-precision sensing in multi-level atomic ensembles.

Why it may be interesting. This work extends quantum sensing theory to multi-level systems (qutrits), providing concrete, experimentally feasible models that surpass the limitations of qubit-only approaches, particularly regarding multiparameter estimation compatibility.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Investigating quantum-enhanced single and multiparameter metrology using qutrit ensembles, moving beyond standard qubit protocols.

Main result. The proposed qutrit models achieve near-Heisenberg scaling for sensing and demonstrate a novel mechanism for compatible multiparameter estimation using commuting generators from the su(3) algebra.

Method. The metrological utility is characterized using the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) toolbox, analyzing the scaling of the QFIM and the Uhlmann curvature.

Model / system. The system involves N three-level atoms (qutrits) whose dynamics are governed by generalizations of the One-Axis Twisting (OAT) model, specifically QOAT, BOAT, and QXY Hamiltonians, operating within the su(3) algebra.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Uhlmann Curvature, and the scaling of incompatibility measures (R).

Abstract

Quantum-enhanced sensing with atomic ensembles has predominantly focused on qubit-based protocols, despite the growing ability of many experimental platforms to coherently control and entangle multi-level systems. Here, we investigate quantum-enhanced sensing with qutrit ensembles by introducing three experimentally feasible qutrit generalisations of the one-axis twisting (OAT) model that involve entangling operations only between two levels, while the third level primarily acts as a spectator. We characterize the metrological utility of the dynamically generated states using the quantum Fisher information toolbox. We find numerically that all three models offer considerable freedom in encoding direction for quantum-enhanced sensing, with up to 6 out of 8 possible directions exhibit near-Heisenberg scaling after a short evolution time. We discuss experimental access to this enhanced metrological precision via effective time-reversal protocols. Furthermore, we examine the practical issues of estimation ambiguity and local dissipation, and show that they can be largely overcome by optimizing the sensor operating point. Finally, we show that the measurement incompatibility in estimating multiple parameters simultaneously encoded in different directions with near-Heisenberg scaling of precision is suppressed at the zero operating point as the system size increases. In the process, we find that one of the models enables near-Heisenberg scaling metrology with a pair of commuting generators, a possibility that arises from the $su(3)$ algebra and is thus absent in qubit-ensemble based sensors of collective $SU(2)$ rotations.

Dynamical phase selection controls compute scaling in looped transformers

Authors: Gunn Kim
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26556v1

Summary. This work treats looped transformers as dynamical systems, showing that their computational cost scaling is not fixed by their design but by the specific dynamical phase selected during training. By analyzing bifurcations, the authors derive universal power-law scaling laws for certain phases, demonstrating that the underlying physics of the solution dictates its computational limits.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between the stability analysis of a complex, high-dimensional dynamical system (the transformer) and a universal, low-dimensional critical scaling law ($N^{-2}$) provides a powerful, physics-based lens for understanding AI computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates what fundamentally governs the computational scaling of looped transformers, arguing it is determined by the network's underlying dynamical phase rather than just its architecture or training objective.

Main result. Distinct dynamical phases (like fold vs. Neimark-Sacker) can emerge for networks with identical structure, and the specific phase dictates the universal power-law scaling of test-time compute.

Method. The analysis uses dynamical systems theory, classifying network behavior based on bifurcations (saddle-node fold, Neimark-Sacker) of the weight-tied iterative map.

Model / system. The model is a looped transformer performing inference by iterating a weight-tied map, $z_{t+1} = F_ heta(z_t; h)$, which is analyzed as a dynamical system.

Key observables. The dynamical phase (fold vs. Neimark-Sacker), the relaxation time $ au(\epsilon)$, the spectral gap $\lambda_{\max}(-\epsilon)$, and the workload tail probability $P( au > N)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The control field $h$ (or $\epsilon$), which drives the system through bifurcations, and the initialization state of the network weights.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on local normal-form reductions near codimension-one bifurcations (fold and Neimark-Sacker crossings).

Figures summary. Figure 1 summarizes phase assignments across different conditions; Figure 2 shows the collapse of the cumulative distribution of relaxation times for fold-phase networks onto a universal power law; Figure 3 compares escape times for fold and Neimark-Sacker dynamics.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the looped transformer as a dynamical system, then analyzes the fixed-point stability and bifurcation mechanisms to classify the system into distinct phases, deriving universal scaling laws for the fold phase and showing their absence in the Neimark-Sacker phase.

Abstract

A looped transformer performs inference by iterating a weight-tied map, making its computation a dynamical process whose cost is set by the resulting inference dynamics. Here we show that networks with identical architecture and objective, trained to identical accuracy, nevertheless realize distinct dynamical phases depending strongly on initialization, and that the bifurcation defining each phase determines how test-time compute scales. The phases are distinguished by their bifurcation mechanisms, including a saddle-node fold and a Neimark-Sacker-type transition to bounded nonstationary motion. In the fold phase, a one-dimensional normal-form reduction predicts both the relaxation-time and spectral-gap amplitudes from local derivatives of the trained map, yielding the parameter-free relation $τ(\varepsilon)[1-λ_{\max}(-\varepsilon)]\toπ$. Composed with a regular distribution of problem difficulty, the same critical slowing down produces the workload-level tail $P(τ>N)\sim N^{-2}$. In the Neimark--Sacker phase, the fold scaling law disappears rather than merely changing its prefactor. Thus, test-time compute is not determined by architecture alone. It is governed by the dynamical phase of the solution found by training.

Optimal Gaussian networks for private distributed quantum sensing

Summary. This work establishes a general framework for designing Gaussian quantum networks for distributed sensing that guarantees perfect local privacy. By characterizing the required structure of the quantum state using the pairing matrix, the authors derive an optimal probe state based on TMSV states. This state achieves Heisenberg scaling for the collective parameter estimate while ensuring individual local parameters remain hidden, even under realistic noise like optical loss.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous, general framework for designing quantum sensors that are robust against information leakage, which is crucial for practical, multi-user quantum network applications.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To characterize Gaussian quantum networks that can estimate a collective parameter in a distributed setting while ensuring that no individual local parameter can be estimated independently (perfect local privacy).

Main result. The optimal private Gaussian probe state is constructed from Two-Mode Squeezed Vacuum (TMSV) states, achieving Heisenberg scaling in sensitivity while rigorously maintaining perfect local privacy.

Method. The authors analytically derive necessary and sufficient conditions on the pairing matrix of the Gaussian state imposed by the local privacy constraint, leading to a structural characterization of the optimal state.

Model / system. The system involves continuous-variable quantum states (Gaussian networks) used for distributed quantum sensing (DQS), where local phase shifts are encoded onto a probe state.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information Matrix (QFIM), Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the global phase estimate, and local MSEs for individual phases.

Important parameters / regimes. Photon number ($N_s$), number of sensing modes ($J$), and the weights ($w_j$) defining the collective parameter.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the use of local homodyne measurements and that the optimal sensitivity can be attained by a pure state; it also considers phase-independent quantum channels.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of private DQS, the graph structures of privacy-preserving vs. violating networks, and the comparison of the global phase MSE converging to the CRB while local MSEs remain near the random-guessing benchmark.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by defining the privacy condition mathematically using the QFIM, deriving the structural constraints on the Gaussian pairing matrix, identifying the elementary building blocks (TMSV states), and finally optimizing the probe state and measurement scheme to achieve the desired scaling.

Abstract

Private distributed quantum sensing aims to estimate an authorized collective parameter while preventing independent estimation of individual local parameters. Here, we analytically characterize Gaussian quantum networks satisfying this perfect local privacy condition. Using a graph representation of the Gaussian pairing matrix, we show that two-mode squeezed vacuum states constitute the essential building blocks of privacy-preserving Gaussian states. We then analytically derive the optimal sensitivity under perfect local privacy and determine a Gaussian probe that achieves Heisenberg scaling with respect to both the photon number and the number of sensing modes. Using local homodyne measurements and maximum-likelihood estimation, we numerically verify quantum-enhanced sensitivity beyond the shot-noise limit while preserving perfect local privacy. We further show that the local-privacy condition is preserved under arbitrary phase-independent quantum channels, including optical loss. Our results provide a general framework for constructing and optimizing privacy-preserving continuous-variable quantum sensing networks.

Quantizing the guiding center: do quantization and coarse graining commute?

Authors: Rodrigo Andrade e Silva, Ted Jacobson
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26277v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work addresses the fundamental question of whether quantizing an effective theory yields the same results as coarse-graining the full microscopic theory. Using a charged particle in a magnetic field, the authors find that while the theories match generally, the effective theory predicts unphysical quantization effects for closed magnetic paths. This serves as a cautionary example regarding the reliability of effective quantum descriptions.

Why it may be interesting. The core theme—the failure of quantization procedures to commute with coarse-graining—is a fundamental issue in effective field theories, relevant across quantum gravity, condensed matter, and quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the process of quantizing an effective classical theory commutes with the process of coarse-graining the underlying microscopic quantum theory.

Main result. While the quantized effective theory generally agrees with the microscopic theory, it predicts spurious spatial discreteness (quantization of 'radius') for closed magnetic contours, suggesting quantization of effective theories can yield nonphysical predictions.

Method. The authors use nonperturbative, group-theoretic quantization techniques and compare the results against the coarse-graining procedure applied to the full microscopic theory.

Model / system. The system modeled is a charged particle drifting in a two-dimensional magnetic field. The analysis uses the guiding center approximation, which simplifies the dynamics by ignoring small-scale gyro motion.

Key observables. Quantization of 'radius' (spatial discreteness), density of states (Landau levels), and the consistency of the matching prescription.

Important parameters / regimes. Magnetic field strength B(x), magnetic moment $\mu$, and the gyro scale (which sets the limit for spurious effects).

Assumptions / limitations. The guiding center approximation is valid when the gyro radius is much smaller than the magnetic field variation length scale. The analysis also assumes the existence of a matching prescription between the two quantum descriptions.

Paper structure. The paper develops the effective theory via the guiding center approximation, derives the reduced phase space dynamics, performs the nonperturbative quantization, and then rigorously compares this quantum result against the coarse-grained microscopic theory, highlighting discrepancies in specific geometric regimes.

Abstract

We develop a nonperturbative, group-theoretic quantization of the effective guiding center theory of a charged particle drifting in a magnetic field in two spatial dimensions, and compare the resulting quantum theory with a corresponding coarse graining of the underlying microscopic theory. In the classical effective theory, the small gyro motion is not resolved, while the motion of the center of the gyro orbit remains observable. The reduced phase space is the physical space itself, so quantization leads to noncommuting spatial coordinates, and the effective theory loses access to the metric structure of physical space, retaining only its area structure. By formulating a prescription to match between the microscopic and effective quantum theories, we find that the predictions of the quantized effective theory are generally consistent with those of the microscopic theory. However, for closed isomagnetic contours the effective theory predicts a quantization of ``radius'', a spatial discreteness absent from the microscopic theory. This illustrates that quantization of an effective theory may yield spurious nonperturbative predictions, even if that quantum theory shows no internal signs of breakdown.

Abstract

CePdAl is a heavy-fermion compound with a quasi-kagome structure, where geometrical frustration competes with the Kondo effect. Using a high-sensitivity magnetometer, we observe clear first-order metamagnetic transitions without magnetization plateaus in CePdAl at 80 mK, indicating a lifting of frustration through the suppression of the Kondo effect and spin flips of ordered moments under fields. An anomaly in the nonlinear magnetic susceptibility at 4.3 T suggests nondipolar correlations in the polarized paramagnetic state. Furthermore, we found no evidence of non-Fermi-liquid behavior in the high-field region, where the antiferromagnetic order is completely suppressed. These findings establish the essential thermodynamic constraints for understanding the field-induced spin-liquid state in CePdAl.

Abstract

Magnetic anisotropy defines the easy axis of a magnetic material and governs the spatial arrangement of its domains. To date, anisotropy engineering has focused on reorienting the easy axis or tuning the anisotropy energy, both of which demand substantial energy input. Here, we demonstrate that magnetic domain textures can be switched without reorienting the easy axis, as observed in a kagome ferromagnet EuTi3Bi4 crystal. Using low-temperature magnetic force microscopy, we observe that the preferred orientation of magnetic domains switches from the a-axis to the b-axis upon temperature variation, and that this switching can also be triggered by an out-of-plane magnetic-field reset. Magnetization measurements and density functional theory calculations confirm a robust c-axis easy magnetization, ruling out a conventional spin-reorientation transition. Instead, the texture switching is governed by the temperature dependence of the in-plane variation of the Magnetic anisotropy energy landscape, which arises from two competing interactions with different decay rates: single-ion anisotropy favors a-oriented spin components, while nearest-neighbor anisotropic exchange favors b-oriented ones. Furthermore, the critical switching temperature is substantially elevated in a mechanically exfoliated EuTi3Bi4 flake. Our findings establish that macroscopic magnetic textures can be effectively manipulated by tuning the competition between in-plane anisotropic interactions, without the energy cost of reorienting the easy axis.
