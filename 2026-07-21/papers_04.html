Programming with Quantum-Controlled Quantum Channels

Authors: Kengo Hirata, Takeshi Tsukada
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.15873v1

Summary. This paper addresses the ambiguity in defining quantum control flow by distinguishing between controlled operations and the quantum SWITCH. It introduces a novel quantum programming language enforced by a linear type system to correctly model the quantum SWITCH over arbitrary noisy channels. This provides a mathematically sound framework for writing complex quantum algorithms involving conditional logic.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous foundational tools for programming with general open quantum systems, which is crucial for simulating realistic physical processes involving dissipation or measurement.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper distinguishes between 'controlled operations' and the 'quantum SWITCH,' arguing they are fundamentally different control mechanisms in quantum computation.

Main result. It develops a novel, well-behaved quantum programming language with a linear type system capable of expressing the quantum SWITCH over general quantum channels.

Method. The authors use semantic analysis based on program transformations and develop both denotational and operational semantics to formalize the control flow structures.

Model / system. The model is abstract quantum computation using qubits, quantum channels (represented by CPTP maps/Kraus operators), and programming language constructs like quantum conditional branching ($ ext{qif}$).

Key observables. Well-definedness of control mechanisms; preservation of semantics under translation between formalisms.

Important parameters / regimes. Linearity constraints, the structure of Kraus decompositions, and the distinction between unitary vs. general channel operations.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that controlled operations and the quantum SWITCH must be treated as distinct control primitives to maintain mathematical rigor for arbitrary channels.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the definition of controlled operations, the syntactic dilation rules mapping constructs from $ ext{QUGCL}'$ to $ ext{QifUnitary}$, and the semantics of various control flow structures using Kraus operators.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical distinction between quantum control types, develops a formal language framework incorporating linearity constraints, and proves semantic preservation theorems by translating constructs between different formalisms (e.g., $ ext{Vac}$ to $ ext{QifUnitary}$).

Abstract

In contrast to a classical bit, which can only take the value $0$ or $1$, its quantum counterpart -- a qubit -- can exist in a superposition of $0$ and $1$. This is a superposition of data values, naturally raising the question of whether one can superpose not only data but also programs. For example, a particular superposition of programs, known as the quantum SWITCH, has attracted much attention, and its implementations and computational advantages have been studied extensively within the physics community. A naive way to control a program by a qubit is by means of a controlled operation. Given an operation $F$, this amounts to considering an operation that behaves as $F$ when the control qubit is $|1\rangle$, and as the identity operation when the control qubit is $|0\rangle$. This idea works well when $F$ is a unitary operation, but it is not well-defined for a general quantum channel. By contrast, the quantum SWITCH is free from the well-definedness issue. This contrast leads to the key insight of this paper: controlled operations and the quantum SWITCH should be regarded as different kinds of quantum control mechanisms. Building on this insight, we develop a novel quantum programming language with quantum control and measurement that can express the quantum SWITCH over quantum channels. Using a semantic analysis based on program transformations, we identify the source of the ill-behavedness of controlled operations as the \emph{correspondence problem}: a lack of coordination between the measurements performed in the then- and else-branches of quantum conditional branching. We address this problem with a linear type system that enforces alignment of the quantum operations used in the two branches, yielding a well-behaved language capable of expressing the quantum SWITCH.

Summary. This theoretical work examines how thermal fluctuations can induce quasi-long-range order in RVB states on the checkerboard lattice. By showing that decoherence suppresses destructive quantum interference, the system's correlations transition from an expected exponential decay to a power-law decay characteristic of dimer models. The paper rigorously proves the mathematical basis for this state using advanced techniques involving local projector operators.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of thermal order by disorder, where temperature drives a system into an ordered phase by suppressing quantum interference effects, is highly relevant for understanding exotic phases in strongly correlated electron systems like high-Tc superconductors.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates a novel mechanism for 'thermal Order by Disorder' within Resonating Valence Bond (RVB) states on the checkerboard lattice.

Main result. Thermal decoherence suppresses destructive quantum interference, causing singlet-singlet correlations to decay with a power law ($1/r^2$) rather than exponentially, indicating quasi-long-range order.

Method. The authors derive an effective local spin Hamiltonian and analyze correlation functions by comparing the pure RVB state (exponential decay) against the mixed state resulting from thermal decoherence (power-law decay).

Model / system. The system is a spin-1/2 model on the checkerboard lattice, modeled using Resonating Valence Bond (RVB) states and related to Quantum Dimer models. The Hamiltonian involves complex local projectors and twelve-spin interactions.

Key observables. Singlet–singlet correlation function $C(r)$ and dimer–dimer correlations; the diagnostic is the transition from exponential decay to power-law decay.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis focuses on the temperature regime $K \gg T \gg J$.

Assumptions / limitations. The conclusion regarding power-law decay hinges on the linear independence of Valence Bond (VB) states, which is established through a detailed patch expansion procedure assuming periodic boundary conditions.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate mappings between singlets and six-vertex configurations, show the action of plaquette operators, and detail the iterative process of proving linear independence by expanding patches on the lattice.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the Hamiltonian and RVB state to analyzing correlation functions in different regimes. It then dedicates significant effort to mathematically proving the linear independence of singlet coverings via a patch expansion procedure using local projector operators.

Abstract

We derive a local spin-1/2 Hamiltonian with a resonating valence bond ground state on the checkerboard lattice. The state is characterized by the exponential decay of singlet-singlet correlations, whereas dimer-dimer correlations decay with a power law in the corresponding Quantum Dimer model. This observation leads to a novel mechanism for thermal Order by Disorder whereby thermal decoherence suppresses destructive quantum interference between different contributions to the correlations in the ground state and results in a qualitatively different, quasi-long-range ordered mixed state.

Field-induced first order transitions and phase coexistence in the Kitaev quantum spin liquid candidate BaCo2(AsO4)2

Summary. This study experimentally investigates field-induced transitions in the Kitaev candidate BaCo2(AsO4)2. Using neutron scattering and spin transport measurements, the authors find evidence for first-order phase coexistence rather than a continuous quantum liquid evolution. They conclude that observed excitations are better explained by bound magnon pairs, challenging previous interpretations of anomalous thermal transport.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant because it uses advanced spectroscopic and transport techniques to test the boundaries between exotic quantum states (QSLs) and conventional magnetically ordered phases, providing crucial insights into strongly correlated magnetic materials dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates field-induced first-order magnetic transitions and phase coexistence within BaCo2(AsO4)2, a material proposed as a Kitaev quantum spin liquid candidate.

Main result. Neutron scattering and transport measurements reveal that the transitions are first-order, characterized by phase coexistence (UUD/FM), rather than continuous evolution toward a QSL state. Theoretical modeling suggests observed excitations arise from bound spin-flip pairs.

Method. The research combines inelastic neutron scattering to probe magnetic excitations with transport measurements like the Spin Seebeck effect and theoretical modeling of magnon interactions.

Model / system. The system is BaCo2(AsO4)2, an insulating material with Co2+ ions arranged on a honeycomb lattice. It exhibits multiple magnetic phases (double-zigzag, UUD, FM) whose transitions are studied under applied magnetic fields.

Key observables. Magnetic order parameters (UUD, FM), excitation spectra (spin waves, localized modes), thermal conductivity ($\kappa/T$), and the sign/magnitude of the Spin Seebeck coefficient.

Important parameters / regimes. Applied in-plane magnetic field ($B$) near critical fields like 0.15 T and 0.5 T; temperature ($T$); energy scales (meV).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that observed excitations can be modeled by bound spin-flip pairs rather than requiring a gapless spinon Fermi surface characteristic of a true QSL.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the magnetic phase diagram, field-dependent excitation spectra showing mode separation and collapse at transitions, and transport measurements (SMR/SSE) confirming hysteresis associated with first-order changes.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the complex magnetic phase diagram via neutron scattering to detailing evidence of first-order transitions through multiple probes (INS, SMR, SSE), culminating in theoretical modeling that reinterprets anomalous transport signals as bound excitations rather than QSL signatures.

Abstract

BaCo2(AsO4)2 (BCAO) is an insulating Kitaev quantum spin liquid candidate with a rich low-temperature phase diagram. Below 5 K, it exhibits double-zigzag magnetic order. Upon application of an in-plane magnetic field, the magnetic structure first transforms into an up-up-down (UUD) state near 0.12 T and then enters a fully spin-polarized ferromagnetic (FM) state near 0.5 T. In the narrow field regime close to the polarized phase, a finite residual thermal conductivity has been reported, suggesting a possible field-induced quantum spin liquid phase. Using neutron scattering, we show that the field-induced double-zigzag-to-UUD transition near 0.15 T is accompanied by the emergence of a cluster of localized UUD spin excitations that coexist with conventional spin waves. Upon further increasing field, BCAO undergoes a first-order UUD-to-FM transition with coexistence of UUD and FM phases, accompanied by a sharp response in the spin Seebeck coefficient. These results do not support a quantum spin liquid scenario near the UUD-to-FM critical field. Instead, modeling indicates that the broad excitations arise from bound spin-flip pairs, while a low-lying dispersive branch near the FM phase boundary carries the same sign of magnetization as the FM order. These excitations naturally account for the observed sign of the spin Seebeck response and are likely relevant to the thermal conductivity near the critical field.

How Topology Shapes the Phase Behavior of Polyelectrolytes

Summary. This work establishes a theory showing that polymer topology dictates the phase behavior of polyelectrolytes, enabling control over coacervation independent of simple chemical changes. By using RPA within a statistical mechanics framework, the authors demonstrate that compact architectures enhance liquid-liquid phase separation propensity. This provides a powerful design principle for charged macromolecular systems.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical polyelectrolyte physics, the rigorous application of mean-field approximations (RPA) to complex many-body systems and the development of topology-dependent interaction parameters share conceptual links with modeling correlations in quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a topology-specific theory for polyelectrolyte coacervation and understand how polymer architecture influences the phase behavior of charged macromolecules.

Main result. Polymer topology acts as a powerful design lever, allowing fine-tuning of phase separation propensity independently of molecular weight or net charge. More compact topologies exhibit a greater tendency for liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS).

Method. The study employs a minimal theory combining the Flory-Huggins free energy with an electrostatic correlation term derived within the Random Phase Approximation (RPA).

Model / system. The system consists of polyelectrolytes (charged macromolecules) forming simple or complex coacervates. Topologies studied include linear chains, stars, and dendrimers.

Key observables. Propensity for liquid-liquid phase separation (coacervation), effective $\chi$ parameter, charge–charge structure factor $S_{cc}(k)$, and salt partition coefficients.

Important parameters / regimes. Bjerrum length ($\lambda_B$), polymer topology parameters (e.g., number of arms $f$, generations $N_b$), molecular weight ($N$), and salt concentration.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the Random Phase Approximation (RPA) for electrostatic interactions and assumes Gaussian chain statistics for structure factor derivations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams showing coexistence boundaries as a function of $\lambda_B$ or salt concentration, comparing different topologies. Other figures show salt partitioning behavior and scaling laws related to topology.

Paper structure. The paper develops the free energy functional using RPA, applies it to model various polymer topologies (linear, star, dendrimer), calculates phase diagrams for simple/complex coacervation under varying salt conditions, and analyzes the scaling dependence of effective interactions on molecular weight.

Abstract

We develop a topology-specific theory of polyelectrolyte coacervation using the random phase approximation and apply it to both simple and complex coacervation. Our results for stars and dendrimers show that more compact chain topologies display a greater propensity for liquid-liquid phase separation, as a function of both Bjerrum length and salt concentration. For mixtures of different topologies, we demonstrate that differences in polymer topology alone are sufficient to drive multiphase coacervation of polyelectrolytes, which we rationalize in terms of an effective $χ$ parameter. Analysis of a simplified global phase diagram reveals that the propensity for such topology-driven phase separation is largest at a finite molecular weight. Overall, our results establish polymer topology as a powerful design lever for tuning the phase diagram of charged macromolecules independently of molecular weight, net charge, and monomer chemistry, since changes in topology enable fine-tuning of the effective charge density without altering these molecular characteristics.

Summary. The researchers demonstrated strong intervalley mixing between edge channels in a silicon MOSFET operating in the Quantum Hall regime. This finding establishes that the valley degree of freedom acts as an effective 'valley beam splitter' for quantum information processing. The results confirm this capability while simultaneously showing that spin-flip processes are strongly suppressed, making Si a promising platform.

Why it may be interesting. This work demonstrates how engineered valley coupling in a solid-state platform can mimic quantum beam-splitter operations, which is highly relevant for building scalable quantum interferometers.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates strong intervalley mixing between copropagating quantum Hall edge channels to establish a fundamental building block for compact silicon quantum Hall interferometers.

Main result. Strong intervalley mixing was observed with a transition probability near 1/2, while interchannel transport requiring spin-flip scattering showed negligible probability, confirming the utility of valley degrees of freedom in Si MOSFETs.

Method. Measurements were performed on a double-layer-gated Si MOSFET in the Quantum Hall regime using low-frequency lock-in techniques to measure conductance and extract transition probabilities via Landauer–Büttiker formalism.

Model / system. The physical system is a double-layer-gated Silicon Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor (Si MOSFET) operating under high magnetic fields in the Quantum Hall regime. The valley degree of freedom is exploited as an alternative to spin for channel manipulation.

Key observables. Transition probability ($T_v$) between valley edge channels, and transition probability ($T_s$) for spin-flip interchannel transport.

Important parameters / regimes. Bulk filling factor ($ u=2$), low temperature (280 mK), high magnetic field (12.92 T).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on accurately subtracting parasitic resistances from raw conductance measurements using the Landauer–Büttiker formalism.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the device setup, two-terminal conductance plateaus confirming filling factors ($ u=2$), and schematics detailing the geometry for measuring intervalley transitions ($T_v$) versus spin-flip transport ($T_s$).

Paper structure. The paper details the experimental setup and measurement techniques using gate voltages to tune edge channel populations, followed by quantitative analysis using Landauer–Büttiker equations to extract the transition probabilities $T_v$ and $T_s$.

Abstract

Copropagating quantum Hall edge channels provide a promising platform for compact electron interferometry and flying quantum states. In silicon, the valley degree of freedom offers a natural alternative to spin-resolved edge channels because spin-flip scattering is strongly suppressed by the weak spin-orbit interaction. Here, we investigate interchannel transitions between copropagating valley edge channels in a double-layer-gated silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor. With the bulk filling factor set to $ν=2$, two spin-polarized valley edge channels are brought into close proximity near a depleted side gate. We observe strong intervalley mixing, with a transition probability close to 1/2, indicating nearly complete equilibration between the two valley edge channels. In contrast, interchannel transport between edge channels with different spin orientations shows negligible transition probability, consistent with suppressed spin-flip scattering in silicon. These results demonstrate that intervalley coupling at a Si/SiO$_2$ interface can provide a beam-splitter-like operation for copropagating valley edge channels, establishing a key building block toward compact silicon quantum Hall interferometers.

Summary. This theoretical work analyzes the energy landscapes on sparse random graphs to determine if they possess a universal ultrametric organization. By comparing a kinetically derived metric with the ideal single-linkage ultrametric, the authors prove that this hierarchy emerges robustly when the energy differences are large. This suggests that local tree-like topology dictates the long-term dynamical structure of such complex systems.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of energy landscape organization into an ultrametric structure is highly relevant for understanding complex dynamics, relaxation pathways, and metastable states in disordered quantum systems or molecular folding processes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates whether the energy landscape organization on sparse random graphs exhibits universal ultrametricity in the limit of large energy spreads.

Main result. The kinetic metric between basins converges pointwise to the classical single-linkage ultrametric as the energy spread ($\Delta F$) tends to infinity, suggesting ultrametricity is a universal property for such systems.

Method. The authors use spectral decomposition on a transformed rate matrix (Mahalanobis metric) and prove limit theorems comparing this kinetic distance to the minimax/single-linkage distance.

Model / system. The system models energy landscapes defined by random free energies ($F_i$) assigned to vertices of sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs. Dynamics are governed by a continuous-time Markov process described by transition rates derived from these free energies.

Key observables. Degree of nontrivial ultrametricity, kinetic Mahalanobis metric $D(B_a, B_b)$, and the single-linkage ultrametric $ ho(0)(B_a, B_b)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Energy spread ($\Delta F$), Temperature ($T$), Graph connectivity (sparse/locally tree-like).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that for large $\Delta F$, the contribution from suboptimal paths is exponentially suppressed, allowing the kinetic metric to be dominated by the minimax path.

Figures summary. Not specified in notes.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the Markovian dynamics and constructing a computable symmetric metric (Chunk 2), to establishing the mathematical link between this kinetic metric and the single-linkage ultrametric via limit theorems (Chunks 1 & 3), culminating in a discussion of topological origins (Chunk 4).

Abstract

We investigate the ultrametric organization of energy landscapes defined on sparse random Erdős--Rényi graphs. Each graph vertex is assigned a random free energy from a uniform distribution over an interval of width $ΔF$, and the kinetics are modeled by a Markov process with Kramers transition rates. Using spectral decomposition of the rate matrix, we construct a kinetic Mahalanobis metric between basins of attraction. Computational experiments for graphs with $V=5000$ vertices and $E=5000$ edges show that the degree of nontrivial ultrametricity increases monotonically from $\approx42\%$ for $ΔF=10$ kJ/mol to $\approx96\%$ for $ΔF=1000$ kJ/mol. We prove a limit theorem: as $ΔF\to\infty$, the logarithmic asymptotics of this metric converge pointwise to the classical single-linkage ultrametric. For finite $ΔF$, corrections from suboptimal paths are exponentially suppressed with increasing $ΔF$, so that the metric becomes asymptotically ultrametric. Our results suggest that ultrametricity is a universal property of sparse, locally tree-like networks with rugged energy landscapes in the limit of large energy spreads.

Summary. This work proves that classical random access codes can violate a conjectured square-root bound governing Quantum Random Access Codes (QRACs). By embedding these classical structures into the QRAC model, the authors construct explicit counterexamples showing the violation holds across an entire open interval of success probabilities. This reveals that the separation stems from the inclusion of classical coding rate limitations within the general quantum framework.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether a conjectured square-root bound for Quantum Random Access Codes (QRACs) holds true.

Main result. Classical random access codes with private randomness violate this conjectured quantum bound, demonstrating that the classical coding rate is the source of separation from the theoretical limit.

Method. The authors embed known classical RAC constructions into the general QRAC model using specific encoding and measurement assumptions. They then apply established achievability theorems for classical codes to construct explicit counterexamples.

Model / system. The study centers on Quantum Random Access Codes (QRACs), which encode $n$ classical bits into $m$ qubits, aiming for a worst-case success probability $p$. The analysis compares the conjectured bound with bounds derived from information theory and classical coding limits.

Abstract

We consider whether every quantum random access code (QRAC) with density-operator encodings and arbitrary decoding measurements obeys the conjectured bound $p\leq(1+\sqrt{m/n})/2$, where $n$ classical bits are encoded into $m$ qubits and $p$ is the worst-case success probability. We find that classical random access codes with private randomness, which form a subclass of this QRAC model, violate the bound. We embed these classical codes as QRACs with diagonal encoding states and commuting decoding measurements, and construct pure-state realizations with identical decoding statistics. The achievability theorem of Ambainis, Nayak, Ta-Shma, and Vazirani then yields violations for every fixed $p\in(1/2,1)$ at sufficiently large input length. The counterexamples span the full open interval between the conjectured and Nayak bounds at each fixed compression rate. A finite-blocklength analysis further yields order-optimal logarithmic qubit scaling for a recovery bias scaling as $\sqrt{\log_2 n/n}$ with a sufficiently large prefactor. These results identify the classical coding rate as the source of the separation and motivate restricted bounds based on quantitative spectral properties of decoding measurements.

Abstract

As practical quantum networks approach large-scale deployment, the need for efficient user-to-user frequency allocation is increasing, yet current approaches only provide partial solutions to the routing and spectrum allocation problem for an arbitrary quantum network. We address this challenge for repeater-less flex-grid quantum networks based on hyperentangled photons using an efficient three-stage pipeline combining leading tools in classical networking with recent advances in numerical optimization. First, double instantiations of Yen's algorithm obtain low-loss route candidates between each pair of users and the entanglement sources. Second, the advanced process optimizer (APOPT) obtains frequency channel allocations that maximize distribution rates under fidelity constraints. Finally, the constraint programming solver using satisfiability methods (CP-SAT) assigns specific frequency bins to each link, ensuring that there is no contention between frequencies from different sources. We numerically demonstrate this approach on a representative ring network and a Manhattan incumbent local exchange carrier topology, realizing significant improvements over prior genetic algorithm approaches in speed, accuracy, and scalability. Overall, this pipeline provides an efficient heuristic workflow for optimizing broadband entanglement distribution, applicable to arbitrarily connected quantum networks integrated within the existing lightwave infrastructure.

Abstract

Quartet correlations in neutron-rich Te isotopes are investigated within the quartet Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) framework. Taking \(^{100}\)Sn as an inert core, we consider two valence protons and valence neutrons occupying the \(2d_{5/2}\oplus1g_{7/2}\) model space, and solve the quartet BCS variational equations with a charge-independent isovector pairing interaction. We discuss the consequences of the geometric inductive biases of both models, as well as how each model contains a different feature importance inductive bias. This short characterization offers an accessible route to quantum machine learning (ML) ideas for readers who have zero background in quantum and are only familiar with linear classification in ML. In support of ML pedagogy, we encourage instructors to utilize this piece to smoothly introduce quantum ML ideas into the undergraduate ML classroom.

Abstract

It is well-known that combinatorial circuits are modeled mathematically by string diagrams in monoidal categories. Given a gate set $Σ$, the circuits over $Σ$ can be thought of as string diagrams in the free monoidal category generated by $Σ$. In this model, circuit semantics are then given by monoidal functors out of this free category. For quantum circuits, this functor is often valued in the category of unitary matrices. This model suffices for concrete quantum circuits, but fails to describe parameterized families of quantum circuits, such as those which arise in the analysis of ansatz circuits. In this paper, we introduce an approach to parameterized circuit semantics, which is based on enriched category theory. We first introduce an abstract categorical construction, and use this to gain new insights on controlled operations and quantum communication. We then study the special cases of Cartesian monoidal parameters and monoidal closed parameters, both endowing the parameterized semantics with useful constructions.We conclude by showing that the monoidal closed case can be used to unify two perspectives on quantum control.

Abstract

Hybrid quantum-classical workflows are expected to underpin practical quantum computing applications, yet the quantum and HPC communities lack a shared framework for reasoning about where and when their integration requirements matter most. Such a framework must separate two distinct levels of analysis: the application level, where communication overhead affects runtime performance, and the real-time level, where it determines feasibility. To address this, we introduce a runtime model that decomposes workflow execution into quantum compute, classical compute, and communication costs. At the application level, a communication-to-computation ratio from this decomposition quantifies whether a workflow is communication-bound or compute-bound; at the real-time level, a feasibility constraint determines whether timing requirements can be met at all, with the reaction time setting the logical clock speed of fault-tolerant computation once they are. Application of this model to representative workflows demonstrates that co-location of quantum processors with HPC infrastructure offers negligible performance benefit for compute-intensive applications today, while tight integration remains crucial for real-time tasks such as quantum error correction needed for large scale quantum computations. However, we discuss how even at the application level these assessments may shift with hardware evolution, illustrating how the model can identify specific crossover conditions, and how, under fault tolerance, the reaction time can set application-level performance.
