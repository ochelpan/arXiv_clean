Quantum undular bores, rainbows, and event horizons in superfluid dam breaks

Authors: Liam M. Farrell, Wyatt Kirkby, Alex Harris, David Tyler, Maxim Olshanii, Duncan H. J. O'Dell
Type: theory · Category: quantum gases · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.32232v1

Summary. This paper studies the complex wave dynamics of a superfluid dam break in a BEC, treating it as a quantum analogue to classical hydrodynamic bores. By analyzing the system in different density regimes, the authors predict the formation of dispersive wavepackets, a 'silent band' analogous to a dark rainbow, and a self-induced sonic event horizon. The work connects quantum gas dynamics to fundamental concepts in general relativity and nonlinear wave theory.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly interesting as it bridges concepts from nonlinear wave physics (bore dynamics, caustics) directly into the realm of quantum many-body systems (BECs), providing a quantum analogue for phenomena usually associated with general relativity (event horizons).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the rich wave dynamics arising from the sudden removal of a potential barrier in a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC), modeling it as a quantum hydrodynamic dam break.

Main result. The dynamics exhibit features analogous to classical phenomena, including dispersive wavepackets resembling undular tidal bores, the formation of a 'silent band' analogous to a dark rainbow band, and the emergence of a self-induced sonic event horizon in the non-perturbative regime.

Method. The analysis uses the Gross-Pitaevskii equation (GPE) to study the quasi-one dimensional case in both perturbative and non-perturbative regimes, drawing parallels with established wave theories.

Model / system. The system is a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) modeled by the GPE, analyzing the dynamics across different initial density differences ($\Delta n$). The physics connects superfluid dynamics to concepts from general relativity, such as event horizons.

Key observables. Dispersive wavepackets (DWs), the 'silent band' density plateau, and the formation of a sonic horizon.

Important parameters / regimes. Initial density difference ($\Delta n$), the ratio of $\Delta n$ to $n_0$ (perturbative vs. non-perturbative regimes), and the characteristic length scale $\hbar^2 t$ for wave growth.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the GPE and makes approximations such as the local density approximation for analytic efforts, while the Thomas-Fermi approximation is used for the horizon calculation.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates three dam breaking classes (dry, wet, perturbative wet) showing the initial step profile and resulting dynamics. Figure 2 shows the sound cone formed in spacetime by outward propagating DWs.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the dam break problem by first establishing the governing equation (GPE), then analyzing the perturbative regime to find analytic solutions related to Airy functions and double rainbows, and finally examining the non-perturbative regime where a sonic horizon forms.

Abstract

The sudden removal of a potential barrier from a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) gives rise to a quantum version of a hydrodynamic dam break and leads to rich wave dynamics that have similarities to other localized defect problems such as domain wall dynamics in spin systems. Denoting $Δn$ as the initial difference in density between the upper $n_{1}$ and lower $n_{0}$ reservoirs on either side of the dam, we use the Gross-Pitaevskii equation to study the quasi-one dimensional case in both perturbative ($Δn \ll n_{0}$) and non-perturbative ($Δn \sim n_{1}$) regimes. In the perturbative regime a pair of outwardly propagating dispersive wavepackets forms which can be viewed as quantum versions of undular tidal bores that have analytic forms at long times in terms of the integrals of Airy functions with a wavelength that grows as $(\hbar^2 t)^{1/3}$. Airy functions are the universal wave functions that dress structurally stable fold caustics where pairs of rays coalesce, and, indeed, we show that the quantum dam break problem has the same ray structure as the naturally occurring caustic phenomenon of a double rainbow, including Alexander's dark band between the two bows where no light is scattered: we identify the intermediate density plateau in the dam break as an analogous `silent band' where only evanescent sound waves can exist. In the opposite regime of a non-perturbative dam break we show that a self-induced sonic horizon occurs when $Δn \geq (8/9)n_{1}$. A discussion of possible experimental schemes for amplification of quantum undulations is included as well as an alternative scheme for dispersive wave generation where a constant flow is imprinted on a BEC in box trap.

Simulation-Based Quantum System Inference with Neural Posterior Estimation

Summary. This paper presents a novel, likelihood-free framework for quantum system inference called Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). It uses neural normalizing flows to learn the posterior distribution of system parameters directly from classical simulation data. This approach significantly accelerates characterization efforts, enabling accurate parameter estimation for large-scale quantum devices by treating the inference problem as a fixed, upfront computational cost.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, general methodology for characterizing complex quantum hardware and processes by bypassing the need to compute intractable likelihoods, which is crucial for experimental quantum information science.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is performing quantum system parameter inference (the inverse problem) when the likelihood function is computationally intractable due to the exponentially large Hilbert space.

Main result. The proposed framework successfully characterizes and improves large-scale quantum systems by accurately estimating parameters from measurement data, even for complex noise models and large qubit counts.

Method. It introduces Simulation-Based Inference (SBI), a likelihood-free framework that pairs polynomial-cost classical simulators with Neural Posterior Estimation (NPE) using normalizing flows.

Model / system. The framework applies to general quantum systems, including circuits up to 81 qubits, Rydberg atom arrays, and processes characterized by a Hamiltonian, where parameters define the system's dynamics.

Key observables. Expectation values of observables (e.g., Pauli observables) collected from measurement ensembles.

Important parameters / regimes. System parameters ($ heta$) such as noise probabilities, Hamiltonian coefficients, or state parameters; the approach scales to 81 qubits and 735 latent parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes the availability of polynomial-cost classical simulators that can accurately approximate the quantum forward map, and the posterior estimation relies on the accuracy of the learned normalizing flow.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of the SBI framework, showing the training phase (sampling $ heta o x$) and the rapid inference phase using the learned posterior $q_\phi( heta|x)$.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the SBI framework, details the use of NPE with normalizing flows, and then demonstrates its versatility across multiple applications: Pauli noise learning, Quantum Error Mitigation, Quantum State Tomography, and Hamiltonian learning.

Abstract

Models of quantum systems faithfully map system parameters to observations, but the inverse problem of parameter inference from measurement data presents a fundamental challenge: computationally intractable likelihoods due to an exponentially large Hilbert space. Here, we introduce simulation-based quantum system inference, a unified, likelihood-free framework that learns parameter posteriors directly from classical simulation data. The central idea is to pair polynomial-cost classical simulators, such as Pauli propagation and tensor networks, with normalizing flows or other neural density estimators for accurate, reusable inference. A single model, trained once, maps any new measurement record to its posterior in one forward pass---turning per-experiment inference into a fixed, up-front cost. We numerically demonstrate the framework's versatility across Pauli noise learning, quantum error mitigation, quantum state tomography, and Hamiltonian learning, with examples involving 81-qubit shallow circuits and 735-parameter inference. In each case, the approach yields accurate estimates of identifiable parameters, while posterior uncertainty provides additional diagnostics of non-identifiability and indicates where further characterization is needed. Our framework reduces data-acquisition requirements in quantum experiments and accelerates parameter inference, providing a practical route to characterizing and improving large-scale quantum systems.

Summary. This work introduces a 'terminal-register certification' framework to reliably reconstruct complex quantum states, like those from the Ising model, using limited measurement data. By developing rigorous mathematical identities and finite-shot bounds, the authors demonstrate that the MERA ansatz provides a superior, verifiable method for capturing long-range entanglement compared to simpler tensor network or local circuit models.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous development of operational certificates that account for finite measurements is crucial for translating theoretical quantum simulation power into practical, verifiable quantum computation protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of learning structured quantum states from finite measurement data while ensuring the operational certificate remains meaningful despite measurement imperfections and finite sampling.

Main result. They establish a 'terminal-register certification' protocol, demonstrating that MERA provides a resource-qualified advantage over MPS and local circuits for reconstructing long-range quantum structure in the near-critical regime.

Method. The core method involves developing an exact identity relating reconstruction fidelity to the top state fidelity and the terminal measurement probability, coupled with finite-shot confidence bounds.

Model / system. The study focuses on pure one-dimensional quantum states, specifically using the inverse binary MERA ansatz to represent the ground state of the open-boundary transverse field Ising model.

Key observables. Reconstruction fidelity (F), conditional top state fidelity (F_top), long-range connected-ZZ error, half-chain-entropy error, and energy density error.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of qubits (e.g., $n=8, n=16$), the number of simulated training measurements ($560$ million), and confidence parameters ($\delta_D, \delta_T$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the causal-discard condition and assumes a calibrated total variation implementation budget is applied to the finite shot certificate to maintain soundness.

Figures summary. The notes summarize comparisons across multiple diagnostic tests, including held-out performance metrics (Table 8 & 10) and paired difference analyses ($\Delta F, \Delta ELR$) comparing MERA against MPS and local circuits (Table 9 & 11).

Paper structure. The paper builds from establishing the fundamental identity for reconstruction fidelity, deriving finite-shot confidence bounds for both the global failure event and the top state fidelity, and finally applying this framework to compare MERA against competing architectures (MPS, local circuits) on the TFIM ground state.

Abstract

Structured quantum-state learning not only depends on an expressive ansatz but also on an operational certificate that stays meaningful with finite measurements and imperfect implementation. We study pure one dimensional states learning by an inverse binary multiscale entanglement renormalization ansatz (MERA). In the learning procedure, the qubits removed during coarse graining are controlled coherently and measured together at the terminal register. We confirm that an ideal sequential and terminal measurement schedule delivers the same complete bit string distribution under matched causal operations, while normalized postselection can amplify perturbations inversely with prefix acceptance. A noise aware theorem introduces an individual calibrated total variation implementation budget to the finite shot certificate. The protocol is estimated on an open boundary transverse field Ising ground state. A frozen 8-qubit schedule using $560$ million simulated training measurements per run achieves fidelity above $0.99$ in all $60$ held-out runs, with a mean fidelity of $0.996886$. 1080 circuit-noise cells and 6480 confidence-coverage rows are covered by fixed-circuit robustness validation without a locked soundness violation. We then address architectural fairness at $n=16$ using three new studies. In a 120-run exact-gradient multistart diagnostic, MERA has higher fidelity in 58/60 paired restarts and lower long-range error in 60/60, although no run met the prespecified stationarity criterion. Finally, a causal cone-complete, parameter matched local circuit achieves $2.62\times$ greater aggregate gate exposure yet loses all 30 paired comparisons in fidelity, long-range error, energy, and entropy.

Attraction-controlled torque organization and rotational states in frictional granular matter

Summary. This work numerically explores how short-range attraction modifies the mechanical behavior of frictional granular matter. The key finding is that attraction's influence is not merely additive stress but fundamentally reorganizes the frictional contact network. This reorganization dictates macroscopic rheological states, such as the emergence of an adhesive plateau and a low-density stress-collapse regime.

Why it may be interesting. The study's focus on non-equilibrium steady states, jamming transitions, and the role of local structural correlations (like torque sign correlation) in determining macroscopic rheology shares conceptual links with understanding phase transitions in complex, interacting many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how interparticle attraction influences stress transmission, torque organization, and particle rotation in a 2D frictional granular material.

Main result. Attraction controls macroscopic rheology by reorganizing the frictional contact network, leading to an adhesive stress plateau, selecting a high-stress state, and creating a distinct stress-collapse regime.

Method. Numerical simulations (LAMMPS) were used to model the system under applied shear rates, analyzing stress decomposition and contact network statistics.

Model / system. The system is a two-dimensional frictional granular material, modeled as a binary mixture of particles subjected to normal, tangential, and attractive central forces.

Key observables. Shear stress ($\sigma_{xy}$), coordination number ($z$), torque amplitude ($ au_{rms}$), mean spin ($\Omega$), and torque-sign correlation ($C_ au$).

Important parameters / regimes. Attraction strength ($f_c$), packing fraction ($\phi$), and shear rate ($\dot{\gamma}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis distinguishes between the direct attractive virial stress and the stress modification arising from the reorganization of the frictional contact network.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate flow curves ($\sigma_{xy}$ vs. $\dot{\gamma}$) showing different rheological branches, and state maps showing the dependence of rotational statistics ($\Omega$ and $C_ au$) on shear rate and packing fraction.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the rheological response across various flow states (low-rate, high-rate, plateau, collapse) by decomposing the shear stress and examining structural metrics like coordination number and torque correlations.

Abstract

In this study, we numerically investigate how interparticle attraction affects stress transmission, torque organization, and particle rotation in a two-dimensional frictional granular material. Although the attraction is a central force and exerts no direct torque on the particles, it strongly modifies the contact network and particle rotation. Attraction produces a low-rate stress state and selects the high-stress state near the shear-thickening regime of the dry system, while leaving the high-rate thickened state almost unchanged. By decomposing the shear stress, we find that the direct attractive contribution accounts for only a fraction of the total stress, while the repulsive contribution is strongly enhanced. Attraction increases the coordination number and torque amplitude and can reverse the local torque-sign correlation. At low packing fractions and intermediate shear rates, a stress-collapse state emerges with strongly suppressed torque and rotation despite a finite contact network. These results show that attraction controls macroscopic rheology through reorganization of the frictional contact network.

Summary. This work analyzes particle transport in the Symmetric Exclusion Process on disordered lattices mimicking percolation clusters. It finds that the system's dynamics are characterized by a power-law decay in the return probability, with the decay exponent continuously depending on particle density. This highlights how topological disorder induces strong, density-dependent dynamical heterogeneity.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between topological disorder (percolation) and non-equilibrium dynamics (SEP) is highly relevant to understanding transport in disordered quantum systems, such as those modeled by disordered spin glasses or quantum impurities.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the waiting-time distribution of hard-core interacting particles undergoing the Symmetric Exclusion Process (SEP) on complex, disordered geometries like percolation clusters.

Main result. The system exhibits strong dynamical heterogeneity, leading to a power-law decay of the return probability $P_{ret}(t) \sim t^{-\omega}$, where the exponent $\omega$ varies continuously with particle density $ ho$.

Method. The authors combine numerical simulations with approximate analytical treatments, modeling the system as a backbone with attached side-branch traps.

Model / system. The model is the SEP on a lattice structure derived from a supercritical percolation cluster, simplified initially by a 'carpet' geometry. The dynamics are governed by hard-core interactions, leading to trapping in side branches.

Key observables. Waiting time distribution ($\psi( au_w)$), return probability decay exponent ($\omega$), and dynamical heterogeneity peaks in $\log t_w$.

Important parameters / regimes. Particle density ($ ho$), percolation bond probability ($p$), and the correlation length ($\xi$) related to the cluster structure.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis often relies on approximating the complex cluster dynamics by considering contributions from individual side branches (single-branch approximation), though multi-branch models improve accuracy.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate representative spanning clusters, schematic diagrams of the carpet/comb model, and plots showing the power-law decay of survival probabilities and waiting time histograms.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from studying the SEP on simplified geometries (carpet) to developing analytical tools based on eigenvalue expansions for single branches, culminating in the application to full, irregular percolation clusters to derive the density-dependent exponent $\omega$.

Abstract

We study the waiting-time distribution of hard-core interacting particles in the symmetric exclusion process on one- and two-dimensional lattices with side branches attached to each lattice site in the steady state. We use numerical simulations together with an approximate analytical treatment of particles trapped in side branches in the steady state. Such a two-dimensional carpet serves as a simplified model for trapping of a supercritical percolation cluster. At high particle densities, the system exhibits strong dynamical heterogeneity, with the distribution of logarithms of waiting times developing well-separated peaks corresponding to particles trapped at different depths from the backbone. We show that the probability that a tagged particle occupies the same position at time $t_0+t$ as at time $t_0$ decays algebraically as $t^{-ω}$, where the exponent $ω$ varies continuously with particle density for densities above a threshold value $ρ^*<1$. We also investigate the correlations between successive waiting times along the trajectory of a tagged particle.

Summary. This work introduces the correlation index, a general measure quantifying correlations in quantum and classical statistical systems. It applies this index to model complex phenomena in Bose-Einstein condensates, superconductors, and spin lattices. The resulting tool offers a powerful, unified diagnostic for studying correlation strength across equilibrium and non-equilibrium regimes.

Why it may be interesting. The index provides a unified, mathematically rigorous diagnostic tool applicable across quantum many-body physics, linking concepts like entanglement entropy to measurable correlation strengths in systems ranging from superfluids to magnetic materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a general, quantitative measure called the 'correlation index' ($\kappa$) to quantify the degree of correlations in diverse statistical systems.

Main result. The index successfully measures entanglement in quantum states and provides quantitative diagnostics for correlations in both equilibrium (e.g., BEC, superconductors) and non-equilibrium systems.

Method. The index is defined using the ratio of operator norms: $\kappa(\hat{A}) \equiv \log rac{||\hat{A}||}{||\hat{A}^{\otimes}||}$, applied to reduced density matrices ($\hat{ ho}_2$) derived via partial tracing.

Model / system. The theory is applied across multiple systems, including Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), superconducting systems (BCS model), and general spin systems, covering both equilibrium and non-equilibrium dynamics.

Key observables. Correlation Index ($\kappa(\hat{A})$), Reduced Density Matrices ($\hat{ ho}_2$), Von Neumann Entanglement Entropy ($S_E$), Condensate fraction ($n_0$).

Important parameters / regimes. Number of particles ($N$), Coupling parameter ($g$), Time ($t$), and the nature of the correlation (e.g., normal vs. anomalous averages in superconductors).

Assumptions / limitations. Approximations include the Hartree-Fock-Bogolubov (HFB) approximation for superconductors and the use of partial traces to simplify density operators.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how $\kappa(\hat{ ho}_2)$ changes with system size ($N$) or coupling strength ($g$) for BECs and superconductors, and how time-dependent correlations evolve in spin systems.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the general definition of the correlation index, then applies it to equilibrium systems like BECs and superconductors, and finally extends the analysis to non-equilibrium dynamics in spin and pseudospin systems.

Abstract

A measure of correlations for statistical systems is advanced, called correlation index. The measure is general, being applicable to quantum and classical, equilibrium and nonequilibrium statistical systems. After formulating the general notion of correlation indices for arbitrary trace-class operators, the calculation of the correlation indices are illustrated by several examples of density operators and reduced density matrices. The computation of the correlation indices for equilibrium systems is exemplified for bosonic systems with the Bose-Einstein condensate, superconducting systems, and for spin systems. Correlation indices for nonequilibrium systems are considered for nonlinear dynamics of spin and pseudospin systems.

Summary. This paper presents an efficient simulation framework for quantum systems that combine discrete qubits and continuous bosonic modes. By utilizing Linear Combination of Gaussians (LCoG) decomposition, the authors overcome the exponential scaling limitations of simulating these hybrid systems. This tool allows for the analysis of complex quantum algorithms and state generation protocols in various physical platforms.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial computational tool for analyzing complex quantum algorithms and quantum simulation protocols in modern hybrid quantum hardware architectures, bridging theoretical formalism with practical simulation limits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The classical simulation of hybrid quantum systems combining discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable (CV) subsystems is computationally intractable due to exponential cost increases.

Main result. The authors introduce a simulation framework using Linear Combination of Gaussians (LCoG) decomposition that scales polynomially with the number of modes, avoiding Fock-space truncation.

Method. The framework decomposes the hybrid density matrix into blocks in a DV basis and represents each block using an LCoG expansion, deriving analytic rules for controlled Gaussian operations.

Model / system. The method applies to hybrid quantum systems encompassing both qubit (DV) and bosonic modes (CV), relevant to platforms like cavity-QED and photonic circuits.

Key observables. The Wigner function of the density matrix, $\hat{ ho}$, and its representation as a linear combination of Gaussian functions.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of bosonic modes ($M$), the number of qubits ($N$), and the number of Gaussian terms ($L$).

Assumptions / limitations. The state can be accurately represented as a linear combination of Gaussian states (LCoG).

Paper structure. The paper first reviews the necessary formalism (Wigner function, Gaussian states, LCoG) before deriving the transformation rules for Gaussian and controlled Gaussian operations acting on the hybrid LCoG state representation.

Abstract

Hybrid quantum systems combining discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable (CV) subsystems arise across a broad range of physical platforms and enable quantum information processing beyond purely qubit-based designs. Their classical simulation, however, is challenging since computational costs of both CV and DV systems exponentially increase. In this paper, we introduce a simulation framework for CV--DV hybrid systems based on linear combination of Gaussian (LCoG) decomposition. We decompose the hybrid density matrix into blocks in a DV basis and represent each block as an LCoG expansion. Within the representation, Gaussian operations on the bosonic subsystem independently act on each Gaussian function. We derive analytic formulas describing how complex Gaussian functions transform under controlled Gaussian operations. We further extend the framework to controlled Gaussian channels with state-dependent Gaussian noise. The method avoids a Fock-space truncation for the CV subsystem, and the cost of updating each Gaussian term scales polynomially with the number of modes. The framework provides a general computational tool for analyzing hybrid quantum algorithms, quantum simulation protocols, and non-Gaussian state generation schemes. As an example, we simulate Gottesman--Kitaev--Preskill (GKP) state generation based on a cavity-QED system.

Summary. This work establishes a rigorous, finite-time bound on the information rate achievable by a quantum neuron subject to environmental dephasing and repeated measurements. The key finding is that while quantum coherence can modify the measurement record, the ultimate operational information throughput is limited by a classical process. This separates the effects of noise-enhanced activation from true information capacity.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a hardware-aware benchmark for quantum neural processing, quantifying the fundamental limit on information extraction from open quantum systems, which is crucial for understanding decoherence effects in quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine the operational information throughput (Fisher information rate) of a quantum neuron, distinguishing it from stationary measures, especially under repeated projective readouts.

Main result. The optimal information rate bound is $16 u/27$, which is saturated by a classical two-state jump process, indicating that quantum coherence does not enhance the maximum achievable throughput.

Method. The authors derive the exact local Fisher information rate for the complete measurement record, optimizing over readout intervals and retained amplitudes ($s$).

Model / system. The system is a single-qubit quantum neuron modeled by a Lindblad master equation incorporating partial-reset updates ($ u$) and dephasing ($\gamma$).

Key observables. Fisher information rate ($R(\Delta)$), stationary activation ($\sigma^*(a)$), and susceptibility ($\chi(\omega)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Update rate ($ u$), dephasing rate ($\gamma$), retained amplitude ($s$), and readout interval ($\Delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis considers the complete measurement record and assumes the optimal bound is classical, even if coherence modifies the record at fixed cadences.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of the neuron, the stationary response vs. dephasing rate, and the dependence of the information rate on the readout interval ($\Delta$) and retention parameter ($s$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the system dynamics via the Lindblad equation to deriving the time-normalized information rate bound, optimizing this bound over parameters like $s$ and $\Delta$, and finally comparing the quantum bound to classical saturation limits.

Abstract

Dephasing can sharpen the activation of a dissipative quantum neuron, but a stationary response does not determine how much input information its output delivers per unit time. For a single qubit with partial-reset updates at rate $ν$, we derive the threshold response bandwidth and the exact local Fisher information rate of the complete record from repeated projective readouts, including their backaction. Near the activation threshold, the rate obeys $\mathcal R\leqν(1-s)(1+s)^2/2\leq16ν/27$, where $s$ is the retained amplitude of each update. For every fixed finite Markovian dephasing rate, optimization over the readout interval and $s$ gives the same supremum, approached at $s=1/3$ with arbitrarily rapid ideal readout. A finite measurement dead time instead selects a finite optimal interval, which we obtain by a dimensionless two-parameter optimization. The ideal bound is saturated by a classical two-state jump process: quantum coherence changes finite-cadence performance but cannot raise the optimized bound. These results separate noise-enhanced activation from the operational information throughput of a quantum neuron and provide a hardware-aware benchmark for finite-time quantum neural processing.

Gauge freedom and efficient algorithms for Lindbladian learning

Authors: Steven T Flammia, Savar D Sinha, Yu Tong
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.35265v1

Summary. This paper tackles the difficult problem of extracting the true dynamics (Lindbladian) of an open quantum system from noisy measurements corrupted by SPAM noise. It mathematically proves that only certain 'gauge-invariant' parts of the dynamics are fundamentally measurable. The authors then develop efficient, resource-bounded quantum algorithms to estimate these robustly measurable parameters using only single-qubit operations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, resource-aware framework for characterizing open quantum systems from noisy experimental data, directly impacting the feasibility of quantum process tomography and quantum control.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of learning the parameters of a local Lindbladian describing open quantum system dynamics when the process is corrupted by State-Preparation-and-Measurement (SPAM) noise.

Main result. It provides a sharp characterization showing that only universally gauge-invariant components of the Lindbladian are fundamentally learnable, while others suffer from an irreducible uncertainty floor due to SPAM noise.

Method. The authors employ gauge transformation theory to classify Lindbladian components and develop specialized, SPAM-robust quantum estimation algorithms restricted to single-qubit operations.

Model / system. The system is an open quantum system whose dynamics are governed by a local Lindbladian $\mathcal{L}$, which is characterized by Hamiltonian coefficients and dissipative Kossakowski matrix elements. The learning is performed experimentally using only trusted single-qubit control.

Key observables. Lindbladian coefficients (Hamiltonian $h_c$ and dissipative $\alpha_{ab}$), and the ability to distinguish between universally gauge-invariant and generically gauge-dependent components.

Important parameters / regimes. The required experimental resources scale as $\widetilde{\mathcal O}(\epsilon^{-2}\log(N/\delta))$ for learnable components, where $\epsilon$ is the desired precision and $N$ is the system size.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of trusted single-qubit control and models the noise as SPAM noise, leading to the classification of learnable vs. unlearnable parameters.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first establishing the theoretical obstruction caused by SPAM noise, then using gauge theory to classify Lindbladian components into invariant and dependent sets, and finally constructing efficient, resource-bounded algorithms for the invariant components.

Abstract

We study the problem of learning a local Lindbladian in the presence of state-preparation-and-measurement (SPAM) noise. Although recent work has developed scalable learning algorithms under idealized access assumptions, SPAM can make distinct Lindbladians experimentally indistinguishable, thus making part of the Lindbladian fundamentally unlearnable. We give a sharp characterization of this obstruction for bounded-degree local Lindbladians. We identify a family of locality-preserving gauge transformations that commutes with trusted single-qubit control, and use it to classify Lindbladian components. Paper structure. The paper establishes the measurement of intrinsic dimension for VMC, tests this metric on frustrated magnets, compares linear vs. nonlinear subspace training methods, and analyzes how the required dimension changes across quantum phase transitions.

Abstract

How many directions in weight space does training need? The intrinsic dimension answers this with the smallest number of random directions in which training still reaches a target accuracy, and small values have motivated parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA. We measure it for variational Monte Carlo (VMC), which trains a neural network to represent the ground state of a quantum many-body system. VMC is a demanding test, because the network generates its own training samples and every gradient is noisy, and a revealing one, because the exact answer is known and every run can be scored. We train only a small latent vector that a frozen random map turns into the network's weights, with no change to the standard natural-gradient optimizer. We find that a small dimension can be misleading, while the stability it brings is real. On a magnet with a hard sign pattern, a network that cannot represent signs reaches its best energy in 8 of 28,642 directions, but only because no such network can go lower; once signs are learnable, neither the signs nor the magnitudes are cheap. The dimension rises across a quantum phase transition, so it tracks how difficult a state is at far less compute than fitting a scaling law, yet it never falls below a floor set by the random subspace itself, even where the ground state is nearly trivial. Training in the subspace, in contrast, never diverged in our experiments, whereas full-parameter training with the same settings did, and a control with matched solvers attributes the difference to the reduced dimension.

Summary. This paper introduces Backward Kolmogorov Transport (BKT), a novel method to sample the invariant distribution of a reversible diffusion process solely from observed trajectories. Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of constructing accurate samplers for the invariant law of a reversible diffusion process when only noisy trajectory data is available, overcoming limitations inherent in density or score estimation.

Main result. The proposed Backward Kolmogorov Transport (BKT) method provides a theoretically sound and numerically superior way to sample the invariant law, showing error cancellation and convergence rates comparable to independent sampling when spectral information is accurate.

Method. BKT utilizes the backward Kolmogorov equation, propagating the ratio between the process law and the invariant law using estimated eigenpairs of the generator, defining a non-interacting transport map in the Wasserstein gradient flow.

Model / system. The study focuses on reversible diffusion processes, exemplified by molecular systems like the alanine dipeptide and multi-well potentials, whose invariant law is assumed to be known or approximated.

Abstract

We consider a reversible diffusion with unknown drift, observed through trajectories, and construct samplers of its invariant law. The trajectories contain samples of this law, but a sampler built from them by density or score estimation inherits the errors of these estimates, in particular on the barriers between metastable states. The trajectories also contain the dynamics, and for a reversible diffusion the ratio between the law of the process and its invariant law solves the backward Kolmogorov equation, so that the eigenpairs of the generator propagate it in closed form. Backward Kolmogorov transport (BKT) evaluates this ratio with eigenpairs estimated from the trajectories and coefficients computed once from the initial particles, and moves the particles along the Wasserstein gradient flow of the Kullback-Leibler divergence. The particles do not interact, BKT defines a transport map, and no density, score or drift is estimated. With exact eigenpairs BKT transports the spectral projection of the initial law exactly when this projection is positive. For estimated eigenpairs we prove a transport identity in which truncation enters only through the initial law and estimation only through the residual of the eigenpairs, and a local stability bound in the 2-Wasserstein distance. With coefficients computed from the transported particles, the sampling error of the retained modes cancels, and a central limit theorem identifies the asymptotic variance. Experiments on Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, multi-well potentials, separable products and alanine dipeptide, including comparisons with particle methods based on density estimates, agree with these results, and with an accurate spectrum the errors of BKT reach or fall below those of independent samples.

Summary. This paper introduces an information-theoretic measure of privacy leakage for multi-tenant quantum processors, arguing that coherence, not average error, dictates security. Paper structure. The paper establishes the information-theoretic definition of leakage, derives bounds relating it to conditional spread ($\Gamma$), analyzes the failure of error rate metrics, and concludes with experimental demonstrations on superconducting hardware showing the efficacy of the proposed privacy measures and defenses.

Abstract

Multi-tenant computing enables providers of commercial cloud quantum processors to rent disjoint sectors of a device to independent users. Average gate error, which cloud quantum computing providers report, does not determine how much one tenant learns about another. We propose an information-theoretic notion of information leakage across co-tenancy boundaries stemming from quantum state distinguishability. We measure this leakage on commercially-available 156-qubit (IBM Kingston) and 20-qubit (IQM Garnet) devices. Boundaries with identical benchmarked error can offer significantly different amount of information leakage because standard reported measures of error are blind to coherent-versus-stochastic nature of the error while the proposed notion of information leakage is not. A uniform Pauli randomisation implemented over the victim's whole register is used as a defence mechanism to reduce the information leakage to zero. The defence theoretically does not incur a fidelity cost, but the experiments show a non-trivial degradation caused by accumulation of errors. We provide a specific call-for-action to the providers of quantum cloud computing to report information leakage in addition to standard error rates in their device datasheet to enable users to compute privacy and security risks prior to engagement with the device.
