Abstract

We numerically study laser deceleration, cooling and trapping of host fast ortho-positronium (Ps) bunches as produced in silica microchannel targets using positron bunches from a Surko trap. While Doppler laser cooling routinely allows to cool neutral atom species below milli-Kelvin kinetic temperatures, the fast ground state annihilation and the high recoil shift to line-width ratio in Ps pose new challenging limitations on timing and the required laser power. Earlier theoretical work and first experiments already exhibit viable prospects for radiation pressure cooling to create unprecedented cold and dense Ps ensembles. Our extended numerical studies confirm very good prospects to simultaneously decelerate, cool and optically trap Ps atoms in a standard Doppler cooling geometry on the $1^3\mathrm{S}$--$2^3\mathrm{P}$ transition. Including the full Zeeman state manifolds and adding additional transverse lasers on the $2^3\mathrm{P}$--$3^3\mathrm{D}$ transition improves timing and final temperature to allow for optical trapping at the end. The required laser powers and geometry parameters to implement effective slowing and trapping within a time frame, where the majority of atoms is not annihilated, is in reach of current technology of $\mathrm{mJ}$ energy pulses of approximately $100\ \mathrm{ns}$ duration. Future extensions to collectively enhance cooling and trapping in optical cavities or hollow-core fibres should finally allow fast preparation of ultra-cold Ps systems as future basis of superradiant lasing and Bose--Einstein condensates.

Abstract

Digital quantum simulation (DQS) is one of the most promising applications of quantum computing. Trotterised DQS models quantum dynamics by decomposing the evolution into a sequence of gate-based Trotter steps. Using larger Trotter steps reduces the required gate count, but it is now understood that the Trotterisation step size cannot be increased beyond a certain threshold value, beyond which there is a sudden breakdown in simulation accuracy, due to the onset of quantum chaos. To date, this behaviour has only been studied for closed systems not subjected to the environmental noise and decoherence to which real-world quantum computers are highly sensitive. In this work, we study a range of experimentally relevant open-system quantum simulator models and show that Trotterisation thresholds persist even when open-system effects are included, even in the long-time, steady-state limit, once all coherence has been lost. We show that open DQS can accurately reproduce the dynamics of open target systems prior to the threshold, but still exhibits a sudden performance breakdown beyond it. We introduce new, statistical techniques to rigorously characterise the onset of quantum chaos beyond the Trotterisation threshold for both approximately unitary and genuine dissipative quantum chaos, based on random matrix theory (RMT), building on techniques developed for closed systems. Finally, we demonstrate that dissipative quantum chaos emerges when the dissipation is in balance with the presence of underlying unitary quantum chaos; the dissipation must be strong enough to induce genuinely dissipative behaviour, yet not so strong as to suppress the underlying chaotic structure.

Abstract

The coherence of free electrons sets fundamental limits on the resolution and contrast of phase-sensitive electron microscopy because coherence is lost whenever the electron leaves a distinguishing excitation in its environment. We develop a quantitative theory of fast-electron decoherence in two canonical geometries: a cylindrical hole drilled through a realistic metal and a circular aperture in a thin perfectly conducting film. By combining an electromagnetic Green-tensor formulation with the fluctuation--dissipation theorem while fully retaining retardation, we obtain the decoherence probability and elastic phase as functions of the electron trajectory, temperature, and material response. For the cylindrical hole, we obtain a closed-form, azimuthally resolved expression that separates the dependences on path position, temperature, and conductivity. In the high-conductivity and high-temperature limits, this result reduces to a universal expression that is linear in temperature and independent of both conductivity and electron velocity. For the circular aperture, which we solve using a cylindrical-wave modal expansion, the energy-loss probability diverges as $1/ω$ at low frequency, whereas the decoherence probability remains finite and reaches a maximum when the aperture radius is comparable to the relevant electromagnetic wavelength. Because the interaction is spatially localized, aperture-induced decoherence is generally weaker than that produced by a cylindrical hole, except for sufficiently large apertures at high temperature. Finally, we show that, for sufficiently large holes, decoherence can dominate the spatial broadening of a focused electron probe and must therefore be incorporated into the design of coherent electron-beam instruments.

Abstract

We investigate the dynamics of full counting statistics (FCS) of the growth of the total number of fermions in a one-dimensional non-interacting lattice subjected to a localized particle gain (source) at one edge. The dynamics of the setup is modeled by the Gorini--Kossakowski--Sudarshan--Lindblad (GKSL) quantum master equation. We recast the counting problem that involves counting particle number at every lattice site to a problem where only the local injected site is involved. We employ the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral formalism within the GKSL framework and derive an analytical expression for the cumulant generating function at arbitrary times and obtain a Levitov-Lesovik type formula in the long-time limit, earlier derived for boundary driven setups in the steady-state. For clean lattices with either short- or long-range hopping, supporting single-particle delocalized eigenstates, we show that all cumulants grow linearly with time in the asymptotic regime. Our analytical results are in excellent agreement with direct numerical simulations. These findings establish a general framework for characterizing FCS and quantum fluctuations in driven open fermionic systems with localized particle injection.

Abstract

We employ a projection operator technique to study the equilibrium phases and the non-equilibrium quench and ramp dynamics of ultracold bosons described by a two-dimensional (2D) Bose-Hubbard model coupled to a high-finesse cavity with competing local short-range and cavity-mediated long-range interactions. We find that the phase boundaries between the superfluid (SF), supersolid (SS), charge-density-wave (CDW) and Mott insulating (MI) phases are significantly modified by the presence of these fluctuations. An explicit comparison with Gutzwiller mean-field theory shows the importance of quantum fluctuations in shaping such post-quench dynamics; this feature may be tested experimentally as we discuss. We also study the ramp dynamics of these bosons and identify a bound on the ramp rate above which the present method is expected to produce accurate results.

Abstract

Summary. This paper rigorously analyzes asymmetric readout errors in NISQ quantum computing experiments, building on previous work. By applying advanced statistical tools, the authors tested hypotheses regarding gate error contamination using data from Google's 2019 experiment. The key finding is that while some simulations show evidence of gate error effects, the primary experimental data suggests readout asymmetry is the dominant source of error, though temporal instability remains a concern.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed analysis of readout error characterization is crucial for understanding the limitations of current quantum hardware, directly impacting the feasibility of running complex quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes asymmetric readout errors in Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) experiments, extending previous work that focused on symmetric errors.

Main result. The effect of amplitude damping gate errors, predicted by some models, was not observed in the Google 2019 experimental data for $12 \le n \le 53$ qubits, although discrepancies were found in certain simulators.

Method. The authors use Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) and Fourier methods to model and quantify the asymmetric readout error probabilities ($q_{1 o 0}$ and $q_{0 o 1}$).

Model / system. The study focuses on NISQ devices, referencing the Google 2019 'quantum supremacy' experiment and using quantum simulators (IBM's and Google's). The noise is modeled using a 3-parameter readout error model.

Key observables. Readout error probabilities ($q_{1 o 0}, q_{0 o 1}$), the fraction of zeroes and ones in measurement samples, and the anticoncentration measure ($c$).

Important parameters / regimes. Number of qubits ($n=12$ to $n=53$), and the error parameters derived from MLE.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the observed variation in measured proportions is primarily due to readout errors, though later sections suggest temporal instability might stem from gate errors rather than readout drift.

Figures summary. Figures compare measured proportions of 1's from Sycamore/Zuchongzhi against stable simulations, showing that measured variation exceeds expected binomial distributions, suggesting instability.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from analyzing the theoretical framework for asymmetric errors using Fourier tools, to applying this to Google's 2019 data (showing no gate error signature), and finally comparing results across multiple simulators and experimental runs to pinpoint the source of observed instability.

Abstract

This paper continues our analysis of NISQ experiments and especially the Google 2019 ``quantum supremacy" experiment. In arXiv:2008.05177 we proposed statistical tools to analyze symmetric and asymmetric readout errors, and the symmetric case was extensively developed and studied in arXiv:2404.00935, where we used Fourier methods combined with statistical tools. Here we extend and complement our earlier study and analyze asymmetric readout errors for data from experiments and simulations. Our study was further motivated by a paper by Fefferman et al. arXiv:2306.16659, who showed that the effect of amplitude damping gate errors is to have larger fractions of zeroes in the samples produced by the quantum computer. We show that this effect of gate errors is not observed in the Google experimental data, where, for the entire range of the number of qubits, $12 \le n \le 53$, the fraction of zeroes and ones reflects only the asymmetry of reported readout errors, and report on simulations with $n=12,14$ using IBM's and Google's simulators.

Characterising the precision of a clock without any external time reference

Authors: Pablo Á. Domínguez, Christopher Chubb, Mischa P. Woods

Summary. This paper presents a novel technique to determine the intrinsic precision of a clock using only its own ticking signal and a simple electronic delay mechanism. By analyzing the statistical moments of the tick count until a delayed trigger fires, the authors derive a formula that bypasses the need for external time standards. This method is significant for developing autonomous timekeeping systems.

Why it may be interesting. While not strictly quantum, the concept of self-referential measurement and characterizing fundamental physical constants (like time precision) using only internal statistics is highly relevant to quantum metrology and quantum clock comparisons.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is characterizing the intrinsic precision ($R$) of a clock without relying on any external, ideal time reference or a second, equally precise clock.

Main result. The authors derived and experimentally validated a formula to estimate clock precision using only the statistical properties of the clock's own ticks and a delayed trigger circuit.

Method. The method involves connecting the clock to a signal splitter, using one output to drive a delayed trigger, and counting the ticks at the other output until the trigger fires. The precision is extracted from the mean and variance of this tick count.

Model / system. The system consists of a single autonomous clock, a signal splitter, and a delayed trigger circuit. The analysis treats the clock as a source of weakly dependent ticks, and the delay element's uncertainty is explicitly incorporated into the final estimator.

Key observables. The primary observables are the mean tick count ($E[ au_D]$) and the variance ($\Delta^2( au_D)$) measured before the delayed trigger fires.

Important parameters / regimes. The key parameters are the clock precision $R = \mu^2/\sigma^2$, the average ticking period $\mu$, and the normalized uncertainty from the delay element $\Delta^2(D)/\mu^2$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the asymptotic regime where the delay uncertainty is small relative to the tick count variance, and that the delayed trigger only marks the end of the interval without defining a frequency standard.

Figures summary. Figure 2 illustrates the block diagram of the proposed setup: the clock feeds a signal splitter, which connects to both the delayed trigger and the tick counter.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theoretical framework using renewal theory and central limit theorems to derive the asymptotic formula for $R$. This is followed by a detailed description of the experimental setup and validation using a microcontroller-based clock source.

Abstract

Determining a clock's precision usually requires comparison with a better time reference, yet no ideal reference is physically attainable. We show how a single clock can characterise its own precision using only a signal splitter and delayed trigger, whose uncertainty is explicitly accounted for. The resulting estimator depends only on tick-count statistics. A purpose-built electronic circuit validates the method without an external timekeeper or a second clock.

Cubic Phase Gate with a Trapped Ion Oscillator

Type: experiment · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.34774v1

Summary. The authors experimentally demonstrate a protocol to implement the Cubic Phase Gate (CPG) on the motional modes of a trapped ion. By optimizing sequences of state-dependent force pulses, they achieve high-fidelity gate operations for both vacuum and coherent states. This work is significant because it realizes a necessary anharmonic nonlinearity required for universal quantum computation in continuous-variable systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, high-fidelity experimental realization of a non-Gaussian, anharmonic gate (CPG) using trapped ions, which is a crucial building block for scaling up CV quantum computation beyond simple linear operations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central goal is to experimentally demonstrate and characterize the Cubic Phase Gate (CPG), which is a required anharmonic nonlinear unitary for achieving universal gate sets in Continuous-Variable (CV) quantum computation.

Main result. The protocol successfully approximates the CPG for both vacuum and low-amplitude coherent state inputs, achieving high state overlaps (up to 0.998) and demonstrating nonlinear squeezing below the vacuum limit.

Method. The CPG is approximated by implementing a sequence of state-dependent force (SDF) pulses, with parameters optimized numerically to maximize the state overlap with the target unitary evolution.

Model / system. The experiment uses a single trapped $^{171} ext{Yb}^+$ ion, utilizing its motional modes (specifically the R1 mode) as the quantum resource. The gate operation is implemented via controlled interactions derived from the ion's motional Hamiltonian.

Key observables. State overlap (fidelity), Wigner function, and Nonlinear Squeezing (NLS) of the characteristic function.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of pulses ($N=6, 10, 14$), initial coherent state amplitude ($|\alpha| \lesssim 0.5$), and the squeezing parameter $\lambda$.

Assumptions / limitations. The fidelity analysis relies on approximating the target unitary via a finite Fourier series expansion using the SDF pulses; NLS analysis requires fitting the experimental characteristic function to a theoretical model.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental circuit diagram, and supplementary figures compare simulated and measured Wigner functions and NLS curves for both vacuum and cubic phase states.

Paper structure. The paper details the theoretical requirement for anharmonic gates, presents the experimental protocol using optimized SDF pulses, and concludes with detailed characterization of the resulting state using Wigner functions and NLS measurements.

Abstract

The motional modes of trapped ions are promising candidates for continuous-variable (CV) quantum computation and simulation. However, to achieve a universal gate set for CV quantum computation, anharmonic nonlinear unitaries are required. Here, we experimentally demonstrate a protocol that allows us to approximate the cubic phase gate. By numerically optimizing the phase angles and amplitudes of a set of state-dependent force pulses, we demonstrate the cubic phase gate for both vacuum and low-$α$ coherent state inputs and verify its performance. Our protocol can also be extended to approximate other nonlinear unitaries.
