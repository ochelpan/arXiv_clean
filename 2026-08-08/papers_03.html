Summary. The paper develops a novel intrinsic measure of information loss for general quantum channels operating under a prepare-evolve-measure framework. It proves that zero information loss is equivalent to the channel possessing 'universal pristineness,' which mathematically guarantees perfect state recovery, mirroring the conditions required by Quantum Error Correction theory. This provides a powerful, purely channel-theoretic tool to analyze phenomena like black hole evaporation.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a fundamental, operational measure of information degradation in open quantum systems, linking concepts from quantum error correction directly to physical processes like Hawking radiation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To introduce a rigorous, intrinsic measure of information loss for any quantum process modeled by a prepare-evolve-measure (PEM) scenario.

Main result. The vanishing of this intrinsic information loss is shown to be equivalent to the channel satisfying 'universal pristineness,' which in turn is mathematically equivalent to the necessary and sufficient conditions for perfect state recovery (Quantum Error Correction).

Method. The authors define information loss using conditional entropy over the PEM framework, deriving key equivalences between zero information loss, the existence of a perfect retrodiction map, and specific channel properties.

Model / system. The analysis uses general quantum channels ($\mathcal{E}$) acting on an input state $ ho$, modeled by Alice preparing an ensemble and Bob performing a POVM measurement. A key application is to the Hayden-Preskill model describing black hole evaporation.

Key observables. Intrinsic Information Loss ($K( ho, E)$), Universal Pristineness, Root Fidelity ($ ext{Fr}$), Perfect Retrodiction Map ($g$).

Important parameters / regimes. The code subspace $\mathcal{H}_{ ext{code}}$, the state $ ho$, and the channel $\mathcal{E}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes a process modeled by the PEM scenario, and later proofs rely on established theorems like the Knill-Laflamme conditions.

Figures summary. Not specified in detail, but figures illustrate the relationship between stochastic maps, retrodiction maps, and error-free communication.

Paper structure. The paper builds from defining $K$ via entropy differences to establishing its equivalence with perfect retrodiction ($K=0 \iff g$ exists), then generalizing this to prove that zero loss implies universal pristineness, culminating in the application to black hole evaporation.

Abstract

We introduce a measure of information loss for any quantum process that may be modeled by a prepare-evolve-measure scenario: Alice prepares an ensemble of states that gets sent via a quantum channel to Bob, who then measures the output. As a quantum channel models open system dynamics, our measure of information loss quantifies Bob's inability to retrodict with certainty which state Alice sent through the channel. By minimizing this measure over all possible pure state ensemble decompositions of a fixed state $ρ$, and over all POVMs on the output of a channel $\mathcal{E}$, we arrive at an intrinsic notion of information loss for any state-channel pair $(ρ,\mathcal{E})$. We show that the vanishing of information loss with respect to all states supported on a fixed codespace $\mathcal{H}_{\text{code}}$ is equivalent to a condition we term \emph{universal pristineness}, which ensures that orthogonal pure states in $\mathcal{H}_{\text{code}}$ get sent via the channel $\mathcal{E}$ to possibly mixed states whose supports are orthogonal. Moreover, we prove universal pristineness is equivalent to the Knill-Laflamme conditions in quantum error correction, which are necessary and sufficient for the existence of a perfect recovery channel for all states supported on $\mathcal{H}_{\text{code}}$. As an application, we apply our framework to the Hayden-Preskill model of black hole evaporation, demonstrating that the evaporation channel becomes asymptotically universally pristine, thereby providing a purely channel-theoretic formulation of Page-time information retrieval.

Summary. The researchers characterized the quantum state of electron beams generated by programmable electrostatic phase plates using mixed-state ptychography. They found that the delivered beam is significantly mixed, contradicting idealized assumptions used in current imaging proposals. This work provides essential, quantitative measurements of beam purity and coherence for next-generation electron optics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial quantum characterization of an electron source used in advanced imaging, directly addressing the necessary input parameters (purity/coherence) that theoretical models often assume but fail to quantify experimentally.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is that proposals for advanced electron microscopy techniques rely on assuming the delivered electron wave is pure and fully coherent, a property that has never been quantitatively measured.

Main result. The study successfully reconstructed the transverse density matrix of an electrostatic spiral phase plate, revealing that the delivered beam is substantially mixed, with purity dropping significantly as the applied bias increases.

Method. Mixed-state ptychography was employed using standard 4D STEM scans to reconstruct the full transverse density matrix ($ ho$) for various programmed charge states.

Model / system. The physical system is a microelectromechanical electrostatic spiral phase plate (SPP) used to generate and control electron vortex beams with tunable Orbital Angular Momentum (OAM). The analysis models the beam state using its density matrix formalism.

Key observables. Transverse density matrix ($ ho$), purity ($ ext{Tr} ho^2$), von Neumann entropy ($S$), mode powers, and spatial coherence as functions of applied bias/charge.

Important parameters / regimes. The measured purity drops from $\approx 0.47$ to $\approx 0.24$ with increasing bias; the reconstructed OAM ($\langle\ell angle$) scales linearly with voltage.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation is that current diagnostics only measure diagonal elements or assume pure states, and the reconstruction technique itself relies on specific modeling assumptions regarding coherence transfer.

Figures summary. Figures show reconstructed probe modes in real space (annular intensity) and aperture plane (azimuthal winding); spectra track mode power, purity, and entropy as a function of programmed charge; one figure shows the linear calibration of mean OAM vs. voltage.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the gap by noting unmeasured beam purity. It then details the mixed-state ptychography method applied to the SPP. Results quantify the state mixing and coherence for multiple vortex states, concluding with implications for dose efficiency improvements.

Abstract

Programmable electron optics -- electronically controlled phase plates -- underpin proposals from dose-efficient phase imaging to shaped-electron X-ray sources, nearly all assuming a pure, fully coherent delivered wave whose purity has never been measured. Here we reconstruct the transverse density matrix of a microelectromechanical electrostatic spiral phase plate by mixed-state ptychography, from one four-dimensional STEM scan per state and without added hardware. The delivered beam is substantially mixed: its purity falls from approximately 0.47 to approximately 0.24 as the applied bias grows, inconsistent with a fixed lateral source-blur model, while the real-space coherence width stays near 1 nm. The same scans calibrate the device in situ, allow virtual orbital-angular-momentum sorting and, through a partial-coherence-aware transfer theory, indicate that purifying the output could improve dose efficiency roughly threefold. One acquisition thus becomes a quantum-state acceptance test for programmable electron optics, supplying the purity and coherence that emerging phase-plate and diffractive-imaging schemes assume but leave unquantified.

Summary. This paper develops an advanced, geometrically invariant effective string theory toolbox for Majorana fermions residing at fluctuating quantum Hall interfaces. It successfully derives the low-energy dynamics by treating the interface's movement and curvature explicitly within the fermionic action. The resulting framework is crucial for building comprehensive theories of dynamical non-Abelian edge physics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful effective field theory toolkit for describing boundary physics in strongly correlated electron systems (like FQHE states), which is highly relevant to understanding exotic edge modes beyond simple Luttinger liquid descriptions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is constructing a spatially reparametrization-invariant effective theory for chiral Majorana fermions residing on an interface whose geometry fluctuates freely.

Main result. The framework successfully separates universal kinematic transport laws from microscopic couplings, identifying the Majorana stress tensor as the mediator between neutral and geometric dynamics.

Method. The authors employ worldsheet formulations, variational principles, and canonical quantization using half-density variables to derive the effective action for the fermionic sector coupled to geometry fluctuations.

Model / system. The system models a quantum Hall interface derived from the Moore-Read (Pfaffian) state, focusing on the dynamics of its neutral chiral Majorana mode ($\psi$) as the boundary bends and moves nonrelativistically.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes a nonrelativistic worldsheet, that fixed-edge theory is insufficient, and initially focuses on a single-channel description (Moore–Read–vacuum).

Paper structure. Abstract

A Moore--Read interface carries a chiral Majorana mode on a boundary whose geometry may itself fluctuate. Fixed-edge theory does not determine how this neutral mode should be transported when the interface bends and moves, or how its dynamics couples to the fluctuating shape. Here we construct a spatially reparametrization-invariant Majorana theory on the nonrelativistic worldsheet of a freely moving interface. The changing line element fixes a universal half-density transport law, while additional curvature- and velocity-dependent couplings remain controlled by microscopic interface physics. The resulting framework identifies the Majorana stress tensor as the mediator between neutral and geometric dynamics and provides the neutral sector needed for effective theories of dynamical non-Abelian quantum Hall interfaces.

Summary. This paper analyzes a lattice model of active search-and-capture where mobile walkers compete for immobile targets. The core finding is that optimizing the assignment policy—specifically using maximum-cardinality matching—is a more powerful control parameter for reducing capture time than adjusting walker movement persistence. This highlights how coordination in resource allocation dictates the system's extreme-time behavior.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly quantum, the study models complex non-equilibrium dynamics involving resource competition and state transitions, which shares conceptual parallels with modeling energy landscapes or information flow in open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates collective search-and-capture dynamics on a lattice, focusing on how different assignment policies affect the overall completion time ($T_c$).

Main result. The assignment policy can control $T_c$ more strongly than walker persistence ($\alpha$), with maximum-cardinality matching providing significant speedups over greedy approaches.

Method. The authors use a minimal lattice model simulation approach, comparing baseline single-round assignment against cascading reassignment and maximum-cardinality matching algorithms.

Model / system. The system is a minimal lattice model on a periodic square lattice containing mobile walkers and immobile targets. Capture occurs via an irreversible, finite-range, mutually exclusive assignment rule.

Key observables. Completion Time ($T_c$), $T_{50\%}$, $T_{90\%}$, and the instantaneous fraction of assigned walkers ($f_{ ext{assigned}}(t)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Walkers' reorientation rate ($\alpha$) and search radius ($R$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis focuses on extreme-value statistics, where $T_c$ is governed by the late-time tail of the capture process.

Figures summary. Figures show non-monotonic dependence of $T_c$ on $\alpha$ and plateauing behavior with increasing $R$. Figure 3 compares near-optimal completion times for different assignment policies.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the model dynamics, analyzes the effect of walker persistence ($\alpha$) on $T_c$, and then systematically compares three distinct assignment policies (single-round, cascading, max-cardinality matching) to quantify their impact on the extreme capture time.

Abstract

We study a minimal lattice model of active search-and-capture in which persistent random walkers locate and irreversibly capture immobile targets through a finite-range, mutually exclusive assignment rule. We measure the collective completion time $T_c$ as a function of the walkers' reorientation rate $α$ and the search radius $R$. The dependence $T_c(α)$ is non-monotonic, with a minimum at intermediate persistence whose depth decreases as $R$ grows. Capture kinetics show that $T_c$ is not a typical capture time but is governed by the extreme, late-time tail of the capture process, while the bulk of targets are captured much earlier; this tail is controlled mainly by the free-exploration phase rather than by the final directed approach. We then compare the baseline single-round assignment rule with cascading reassignment and with maximum-cardinality matching on a candidate graph. The two greedy policies (single-round and cascading) agree at very small $R$, whereas maximum-cardinality matching already produces a strong speedup at moderate $R$: improved matching reduces $T_c$ by factors of several at large $R$, and by more than an order of magnitude at moderate $R$. Thus, in this collective, depletion-coupled search problem, the assignment policy can control the capture time more strongly than the walkers' persistence.

Summary. This paper presents a method called 'code surgery' to perform measurements of logical Pauli operators in fault-tolerant quantum computation using Iceberg codes. By viewing measurement as gauging a global symmetry, the authors construct explicit gadgets that are proven to be fault-detecting via simulation. This makes it a highly relevant theoretical roadmap for building complex quantum computations on next-generation hardware.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges abstract quantum error correction theory (gauging symmetries) with concrete, near-term physical implementations on platforms like neutral atoms that support long-range connectivity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop a fault-tolerant method for measuring logical Pauli products within quantum error-correcting codes.

Main result. The authors successfully construct explicit, fault-detecting surgery gadgets for the high-rate Iceberg codes, showing that measurement can be achieved by gauging global symmetries of the code Hamiltonian.

Method. The technique involves 'code surgery,' which treats logical measurements as gauging a global symmetry. This is formalized by adjoining auxiliary qubits to make the operator local, followed by a reverse deformation process.

Model / system. The focus is on the Iceberg codes $ ext{[[2N, 2N-2, 2]]}$ and their associated Hamiltonians. The theoretical framework utilizes concepts from lattice gauge theory applied to quantum error correction.

Key observables. Fault detection requires suppressing the post-selected logical error rate by $O(p^2)$ relative to the physical error probability $p$. Key observables include the probability distribution $ ext{Pr}[\mu]$ of measurement outcomes.

Important parameters / regimes. The code parameters are $[[2N, 2N-2, 2]]$, and the construction depends on classifying logical Pauli operators into orbits under the permutation automorphism group $S_{2N}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The gadgets require reconfigurable long-range connectivity for physical implementation. The analysis relies on mapping measurements to algebraic structures defined by boundary and cycle spaces over $\mathbb{F}_2$.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the Pauli-based compilation of a GHZ gate using measurement sequences, while Figure 2 shows the physical layout structure for the Iceberg code.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the concept via a small warm-up example (four spins), then generalizes this to the Iceberg codes by classifying logical operators into orbits. It details the gauging procedure and concludes with circuit simulations confirming fault detection.

Abstract

We construct explicit error-detecting surgery gadgets---small systems of auxiliary qubits and checks---for the high-rate Iceberg codes $[[2N,2N-2,2]]$, to perform fault-detected measurements of logical Pauli products. The construction follows the perspective of surgery as the gauging of a logical operator, regarded as a symmetry of the code. We give a complete classification of logical Pauli operators under the permutation automorphism group of the Iceberg code, reducing the construction to one gadget per orbit, and we verify with circuit-level simulations that the gadgets are fault-detecting, with the expected post-selected logical error rate. The gadgets require reconfigurable long-range connectivity, available on platforms such as neutral-atom arrays, making an error-detected demonstration of Pauli-based computation a natural near-term experiment. The paper doubles as a self-contained introduction to gauging and code surgery, developed alongside a simple worked example.

Abstract

We investigate thickness-dependent charge-density-wave (CDW) transitions in 1$T$-TaS$_2$ using temperature-dependent Raman spectroscopy and electrical transport. Raman measurements show that the incommensurate, nearly commensurate, and commensurate CDW phases persist down to the monolayer limit. These findings highlight the cooperative roles of electron correlation, electron-phonon interaction, and interlayer coupling in shaping the ground state and transition dynamics of atomically thin 1$T$-TaS$_2$, opening pathways for engineering correlated phases in two-dimensional CDW systems.

Abstract

Can one neural wave function describe both a Bose condensate and a chiral topological liquid? We introduce ChernFormer, which combines a fermionic transformer with a fixed Chern-Simons phase that attaches one statistical vortex to every particle pair. Each factor changes sign under exchange, so their product is exactly bosonic. The fixed phase changes statistics but not probability, making every bosonic learning problem equivalent to a fermionic one with the same approximation error and overlap. With enough capacity, ChernFormer can approximate any normalizable bosonic wave function on the plane at fixed particle number. A finite, smooth network still vanishes when particles meet, yet this contact hole can shrink while the wave function and condensate fraction approach those of a nodeless condensate. Following the needle-in-a-haystack target-reconstruction benchmark introduced in \cite{NazaryanGaggioliTengFu2025}, we test ChernFormer on the Kalmeyer--Laughlin ground state and its first two chiral edge states. The overlap curves stay close to unity through their largest sampled sizes, while independent amplitude and phase maps at $N=20$ for all three states recover both local Laughlin vortices and the collective edge vortex. By contrast, a continuous, nonzero product of identical particle-wise factors misses these elementary edge sectors. ChernFormer therefore provides one variational language for conventional bosonic order and chiral topological matter.

Abstract

Quantum state tomography (QST) has attracted considerable attention due to its fundamental role in quantum information processing. In this paper, we develop a unified theoretical framework for analyzing the sample complexity of structured QST under noisy observations arising from state preparation noise, measurement noise, and finite-shot statistical noise. The proposed framework applies to a broad family of structured quantum-state classes, including general mixed states, sparse states, low-rank states, matrix product states (MPSs), matrix product operators (MPOs), projected entangled-pair states (PEPSs), and projected entangled-pair operators (PEPOs), while further introducing physically consistent structured models---including low-rank and sparse states, low-rank MPOs (LR-MPOs), and low-rank PEPOs (LR-PEPOs)---that simultaneously exploit low-dimensional structures and preserve the physical constraint. Abstract

\textit{Shadow tomography} is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory. Given multiple copies of an unknown $d$-dimensional quantum state $ρ$ and a known collection of observables ${E_1,\ldots,E_m}$, the goal is to estimate all expectation values $\{\Tr(ρE_i)\}_{i=1}^m$ to additive accuracy $\varepsilon$ with probability at least $1-δ$. An elusive open question from the seminal shadow tomography work of Aaronson (STOC'18) is whether this task admits a dimension-independent sample complexity with only polylogarithmic dependence on $m$, as suggested by the best-known lower bounds. In this work, we give a quantum protocol for shadow tomography with sample complexity \[ O\left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2} \frac{(\log (m/δ))^4} {(\log\log (m/δ))^3} \right), \] which is polylogarithmic in the number of observables and independent of the dimension of the unknown state thereby answering Aaronson's original question while also providing an exponential improvement in the prior best dimension independent sample complexity of shadow tomography from Sinha (STOC'25). Our approach first reduces the general shadow-tomography problem to a finite-ensemble estimation problem via a minimax argument. We then develop an observable-independent protocol that repeatedly applies the pretty-good measurement and updates the priori distribution over the finite ensemble according to the measurement outcomes. A refined tail analysis of the resulting estimation error yields simultaneous accuracy guarantees for all observables.

Abstract

Circuit cutting lets a large quantum neural network (QNN) run as independent subcircuits on small devices, but rebuilding its outputs by reconstruction carries a classical sampling overhead exponential in the number of cuts - the dominant runtime cost in prior work. We ask whether, for machine-learning tasks, this step is necessary, and replace it with late fusion: each subcircuit is trained and measured independently, and a small classical head combines their outputs - a linear-cost, decision-level combination borrowed from multimodal learning. To characterize the trade-off we introduce a quantumness dial $Q$, a tunable reconstruction budget interpolating from pure fusion to full reconstruction, and a cut-entanglement diagnostic that indicates how much reconstruction a task needs (Spearman $ρ=0.59$ over $104$ runs). Across synthetic and standard datasets, independently trained late fusion matches full reconstruction accuracy within $0.04$ at every point of the controlled sweep and on every classical benchmark, at exponentially lower cost; it is also markedly more robust to shot and device noise. Controlled entangled-data experiments locate the boundary where fusion must fail. We do not claim advantage over classical machine learning - consistent with recent benchmarking, quantum offers no accuracy edge on these datasets. Late fusion is thus an efficient, noise-robust, self-characterizing alternative to reconstruction for circuit-cutting QML.
