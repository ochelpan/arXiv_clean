The charge density fluctuations and the Shrinking Fermi Liquid scenario for strange metallicity in cuprates

Authors: Sergio Caprara, Sauri Bhattacharyya, Carlo Di Castro, Giovanni Mirarchi, Götz Seibold, Marco Grilli
Type: both · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08476v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Kondo & dissipative impurity 2/5 · non-equilibrium dynamics 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5 · non-equilibrium universality 1/5

Summary. This paper proposes that the strange metal state in cuprates arises from itinerant fermions scattering off abundant, low-energy Charge Density Fluctuations (CDF). Using the Shrinking Fermi Liquid theory, the authors show this mechanism consistently explains linear-T resistivity and other anomalous transport properties by incorporating CDFs alongside phonons and paramagnons.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed coupling of fermionic quasiparticles to specific bosonic fluctuations (CDF) provides a concrete mechanism for emergent non-Fermi liquid behavior in strongly correlated materials, which is highly relevant to understanding quantum critical points.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the anomalous 'Strange Metal' (SM) state observed in slightly overdoped cuprates, characterized by resistivity linear in temperature, which defies standard Fermi Liquid theory.

Main result. The SM behavior is successfully described by interpreting transport properties as scattering between itinerant fermions and low-energy Charge Density Fluctuations (CDF), leading to a consistent model for various anomalous measurements.

Method. The authors combine theoretical modeling (Shrinking Fermi Liquid theory) with analysis of experimental data from RIXS and EELS spectroscopy.

Model / system. The system is the cuprate superconductor, modeled by considering itinerant fermions interacting with low-energy bosonic excitations, specifically Charge Density Fluctuations (CDF), phonons, and spin paramagnons.

Key observables. T-linear resistivity ($ ho \propto T$), specific heat coefficient ($\gamma(T)$), Seebeck coefficient ($S/T$), and magnetoresistance.

Important parameters / regimes. Low energy scale of CDF ($\omega_{ ext{CDF}} \sim 5-10$ meV) and the finite energy scale $M/\gamma$.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption for low-temperature behavior is that the CDF dissipation parameter ($\gamma$) grows logarithmically as temperature decreases when superconductivity is suppressed.

Figures summary. Figure 2 shows RIXS and EELS spectra of Bi2212, decomposing signals to identify elastic peaks, CDFs, phonons, and paramagnon continua.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the SM problem, identifies CDF as the key low-energy scatterer via experimental characterization (RIXS/EELS), develops the Shrinking Fermi Liquid theory framework for transport and optical properties, and finally applies this model to explain multiple anomalous thermodynamic and transport measurements across different doping regimes.

Abstract

We interpret the strange metal (SM) properties of slightly overdoped cuprates in terms of the recently proposed Shrinking Fermi Liquid theory. This is based on the pervading presence in the cuprate phase diagram of charge density fluctuations (CDF), which have been identified and characterized in RIXS. These fluctuations are abundant and have a low energy due to the proximity of the charge density wave quantum critical point hidden under the superconducting dome of cuprates, but have a short range and non-critical character with a finite energy M/γ~10 meV as measured above Tc. Here M~ ξ^-2 is determined by the short correlation length ξ, while γencodes the Landau damping ruling the lifetime of the charge fluctuations. Besides these low energy CDF, cuprates also display phonons and a broad continuum of particle-hole excitations, mostly due to spin paramagnons arising from their strongly correlated character. With these experimentally characterized ingredients we show that above Tc the SM properties in transport are well described in terms of fermionic Landau quasiparticles scattering with CDF and phonons. The optical properties can instead be interpreted by the combined effect of low energy CDF determining the temperature dependence, and of the paramagnon continuum determining a linear in frequency scattering rate. Remarkably, the combined effect of these simple ingredients also induces ω/T scaling properties for frequencies larger than M/γ. When superconductivity is suppressed by strong magnetic fields the SM properties extend down to a few Kelvin. By assuming that the CDF dissipation parameter γgrows logarithmically by lowering T, we account for all anomalous transport and thermodynamic properties of cuprates (specific heat, Seebeck, heat transport, resistivity, and magnetoresistance) thereby providing a consistent scenario for the SM phase of cuprates.

Summary. This paper introduces 'Chiral Tube Algebras' to create a unified mathematical framework bridging Chiral Algebras (local symmetries) and Topological Defect Lines (topological symmetries) within 2D CFTs. It shows how this new structure naturally describes the behavior of chiral algebras when subjected to processes like finite gauging or interaction with defects. This advances the understanding of symmetry in topological quantum field theories.

Why it may be interesting. While highly abstract, the concept of generalizing symmetry structures from local operators to defect lines is crucial for understanding boundary conditions and topological phases in condensed matter systems, which can inform models used in AMO physics or quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to unify two complementary symmetry concepts in 2D Conformal Field Theories (CFTs): Chiral Algebras and symmetries associated with Topological Defect Lines (TDLs).

Main result. It introduces 'Chiral Tube Algebras' as a generalized framework that incorporates TDL effects, allowing for the description of chiral algebras under finite gauging.

Method. The authors use advanced algebraic techniques involving fusion categories, module theory, and generalizations of vertex operator algebras to construct and analyze these new algebras.

Model / system. The theoretical setting is 2D Conformal Field Theories (CFTs), which are studied using mathematical tools like chiral currents, defect Hilbert spaces ($\mathcal{H}_L$), and tensor categories.

Key observables. Irreducible modules of the algebras; fusion rules ($N^k_{ij}$); mapping between local and defect Hilbert spaces.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensionality (2D) and the nature of the symmetry (local vs. topological/non-invertible).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis often assumes unitary compact CFTs when dealing with chiral algebras, though the framework is designed to extend beyond these limits.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates how a TDL can be interpreted as an operator acting on the local Hilbert space or as a defect defining a twisted defect Hilbert space ($\mathcal{H}_L$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the distinct roles of chiral algebras and TDLs, then proposing Chiral Tube Algebras to unify them, illustrating this unification with known examples (Kac-Moody, W algebras), and concluding with a roadmap for future work on non-local currents.

Abstract

Chiral algebras and topological defect lines (TDLs) represent two complementary notions of symmetry in 2d conformal field theories. In this paper, we introduce chiral tube algebras to unify and extend these two notions. Chiral tube algebras generalize chiral algebras in two ways. First, they extend the action of chiral algebras beyond the local Hilbert space to include defect Hilbert spaces twisted by TDLs. Second, they allow for non-local chiral currents attached by TDLs and thus can map between different defect Hilbert spaces, analogous to the tube algebras of TDLs. Since local chiral currents can become non-local after finite gauging, chiral tube algebras provide a natural framework for describing the image of chiral algebras under such gauging. We illustrate this framework through a variety of examples that generalize familiar chiral algebras, including Kac-Moody algebras, $\mathcal{W}$ algebras, superconformal algebras, and their orbifolds/bosonizations. We construct their irreducible modules, which are isomorphic to twisted modules of the corresponding chiral algebras, and use them to organize local and defect Hilbert spaces. In a subsequent paper, we will study chiral tube algebras generated by non-local chiral currents with fractional spins, which have no counterparts in chiral algebras.

Summary. This paper introduces Procrustes tomography as an efficient method to characterize noisy quantum channels, addressing the computational bottleneck of quantum process tomography. By reformulating the problem as a simple least-squares minimization using input and output states, it outperforms established techniques under realistic noise conditions like uneven sampling or state preparation errors. This makes high-fidelity characterization more accessible for NISQ devices.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a computationally tractable and robust framework for characterizing open quantum systems on near-term hardware by simplifying the complex mathematical requirements of full process tomography.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Quantum process tomography, which characterizes noisy quantum channels, is computationally expensive, especially for NISQ devices.

Main result. Procrustes tomography is presented as an efficient alternative that matches or exceeds the performance of established methods like Choi reconstruction and linear inversion tomography.

Method. The method formulates channel reconstruction by minimizing the Frobenius norm between measured output states (B) and input states (A), solving for $\Lambda$ via $\Lambda = B A^+$.

Model / system. The study focuses on characterizing noisy quantum channels ($\Lambda$) acting on qubits, often modeled using dynamics governed by the Lindblad master equation.

Key observables. Infidelity ($1 - \langle F_{E} angle$), Process matrix ($\Lambda$), Choi-matrix ($ ho_{ ext{Choi}}$), and measurement probabilities.

Important parameters / regimes. Number of independent measurement samples ($N$), relaxation times ($T_1, T_2$), and the degree of state preparation error.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis compares methods under conditions of finite-size sampling effects and imperfect input state preparation, requiring specific corrections for accurate results.

Figures summary. Figures compare infidelity vs. measurement shots ($N$) for different reconstruction techniques (Procrustes vs. Choi vs. Linear Inversion) under scenarios involving uneven state sampling or input state preparation errors.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, reviews established tomography methods, develops and details Procrustes tomography, and then rigorously compares its performance against competitors using various realistic noise models (uneven sampling, state preparation errors).

Abstract

What is the most expensive part of quantum device characterization? Clearly, the answer is quantum process tomography. However, especially for noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers, a comprehensive understanding of the noisy quantum dynamics is essential in interpreting the computational output. In this work, we introduce an efficient method -- Procrustes tomography -- that outperforms established methods in a number of aspects. After a pedagogical and constructive introduction, we demonstrate the utility of the method for representative examples of noisy quantum channels.

Summary. The paper introduces a $(2, m)$-threshold quantum data-hiding scheme to securely hide one classical bit among multiple parties. It proves that while any pair can perfectly recover the secret bit through joint measurement, local operations and classical communication reveal negligible information. The construction is notable because it only requires separable states, enhancing its practical feasibility.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete cryptographic primitive (quantum data hiding) that is physically realizable using only separable states, making it highly relevant for quantum communication protocols in AMO settings where entanglement generation might be difficult.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper proposes a $(2, m)$-threshold quantum data-hiding scheme for concealing one classical bit among $m$ parties.

Main result. It demonstrates that the hidden bit can be perfectly recovered by any pair of parties via joint measurement, while local operations and classical communication (LOCC) reveal only an arbitrarily small amount of information.

Method. The scheme is constructed using a building block ensemble based on orthogonal PPT states shared between pairs of parties, leveraging bounds derived from multipartite state discrimination theory.

Model / system. The model involves $m$ parties sharing quantum states in a tensor product Hilbert space. The security relies on the difference between joint measurements and LOCC measurements on these multipartite states.

Key observables. Discrimination probabilities, specifically $p_L(E)$ (LOCC) versus $p_{ ext{PPT}}(E)$, which quantifies the information leakage about the hidden bit.

Important parameters / regimes. The threshold parameter is $(2, m)$, meaning any pair of parties can succeed in recovery. The security relies on $\epsilon > 0$ being arbitrarily small.

Assumptions / limitations. The scheme is shown to be implementable using only separable states of low-dimensional quantum systems, avoiding the need for entanglement.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the subsystems shared between pairs of parties for $m=3$. Figure 2 details the state preparation and broadcast bits for the $m=3$ case.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the general framework for multipartite quantum state discrimination, deriving bounds using PPT properties. It then constructs the $(2, m)$-threshold scheme by defining the shared states ($\vec{ au}_b$) and the classical broadcast bits ($\vec{c}$) necessary to achieve the desired security gap.

Abstract

We consider multiparty quantum state discrimination and present a multiparty quantum data-hiding scheme for one classical bit to be shared among multiple parties. In the proposed scheme, any pair of parties can collaborate to perfectly recover the hidden bit through a joint measurement, whereas measurements based on local operations and classical communication(LOCC) performed even by all parties reveal only an arbitrarily small amount of information. We further provide bounds on the optimal LOCC discrimination of multiparty quantum states. The proposed scheme can be implemented using only separable states of low-dimensional quantum systems, enhancing its practical feasibility.

Summary. This paper introduces Quantum Dense Network Coding (DNC), a protocol demonstrating that quantum resources allow for significantly more efficient information transmission than classical methods. It proves this advantage is robust, requires shared entanglement and quantum links, and establishes a foundation for a new, secure quantum key growing scheme.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on abstract coding theory, the rigorous analysis of entanglement requirements and noise robustness provides general theoretical tools applicable to characterizing resource limitations in complex open quantum systems or communication channels.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is demonstrating how quantum resources can enable communication protocols, specifically 'quantum dense network coding' (DNC), to achieve superior efficiency compared to classical methods.

Main result. DNC transmits function outputs using provably half the qubits per sender than classically required, an advantage that requires both shared entanglement and quantum communication. This framework also yields a novel, information-theoretically secure key growing protocol.

Method. The authors analyze distributed computation success probabilities in various network models (Classical, Quantum, CE, QE) using MAC formalism and Weyl operators to quantify the resource advantage.

Model / system. The study uses abstract quantum communication models involving multiaccess networks computing functions like addition modulo $2^n$. The core mechanism relies on shared maximally entangled states and generalized Bell measurements.

Key observables. Success probability ($P_f^S$), communication advantage (quantified by success probability ratios), and the achievable key distillation rate ($R$).

Important parameters / regimes. The advantage scales exponentially with the number of senders ($n$); security is governed by error parameters $\epsilon$ and soundness/completeness bounds.

Assumptions / limitations. The quantum advantage critically depends on the availability of both shared entanglement and quantum communication channels; asymptotic rate calculations assume product states ($ ho_{AnBn} = ho^{\otimes n}_{AB}$).

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates four types of multiaccess networks. Figure 2 plots the achievable success probability bounds, showing a clear quantum advantage over classical limits. Figure 3 relates to key growing rate calculations.

Paper structure. Abstract

A central problem in quantum information theory is understanding how quantum resources can be used to communicate information more efficiently than classical resources. We introduce quantum dense network coding -- a protocol that transmits the output of a non-Boolean function to a receiver using provably half as many qubits as bits for each sender by not transmitting the entirety of the function inputs. We show this advantage requires both shared entanglement and quantum communication, is robust to noise, and the gap in success probability between quantum and classical communication can be amplified exponentially in the number of senders. Finally, we show that dense network coding gives rise to a novel, information-theoretically secure, quantum cryptographic protocol, which we call measurement-device-independent quantum key growing.

Summary. This paper introduces a comprehensive framework for compiling quantum circuits onto error-corrected silicon spin qubit architectures using the 'snakes on a plane' model. It proposes and compares two novel routing algorithms—shortest-path and rotation-based—to solve the complex scheduling problem. The findings guide researchers by showing that the optimal algorithm choice depends entirely on whether the target hardware is sparse or highly dense.

Main result. The authors propose two novel routing algorithms—shortest-path and rotation-based—showing that their performance depends critically on the underlying architecture's density (sparse vs. high-density).

Abstract

Silicon spin qubits have emerged as a promising qubit technology due to their favorable scaling and fabrication properties. However, efficiently compiling quantum circuits onto spin qubit platforms remains challenging, particularly when accounting for hardware constraints and the high sensitivity to static defects. Existing compilation approaches for spin qubits either largely ignore error correction, despite its critical role for large-scale quantum computation, or focus on low-level schedule constructions, missing a high-level compilation and routing for logical, error-corrected algorithms. To address this gap, we introduce a compilation framework for spin qubits based on the recent snakes on a plane model, which utilizes a 2D surface code and qubit teleportation to mitigate errors. Building on this model, we propose shortest-path and rotation-based algorithms as two novel classes of qubit-routing techniques, along with additional defect-handling and initial-mapping strategies. We evaluate both algorithms across diverse architectural settings and problem sizes, demonstrating that shortest-path methods excel in sparse, low-defect scenarios, while rotation-based approaches perform better in high-density environments. The implementation is publicly available on GitHub as open source.

Summary. The authors demonstrate that nonlocal electronic correlations in 1D materials can spontaneously generate topological order by inducing Charge-Density-Wave (CDW) or Bond-Order-Wave (BOW) states. Using advanced cluster diagrammatic techniques, they map these interacting many-body phases onto effective Hamiltonians resembling the SSH and Rice-Mele models, confirming their nontrivial topological nature via edge state signatures.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete theoretical link between interaction-driven symmetry breaking in correlated electron models and the emergence of robust topological invariants, which is highly relevant for understanding novel quantum phases in condensed matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to establish a mechanism by which nonlocal electronic correlations can induce topological phases in low-dimensional systems, starting from an initially trivial noninteracting band structure.

Main result. Nonlocal interactions drive the system into Bond-Order-Wave (BOW) and Charge-Density-Wave (CDW) phases, both of which possess nontrivial topological character, evidenced by localized edge states.

Method. The research employs an advanced cluster-diagrammatic many-body approach (D-TRILEX framework) applied to the extended Hubbard model, mapping the interacting system onto effective noninteracting Hamiltonians for analysis.

Model / system. The physical system is modeled by the one-dimensional extended Hubbard model, characterized by hopping ($t$), local repulsion ($U$), and nearest-neighbor nonlocal repulsion ($V$). The analysis focuses on the emergence of BOW and CDW phases.

Key observables. BOW order parameter ($ ext{O}{ ext{BOW}}$), staggered charge density ($ ext{O}$), effective Hamiltonian parameters ($u_k, v_k, w_k$), and edge-localization weight $|\phi_j^{ ext{edge}}|^2$.}

Important parameters / regimes. Nonlocal interaction strength ($V$) relative to local repulsion ($U$); temperature ($T=0.1$).

Assumptions / limitations. The single-site D-TRILEX approach fails for symmetry-broken phases due to the nonlocal nature of the order parameters, necessitating a dimer/cluster formulation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams showing coexisting CDW+BOW regions; energy spectra demonstrate the evolution from bulk states to SSH-like edge modes (BOW) and finally to RM-like gapped states (CDW).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first defining the model and using cluster methods to identify symmetry-broken phases. It then maps these interacting states onto effective single-particle Hamiltonians, analyzing the topological signatures (edge states) associated with both CDW and BOW order parameters.

Abstract

We demonstrate that electronic correlations in low-dimensional systems can induce topological phases starting from a topologically trivial noninteracting band structure. Using an advanced cluster-diagrammatic many-body approach applied to the one-dimensional extended Hubbard model, we show that tuning the nonlocal Coulomb interaction drives the emergence of bond-order-wave (BOW) and charge-density-wave (CDW) phases. Despite being interaction-driven and symmetry-broken, these states admit an effective low-energy single-particle description. In particular, the BOW phase maps onto an effective Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model, while the CDW phase, with subleading bond-order correlations, corresponds to a Rice-Mele model. Both phases exhibit a nontrivial topological character, manifested by the presence of localized edge states. Our results establish a mechanism by which nonlocal electronic correlations generate emergent topology in correlated systems.

Main result. On two chaotic test systems, QRC maintained a stable prediction error and superior feature conditioning compared to matched classical reservoirs as system size grew. This confirms that the quantum architecture's structure yields better stability in the learned feature space.

Method. The authors implement QRC using fixed quantum circuits for feature generation ($\phi_q(x_t)$) and train a linear readout via ridge regression. They employ a matched-complexity scaling protocol to ensure fair comparison with classical Echo State Networks (ESN).

Model / system. The methods are tested on chaotic dynamical systems, specifically the Lorenz-96 model and shallow-water fluid equations (SWE) simulated on large grids.

Key observables. Prediction Mean Squared Error (MSE), the condition number ($\kappa$) of the feature Gram matrix ($F^ op F + \alpha I$), and stability across increasing system/reservoir dimensions.

Important parameters / regimes. The comparison is made by growing both the prediction problem size ($K$) and the quantum reservoir dimension ($q$) together, ensuring $K=q$ for matched complexity.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes noiseless statevector evaluation; the paper also notes that the condition number bound controls conditioning but not the inherent bias of the ridge estimator.

Figures summary. Figures compare QRC vs. ESN test MSE (QRC flat, ESN oscillates) and plot the feature-Gram condition number ($\kappa$): $\kappa_{ ext{QRC}}$ saturates near 1 while $\kappa_{ ext{ESN}}$ grows significantly with dimension.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces QRC for chaotic forecasting. It then details the diagnostic protocol, comparing its spectral properties (condition number) against classical ESNs across varying dimensions using L96 and SWE models. Finally, it presents results showing superior stability and conditioning for the quantum approach.

Abstract

Quantum reservoir computing uses a fixed quantum circuit as a feature generator and trains only a simple linear readout on top of it. This makes it cheap to train and free of the optimisation problems that affect many quantum machine-learning models. A natural worry is that the very large feature space the circuit produces might inflate apparent performance without adding anything real. This paper provides two things. First, it gives a complete, reproducible recipe for one such reservoir applied to forecasting chaotic systems, including how data is fed in, how the circuit is built, and how the readout is trained. Second, it gives a way to tell whether the reservoir's high dimension is actually doing useful work. We grow the size of the prediction problem and the size of the quantum reservoir together, so that extra capacity cannot be the explanation for any improvement, and we track a single stability number that measures how well behaved the readout fit is. On two chaotic test systems, a spatiotemporal chain and a shallow-water fluid model, the quantum reservoir keeps a flat, stable error as both sizes grow, while a matched classical reservoir does not. We report where the classical baseline is in fact stronger, so the comparison is honest. The result is a clean specification plus a diagnostic that other groups can apply to any reservoir whose features have a known scale.

A Sparse and Truncated State Vector Simulator for Peaked Circuits

Authors: Diogo R. Ferreira
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07816v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper presents an efficient classical simulation method for quantum circuits, particularly those with peaked output distributions. It achieves efficiency by using a sparse representation of the state vector and implementing controlled truncation via top-k or p-mass limits. This allows users to trade off computational resources against desired accuracy when exact simulation is infeasible.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, resource-aware numerical technique for simulating quantum dynamics that goes beyond standard tensor network methods by directly managing state vector sparsity based on physical probability concentration, which is relevant for understanding complex many-body states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To simulate quantum circuits efficiently when the state vector size grows exponentially, which exceeds classical computational memory.

Main result. The authors developed an open-source implementation that successfully simulates peaked circuits by combining sparse representation with controlled truncation (top-k or p-mass). This allows resource management while retaining high fidelity for specific circuit types.

Method. The simulation uses a sparse state vector representation and applies unitary operations in blocks, followed by mandatory truncation based on either the maximum number of non-zero terms ($k$) or the minimum retained probability mass ($p$).

Model / system. The model is general quantum circuits applied to an $n$-qubit system. The focus is on 'peaked circuits' where the output probability distribution is highly concentrated, allowing for significant state vector sparsity.

Key observables. Fidelity (approximated by retained total probability mass) and computational resources (runtime/memory usage controlled by $k$ or $p$).

Important parameters / regimes. The truncation parameters ($k$ for top-k, $p$ for p-mass), the number of qubits ($n$), and the structure of the unitary gates.

Assumptions / limitations. The approach assumes that the observable quantities are dominated by a small subset of basis states, allowing for effective truncation. The method can fail if probability mass is spread widely despite having a clear peak.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the scaling of simulation time with $k$ (linear scaling) and show how the number of active terms grows during simulation under different truncation regimes ($k$ vs. $p$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses from introducing the problem of state vector growth, detailing the sparse representation, proposing the combined top-k/p-mass truncation strategy, describing the optimized implementation (vectorization, GPU acceleration), and finally analyzing performance scaling.

Abstract

In a class of quantum circuits known as peaked circuits, the goal is to predict the most probable bit string at the output of the circuit. Since these circuits are designed to have a sharp peak in their output distribution, in principle it should be possible to simulate them using a truncated state vector with a limited number of terms, or a fraction of the total probability mass. This approximate simulation can be carried out on a classical computer with a sparse representation that stores only the nonzero amplitudes of the state vector, in contrast to the dense representations that are common in most quantum simulators. For efficiency, all operations on the state vector should be vectorized to the furthest possible extent and, if available, hardware acceleration can also be used. This work describes how these requirements were met in an open-source implementation, and discusses its performance and limitations.
