Graph-theoretic design of lasing networks for physical vision

Authors: Paul Obernolte, Jakub Dranczewski, Yixiu Yin, Tobias Farchy, Wai Kit Ng, Tobias Simonsen, Elias Großhauser, Alexis Arnaudon, Robert L. Peach, Mauricio Barahona, Riccardo Sapienza, Jack C. Gartside, T. V. Raziman

Summary. The paper introduces a novel, efficient framework to design physical neural networks using random lasing structures. By establishing a three-layer link between simple graph metrics and complex lasing physics, they guide an optimization algorithm that yields superior network topologies for vision tasks compared to random designs.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful methodology for designing complex quantum or nonlinear optical systems by abstracting the design space into graph theory, bypassing expensive full-scale physical simulations of many-body dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Optimizing physical neural network designs, which rely on complex nonlinear dynamics, is computationally prohibitive due to the high dimensionality of possible topologies.

Main result. The authors established a quantitative link where simple graph-theoretic metrics can predict and guide the optimization of lasing physics, leading to network topologies that significantly outperform random designs in image classification tasks.

Method. They developed an evolutionary algorithm guided by graph metrics (like clique sizes sum and edge betweenness centrality) to optimize random lasing networks, which are then tested on simulated image-classification benchmarks.

Model / system. The exemplar system is a random lasing network composed of interconnected nanoscale waveguides hosting strongly coupled lasing modes. The analysis maps graph structure metrics onto the nonlinear lasing physics and subsequent vision performance.

Key observables. Graph metrics (e.g., clique sizes sum, median edge betweenness centrality), physical observables (number of lasing modes, inter-mode competition), and vision proxies (Excitation-inhibition metric, Feature detection metric).

Important parameters / regimes. Total edge length ($L$), shot size $S$ for training, and hyperparameters $(k, C)$ for the logistic regression classifier.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that graph properties can predict emergent nonlinear dynamics and learning performance in active, strongly-interacting physical networks, allowing optimization in an abstract graph space rather than simulating substrate physics.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate a three-layer pipeline (Graph $ ightarrow$ Physics $ ightarrow$ Application), schematics of the lasing network, and show correlations between various graph parameters and vision metrics across different network sets.

Paper structure. The paper establishes a theoretical framework linking graph theory to physical optics via random lasing networks. It then uses this link to guide an evolutionary optimization process based on graph metrics, culminating in testing the optimized topologies on simulated image classification tasks using logistic regression.

Abstract

Physical neural networks perform learning through the intrinsic nonlinear dynamics of matter. Optimising their design presents a considerable challenge: complex many-body physics can provide powerful computation, but are expensive to simulate and large experimental optimisation runs are impractical to fabricate. Hence, the high-dimensional space of possible network topologies cannot be effectively directly searched. Here, we show that this search can be efficiently performed in an abstract graph space that is vastly cheaper to explore. Using random lasing networks -- composed of interconnected nanoscale waveguides and hosting strongly coupled lasing modes -- as an exemplar physical vision system, we establish a quantitative three-layer link: simple graph-theoretic metrics predict the nonlinear lasing physics, which in turn predicts vision performance. After validating this relationship using physical simulations, we exploit it to drive an evolutionary algorithm using graph metrics, producing network topologies that outperform random designs at a fraction of the computational cost (3000$\times$ speed-up compared to physical simulation). On simulated image-classification tasks, graph-optimised networks substantially improve classification accuracy. As our framework operates on network topology rather than substrate-specific physics, we anticipate it can transfer to other network-based physical learning systems, providing an efficient route for the directed design and optimisation of complex, strongly-interacting physical neural networks.

Parity Floors in Quantum Denoisers: A Closed-Form Benchmark for Fixed-Map Denoising Networks

Summary. This work introduces a novel benchmark to analyze fixed quantum feature maps used in diffusion denoisers. By decomposing the reconstruction error into even and odd components, the authors prove that the primary limitation (the 'parity floor') is dictated by the target's odd spectral content rather than the map's ability to represent all features. This guides future research toward nonclassical data access methods.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous mathematical tools for diagnosing fundamental limitations in variational quantum algorithms applied to physical processes like diffusion modeling, suggesting that parity constraints are a more fundamental barrier than simple model capacity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to determine which structural constraint limits fixed quantum feature maps when used within diffusion denoisers, distinguishing between representational limitations and other sources of error.

Main result. The excess risk in the denoising process is shown to be dominated by an irreducible 'parity floor,' which is a property of the noise-conditioned target rather than static map capacity.

Method. The authors develop a closed-form benchmark using analytic heat-kernel noising on a torus, decomposing the excess risk into parity and within-sector residual components based on even/odd subspaces.

Model / system. The model involves quantum feature maps (e.g., depth-1 RY+CNOT+Pauli-Z) applied to a diffusion process defined by a Gibbs law prior on the torus ($\mathbb{T}^n$).

Key observables. Excess risk, parity proxy ($\hat{P}_\sigma$), signed empirical residual ($\hat{\delta}_{\sigma,N}$), and the irreducible lower bound $\|\Pi_{ ext{odd}}m_\sigma\|^2$.

Important parameters / regimes. Noise scale ($\sigma$), feature map depth, and the structure of the prior (e.g., 3-body coupling on $\mathbb{T}^n$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the assumption that the excess risk can be decomposed into parity and within-sector components; the results are limited to fixed feature maps.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how quantum excess tracks the calculated parity proxy across varying noise levels, confirming parity dominance over capacity limitations.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes a theoretical decomposition of the risk based on even/odd subspaces. It then applies this framework to specific fixed quantum feature map families (depth-1) and compares performance against classical benchmarks using various priors (torus, signed-XY).

Abstract

Fixed quantum feature maps are increasingly inserted into diffusion denoisers, but standard image benchmarks do not reveal which structural constraint limits them. We introduce CoupledPhaseTexture, a torus-diffusion benchmark with analytic heat-kernel noising that separates parity, within-sector approximation, and sample-complexity limitations. For the depth-1 RY+CNOT+Pauli-Z family we prove a containment-free parity floor: all reachable features are even functions of the encoded angles while the sine components of the Bayes denoiser are odd, so the excess risk splits exactly into an inaccessible odd part and a within-sector residual. The first term is an irreducible, noise-scale-resolved lower bound holding for every even feature class, with no containment, linearity, or closedness assumption on the feature class. The obstruction is a property of the noise-conditioned denoising target rather than static representability: the floor is re-derived at each noise scale because the target's parity content changes with noise. The measured excess is dominated by the parity proxy on two distinct priors. Higher-order Z readouts improve the even sector, but entanglement does not lower the floor and re-uploading does not reliably close it. Classical controls confirm the deficit is parity rather than quantumness: a cosine-only bank is floored similarly, while adding the sine sector matches the reference. Among tested constructions, odd readouts and a noise-coupled encoder do not match the sine-carrying classical bank. These results motivate nonclassical data access or feature classes without efficient classical surrogates; they do not establish either as sufficient for quantum advantage.

Summary. This paper connects the mathematical classification of local operator algebras (von Neumann algebras) to computational resources. It argues that systems whose local structure requires a Type III algebra—typical in QFTs—must inherently possess an infinite amount of 'magic' resource for their description to be mathematically consistent.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a deep connection between abstract algebraic structures (Type III factors) and concrete physical requirements for computation/simulation, which is highly relevant to understanding quantum field theories and holographic duality.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The work establishes a fundamental link between the mathematical structure of local von Neumann algebras (specifically Type III) and the required quantum resources ('magic') for simulating physical systems in the thermodynamic limit.

Main result. It is proven that Type III von Neumann algebras fundamentally require an infinite amount of 'magic' resource, linking algebraic classification to computational complexity.

Method. The analysis uses the formalism of von Neumann algebras and examines the inductive limits of finite lattice systems (thermodynamic limit) while quantifying resources using the concept of 'magic' gates.

Model / system. The study considers quantum field theories, quantum gravity models, and general lattice systems analyzed in their thermodynamic limit. The mathematical framework relies on local von Neumann algebras describing these infinite-dimensional systems.

Key observables. Magic (number of non-Clifford gates), Type III von Neumann Algebras, Local Subregions in QFTs.

Important parameters / regimes. Thermodynamic limit ($N o \infty$), Bounded vs. Infinite Magic Resource.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the formal structure of local algebras and assumes that the thermodynamic limit can be properly constructed via inductive limits of finite systems.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically represents a nested sequence of lattices ($\Lambda_N$) partitioning into subregions, leading to an inductive limit algebra $A$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by defining the mathematical tools (von Neumann algebras, magic resource) and then constructing the argument: showing that bounded local resources preclude the existence of Type III algebras in the infinite limit.

Abstract

The number of non-Clifford gates needed to perform a task, or simply \textit{magic}, is a resource for fault-tolerant quantum computation. Von Neumann algebras provide a formal mathematical structure to describe infinite-dimensional quantum systems, such as those in quantum field theory or quantum statistical mechanics. A particularly important class of von Neumann algebras is called Type III algebras, for which the standard notions of finite-dimensional systems such as density matrices and traces break down. In this work, we argue that Type III von Neumann algebras fundamentally require an infinite amount of magic. Specifically, we consider the thermodynamic limit of finite-dimensional quantum systems, such as lattice systems, and show that if the states in the thermodynamic limit possess only a bounded amount of magic, the resulting local von Neumann algebra cannot be of Type III. Our result has direct implications for the quantum simulations of quantum field theories, for which the algebra of a local subregion is known to be of Type III.

Summary. The paper analyzes the distribution of basis-independent coherence and quantum correlations in two particles coupled by gravity within double-well potentials. By decomposing total coherence into collective and localized parts, it demonstrates that local coherence is more resilient to temperature effects than inter-particle correlations. This highlights a resource hierarchy where local superpositions persist longer than globally entangled states as the system heats up.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed resource quantification framework for non-local quantum resources (coherence) in a physically relevant setting (gravity), showing how local versus global entanglement survives thermalization differently.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how quantum correlations and basis-independent coherence are distributed between collective (inter-particle) and localized (intra-particle) components in a system interacting via gravity.

Main result. Localized coherence ($C_L$) is found to be more robust against thermal fluctuations than the collective coherence ($C_C$), while increasing gravitational coupling ($\Delta$) preferentially enhances $C_C$.

Method. The analysis uses quantum information measures like Bures distance, Quantum Discord (QD), and a basis-independent measure of coherence derived from the Quantum Jensen–Shannon Divergence (QJSD).

Model / system. The system consists of two massive particles confined in a double-well potential and coupled by their mutual Newtonian gravitational interaction. The dynamics are governed by a bipartite Hamiltonian involving parameters for single-particle energy ($w$) and coupling strength ($\Delta$).

Key observables. Total coherence ($C_T$), Collective coherence ($C_C$), Localized coherence ($C_L$), Bures entanglement, Quantum Discord.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$ or $eta$), Gravitational coupling ($\Delta$), Single-particle energy scale ($w$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on a two-level approximation for the potential eigenstates and assumes thermal equilibrium with a reservoir characterized by temperature $T$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how entanglement (Bures measure) and Quantum Discord vary with $T$, $\Delta$, and $w$; coherence measures show distinct dependencies, confirming that $C_L$ is more robust than $C_C$ against thermal noise.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the physical model and Hamiltonian, defines various quantum correlation and coherence metrics ($C_T = C_C + C_L$), and then systematically analyzes the dependence of these measures on temperature, gravitational coupling ($\Delta$), and single-particle energy scale ($w$).

Abstract

We study the distribution of quantum correlations and basis-independent coherence in a pair of massive particles confined in a double-well potential and coupled through their mutual Newtonian gravitational interaction. Non-classical correlations are characterized using Bures distance of entanglement and quantum discord, while coherence is quantified through the square root of the quantum Jensen--Shannon divergence (QJSD) from the maximally mixed state, yielding a measure that is invariant under arbitrary unitary transformations and is therefore genuinely basis-independent. The total coherence $C_T$ decomposes into two operationally distinct contributions: the collective coherence $C_C$, which captures quantum correlations between the two subsystems, and the localized coherence $C_L$, which captures the intrinsic quantum coherence of each individual subsystem. We analyze how temperature $T$, the gravitational coupling $Δ$, and the single-particle energy scale $w$ govern the redistribution of coherence between its collective and localized components. Our results show that $C_L$ is more robust against thermal fluctuations than $C_C$, and that increasing $Δ$ preferentially enhances collective coherence by strengthening gravitationally induced inter-particle correlations.

Summary. The paper investigates a three-component quantum walker whose dynamics are controlled by an SU(3) coin operator. By tuning the coupling parameters, researchers map out a complex phase diagram that allows for engineering specific transport behaviors. This demonstrates a powerful method to control spreading and localization in multi-component quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a general framework for engineering quantum transport using internal degrees of freedom (the qutrit components) rather than relying solely on external lattice geometry or topological constraints.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to systematically tune and control the transport properties of a multi-component quantum walker by manipulating pairwise couplings within its coin operator.

Main result. The system exhibits a rich phase diagram in parameter space, allowing for engineered regimes ranging from rapid ballistic spreading to strongly anisotropic dynamics with partial localization.

Method. Analysis involves simulating the time evolution of the state using an SU(3) coin operator and quantifying transport via spatio-temporal probability distributions and side-resolved mean/variance ratios.

Model / system. The model is a discrete-time quantum walk of a three-component particle (qutrit) on a 1D lattice. The evolution is governed by $U = S C(\alpha, eta, \gamma)$, where the coin operator $C$ uses Gell-Mann matrices to couple internal components.

Key observables. Spatio-temporal probability distribution $P(x, t)$; ballistic spreading rate $m(eta, \gamma)$; side-resolved mean position ratios ($R_X$) and variance ratios ($R_\sigma$).

Important parameters / regimes. The coupling parameters $(\alpha, eta, \gamma)$, particularly exploring regimes near $eta \sim\pm\pi/2$ and $\gamma \sim\pm\pi/2$.

Abstract

We investigate a discrete-time quantum walk of a three-component quantum particle on a one-dimensional lattice. As coin operators, we employ parameterized rotations generated by the Gell-Mann matrices, which enable systematic tuning of the couplings between the internal components. We analyze how these couplings influence and control the dynamics by systematically exploring the position-space probability distribution across a broad region of the parameter space. To quantify the impact of different inter-component couplings, we further examine the ratio of the mean position to the variance in each half of the lattice. Our results indicates that the system supports a rich variety of transport regimes, ranging from nearly symmetric, rapidly spreading walks to strongly anisotropic dynamics with partial localization. This framework thus provides a new avenue for engineering targeted spreading and trapping behavior in multicomponent discrete-time quantum walks.

Summary. This work investigates how structural defects, specifically antisite mixing, modify the magnetic properties of topological magnetic insulators like $ ext{MnSb}_2 ext{Te}_4$. By combining inelastic neutron scattering with spin-dynamics simulations, the authors demonstrate that these defects induce a strong, measurable antiferromagnetic coupling responsible for an observable optical magnon gap. This highlights defect engineering as a powerful tool to tune fundamental magnetic interactions in quantum materials.

Why it may be interesting. The study provides a concrete example of how disorder (antisite defects) fundamentally alters the magnetic Hamiltonian and excitation spectrum of a quantum material, which is relevant for understanding engineered correlated states in condensed matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to directly probe defect-induced magnetic interactions and understand how antisite mixing affects the spin dynamics of topological magnetic insulators.

Main result. Antisite mixing generates disordered sublattices, leading to a distinct optical magnon mode with a large gap ($\Delta' \approx 1.5$ meV), indicating strong defect-mediated antiferromagnetic coupling ($J'$).

Method. The research combines inelastic neutron scattering (INS) experiments on single crystals with semi-classical spin-dynamics simulations using Landau-Lifshitz Dynamics (LLD).

Model / system. The physical system is $ ext{MnSb}_2 ext{Te}_4$, a topological magnetic insulator in the $ ext{Mn}( ext{Bi}, ext{Sb})_2 ext{Te}_4$ family, which exhibits significant antisite defects. The analysis uses spin Hamiltonians incorporating various exchange couplings ($J_0, J_c, J'$).

Figures summary. Figures show INS data (constant energy slices, in-plane dispersion) for MST compared to MBT, illustrating the distinct magnetic excitation spectrum caused by defects.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the problem of defect influence in topological insulators to presenting experimental INS measurements, followed by theoretical modeling using spin Hamiltonians and LLD simulations to quantify the defect-induced couplings.

Abstract

Magnetic defect engineering offers a route to manipulate and control magnetic and electronic states in quantum materials. In the Mn(Bi,Sb)2Te4 family of topological magnetic insulators, Sb substitution facilitates site mixing between Mn and Sb atoms that affects magnetic order and band topology. Here we directly probe these defect-induced magnetic interactions using inelastic neutron scattering on single crystals of MnSb2Te4. We find that antisite mixing generates inequivalent and disordered magnetic sublattices where strong defect-induced antiferromagnetic coupling produces an optical magnon with a large gap. Semi-classical spin-dynamics simulations accurately capture magnetic excitations in the ordered and paramagnetic states and identify that linear Mn-Te-Mn bonds mediate coupling to antisite magnetic defects.

Summary. This paper introduces SPLIT-Q, a scalable quantum framework designed to solve large power system islanding problems. It overcomes the exponential resource scaling limitations of monolithic quantum formulations by employing regionalization and sequential solving techniques within QAOA. The results demonstrate that this method can accurately find optimal partitions on real quantum hardware for very large networks while maintaining a fixed, manageable qubit footprint.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on power grids, the core methodology—decoupling exponentially scaling combinatorial problems into fixed-width, sequentially solvable quantum subproblems—is highly relevant to developing scalable quantum algorithms for general hard optimization problems in condensed matter physics or materials science.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the computational challenge of solving large-scale controlled islanding problems in power grids, which is a complex combinatorial optimization task.

Main result. A novel sequential distributed QAOA framework was proposed that successfully solves these large problems on current quantum hardware by keeping the required qubit width fixed, unlike monolithic approaches whose complexity scales exponentially with network size.

Method. The method formulates the problem as boundary-conditioned regional QUBO subproblems solved sequentially. This approach partitions the optimization variables into manageable regions to fit within limited quantum resources.

Model / system. The physical system is an electrical power grid modeled as a weighted graph (buses and transmission lines). The goal is to partition this network into electrically sustainable 'islands' by minimizing the total weight of cut edges.

Key observables. Disruption cost (cut value in MW), quantum resource demand (two-qubit gate count $G$, circuit depth $d$), and solution quality compared against classical solvers like Gurobi.

Important parameters / regimes. Network size (up to 300 buses), fixed qubit budget ($Q_{\max}$), and the scaling factor $\chi$ which measures width reduction relative to network size.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that regionalization successfully decouples the quantum resource requirement from the total network size, allowing fixed-width devices to solve arbitrarily large systems.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the workflow of partitioning free buses into regions for sequential QAOA sampling and show reconstructed islanding solutions on various IEEE benchmark systems.

Paper structure. The paper first defines the physical problem (controlled islanding) using graph theory and optimization formulations. It then critiques monolithic quantum approaches due to scaling issues, proposes the scalable regionalized framework, details its implementation via sequential QUBO solving, and finally validates it by comparing results across various IEEE benchmark systems on real hardware.

Abstract

Growing integration of distributed energy resources increases power-system variability and uncertainty. During disturbances, these effects can intensify generation-load imbalances and cascading failures. Controlled islanding limits their propagation by partitioning a compromised grid into connected, electrically sustainable islands. However, classical methods face rapidly growing computational costs as network size and island count increase. Quantum optimization offers an alternative for exploring this combinatorial partition space. Yet monolithic quantum formulations encode all assignment decisions in one circuit, causing qubit demand and circuit complexity to scale with network size. In this study, a qubit-bounded sequential distributed quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) framework is proposed to tackle coherent controlled islanding under limited quantum resources. It formulates the optimization as boundary-conditioned regional quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) subproblems that are solved sequentially within a fixed qubit budget. Thus, circuit width remains independent of network size, with aggregate quantum workload scaling linearly on bounded-degree networks. Evaluation covers eleven IEEE systems from 9 to 300 buses using IBM quantum computing resources, with Gurobi and monolithic QAOA as references. Across all systems, the framework recovers feasible Gurobi-optimal partitions under noise, confirming the resilience of its solution quality. The results further show that the proposed method substantially reduces quantum-resource demand and circuit complexity relative to monolithic QAOA, allowing large islanding problems to be addressed within current hardware limits. The proposed framework provides a feasible and scalable pathway for quantum optimization in large-scale power systems.

Ambient unitaries don't enable shallow group designs

Authors: Maxwell West, M. Cerezo, Martin Larocca

Summary. This paper proves that achieving approximate unitary designs over several important symmetry groups requires circuit depths that scale linearly with system size, even when utilizing unitaries from outside the defined subgroup. This result closes a loophole in prior work and has major implications for quantum information theory by setting fundamental depth overheads for benchmarking protocols.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous theoretical constraints on the resource overhead required for practical quantum characterization protocols (like tomography or benchmarking), directly impacting hardware feasibility assessments.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the fundamental limits on how efficiently approximate unitary designs can be constructed when sampling from specific subgroups of the full unitary group, particularly when using local nearest-neighbor gates.

Main result. It proves that these limitations hold even if 'ambient' unitaries (from outside the subgroup) are used, implying that many natural quantum tomography and benchmarking schemes suffer from a dramatic circuit depth overhead compared to ideal protocols.

Method. The analysis uses lower bounds derived from comparing the $k$-th moment channel fidelity ($\Phi^{(k)}$) against the Haar measure using invariant projectors associated with maximally entangled states.

Model / system. The study focuses on various quantum groups, including the matchgate, orthogonal $ ext{O}(d)$, symplectic $ ext{SP}(d/2)$, and Clifford groups, applied to nearest-neighbor gates on a one-dimensional lattice.

Key observables. Approximate $k$-design fidelity ($\epsilon$), circuit depth ($L$), and the diamond distance between moment channels.

Important parameters / regimes. The required depth scales linearly with system size ($n$) for all tested groups, establishing an $\Omega(n)$ lower bound.

Assumptions / limitations. The core contribution is extending previous results by showing that the limitation holds even when unitaries used are not strictly confined to the target subgroup (i.e., using ambient unitaries).

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates how sublinear-depth designs over groups with invariant states are disallowed via lightcone arguments.

Paper structure. The paper establishes a general lower bound on design fidelity based on invariant projectors, specializes this for pure entangled states, and then applies these theorems to specific quantum groups (matchgate, orthogonal, symplectic, Clifford) to prove the necessary linear depth scaling.

Abstract

Characterising the efficiency with which designs over various subsets of the unitary group may be constructed is an important goal of quantum information theory. While it is now known that approximate unitary designs can be realised in depth logarithmic in the system size, it has recently been shown that ensembles of local nearest-neighbour sublinear-depth one-dimensional circuits over the matchgate, orthogonal, and symplectic groups cannot form approximate 2-designs over their parent groups; similarly, sublinear-depth ensembles of Cliffords cannot form a Clifford 4-design. In this note we show that this remarkable exponential separation is not merely an artefact of restricting to ensembles consisting of unitaries from the subgroups themselves, but rather that no ensemble of local nearest-neighbour sublinear-depth unitaries can realise approximate designs in the aforementioned cases, even when employing "ambient" unitaries from beyond the subgroup itself (possibly acting on ancilla qubits). This implies that various natural tomography and benchmarking schemes which involves sampling from these groups suffer from a dramatic circuit depth overhead compared to similar protocols which involve sampling from the full unitary group. We additionally conclude that, in all of the above cases, the known linear-depth design constructions are up to constant factors optimal.

Correlation versus Causation in Quantum Criticality

Authors: Conrad Wichmann, Ryan Thorngren, Ruben Verresen
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.12770v1

Summary. This work introduces 'causation,' defined as the static susceptibility, as an alternative to standard correlation functions for studying quantum criticality. It demonstrates that causation decays much faster than correlation due to mathematical constraints involving time derivatives. This difference allows researchers to pinpoint fundamental physical operators and accurately predict phenomena like edge-mode splittings in topological phases.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful new diagnostic tool for critical systems that can distinguish between physical influences (causation) and mere statistical correlations, which is crucial for understanding emergent phenomena in strongly interacting quantum matter.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper contrasts standard correlation functions with a novel measure called 'causation' (static susceptibility) to investigate how physical influences decay at quantum critical points.

Main result. Causation can exhibit significantly faster decay rates than correlations, revealing physics—such as specific primary operators or edge-mode splittings—that are otherwise masked by the slower decaying correlation terms.

Method. The analysis employs conformal field theory (CFT) techniques, resolvent calculations involving Green's functions, and numerical methods like DMRG to compare scaling laws for both observables.

Model / system. The study applies to various critical systems, including the $(2+1)$D Ising model, gapless symmetry-protected topological (SPT) phases in 1D, and general quantum critical points described by CFTs.

Key observables. Correlation functions $\langle O_1(x) O_2(0) angle$ vs. Causation/Static Susceptibility $\lim_{\lambda o 0} \partial_\lambda \langle A(x) angle_\lambda$.

Important parameters / regimes. Dimensionality (1+1D, 2+1D), scaling exponents ($\Delta$), and the perturbation strength ($\lambda$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on identifying operators that are not time derivatives to correctly calculate causation, contrasting this with standard correlation measurements.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate numerical comparisons of correlation vs. causation decay rates for various Ising variants in (1+1)D and show the scaling of edge-mode splittings derived from bulk causal influence in SPTs.

Paper structure. The paper systematically introduces the concept of causation, derives its theoretical scaling predictions via CFT, applies this framework to analyze boundary effects in 1D SPTs, and demonstrates how it reveals hidden primary operators in higher dimensions.

Abstract

Correlation functions $\langle O_1(x) O_2(0) \rangle$ reveal scaling dimensions through spatial decay. We instead consider static susceptibility, the change in $\langle O_1(x) \rangle$ from perturbing the Hamiltonian by $O_2(0)$, which we term causation for short. In a conformal field theory (CFT), dimensional analysis predicts decay of $|x|^{-2Δ}$ for correlation and $|x|^{-2Δ+1}$ for causation. Furthermore, we show that the different phases can be characterized by several topological invariants: the conventional momentum-space Chern number of the underlying two-dimensional host system, corresponding to a Chern insulator; the B-space Chern number, defined by coupling the impurity spin to a fictitious local magnetic field $\boldsymbol B$, in the limit $B \to 0$; and the S-space Chern number, defined for a classical impurity spin, i.e., a vector of fixed length. The classical-spin limit is obtained for $B \to \infty$. By varying the field strength, we can therefore continuously interpolate between quantum-impurity-spin and classical-impurity-spin Hamiltonians and investigate whether the corresponding phase diagrams are likewise continuously connected. The gapped underscreened Kondo effect is studied for an impurity spin $S>\frac12$ as well as in the classical-spin limit approached via $B \to \infty$ or $S \to \infty$. Different variants of scattering theory are employed to interpret the resulting phases. Finally, the gapped two-channel overscreened Kondo effect, realized by coupling a quantum impurity spin equally to the local electron spins of both orbitals within a unit cell, is shown to be characterized by spontaneous particle-hole symmetry breaking. This leads to a highly nontrivial quantum-classical phase diagram.

Abstract

It has remained an open question whether a genuinely unextendible product basis (GUPB) exists. We resolve this problem by constructing an explicit three-qutrit GUPB of cardinality fourteen in the smallest tripartite Hilbert space in which a GUPB can exist. Together with the nonexistence of three-qutrit GUPBs of cardinality less than fourteen, our construction proves that fourteen is the minimum cardinality. A padding procedure further extends the construction to all tripartite systems whose local dimensions are at least three. As applications, the normalized projector onto the thirteen-dimensional orthogonal complement of the three-qutrit GUPB is positive under partial transposition and bound entangled across every bipartition, while the GUPB exhibits strong quantum nonlocality without entanglement.

Abstract

As quantum computing progresses from proof-of-principle demonstrations toward practical utility, a significant impediment is the need to augment algorithmic feasibility with system-level optimization across heterogeneous hardware and software stacks. Quantum resource estimation (QRE) plays a central role in this transition, yet existing approaches remain largely compilation-heavy or domain-knowledge-guided symbolic annotations, and tightly coupled to long-term fault-tolerant assumptions, limiting their topical applicability. In this work, we introduce AutoQuREO, an Automated framework for full-stack Quantum Resource Estimation and Optimization. AutoQuREO is built around four core novelties: (i) a flexible, user-defined abstraction of the quantum computing stack; (ii) a modular library of reusable stack components enabling rapid full-stack prototyping; (iii) surrogate modeling of layer-wise resources via algorithmic profiling and neuro-symbolic learning; and (iv) integrated multi-objective optimization that embeds QRE directly into deployment pipelines. Together, these design choices enable AutoQuREO to serve as a digital twin for quantum computing stacks, supporting the tractable exploration of complex design spaces. We demonstrate the capabilities of AutoQuREO through representative co-design case studies, including early-fault-tolerant quantum algorithms, small error correction codes, gate decomposition and variational training of parametric quantum circuits. These examples illustrate how AutoQuREO enables systematic discovery of unexploited resource trade-offs that are computationally intractable or abstruse using existing QRE tools. AutoQuREO is positioned as a general-purpose platform for advancing quantum technology readiness.
