Summary. The paper introduces a quantum-classical hybrid solver to overcome the computational bottleneck of analyzing resonance instabilities in massive power grids. By adapting the RVVQE algorithm for non-Hermitian matrices, it maps the grid's admittance matrix onto a QPU. This scalable approach promises to diagnose continental-scale electrical network failures currently beyond classical computing limits.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly applies advanced quantum algorithms (RVVQE) to a complex engineering problem involving open quantum systems dynamics, bridging theoretical quantum computation with large-scale physical infrastructure analysis.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Evaluating critical resonance modes of large-scale interacting power grids is computationally intractable for classical methods due to the need to diagonalize massive, non-Hermitian admittance matrices.

Main result. The proposed quantum-classical hybrid methodology successfully extracts critical resonance modal impedances with near-perfect accuracy compared to classical solutions on test systems.

Method. A novel Quantum-Classical Hybrid Resonance Mode Analysis (Q-RMA) framework is used, employing the Real Variance-based Variational Quantum Eigensolver (RVVQE) algorithm to handle the non-Hermitian nature of the grid admittance matrix ($\mathbf{Y}_{B,f}$).

Model / system. The system modeled is a multi-terminal power transmission grid, represented mathematically by its frequency-dependent, non-Hermitian admittance matrix ($\mathbf{Y}_{B,f}$), which governs dynamic instabilities.

Key observables. Critical resonance modal impedances ($Z_{cm,f}$) and eigenvalues of the admittance matrix across a wide frequency spectrum (100 Hz to 3000 Hz).

Important parameters / regimes. The size of the grid ($n$-bus system), which dictates the required number of qubits ($\lceil\log_2 n ceil$).

Assumptions / limitations. Standard quantum eigensolvers are insufficient because they only handle Hermitian matrices, necessitating the use of RVVQE for the non-Hermitian $\mathbf{Y}_{B,f}$.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the hybrid framework mapping $\mathbf{Y}_{B,f}$ onto a QPU using RVVQE. Figure 2 shows the iterative workflow (Q-RMA) across frequency sweeps. Figure 4 compares quantum RVVQE results against exact classical solutions for $|Z_{cm}|$.

Paper structure. The paper outlines the problem (harmonic distortions in grids), proposes the Q-RMA methodology involving matrix decomposition ($\mathbf{Y}_{B,f} = \mathbf{H}_f + i\mathbf{K}_f$), details the RVVQE implementation for non-Hermitian operators, and validates the approach by comparing quantum results to classical benchmarks.

Abstract

The proliferation of power electronics in multi-terminal transmission grids has increasingly led to harmonic distortions and dynamic instabilities. While Resonance Mode Analysis (RMA) provides deep insights into these system resonances, evaluating the critical modes of large-scale grids presents a severe computational bottleneck. Classical iterative techniques must continuously diagonalize massively high-dimensional, non-Hermitian admittance matrices across a wide frequency spectrum, a process that rapidly exhausts classical memory and processing limits. To overcome this scaling barrier, we propose a novel quantum-classical hybrid methodology that natively maps the transmission grid's admittance matrix onto a Quantum Processing Unit (QPU). Because the grid's matrix is non-Hermitian, standard quantum eigensolvers are insufficient; thus, we employ the Real Variance-based Variational Quantum Eigensolver (RVVQE) algorithm to accurately extract the complex eigenvalues that represent the system's modes. Validated against a standard 5-bus transmission system, the quantum-derived critical-resonance modal impedances demonstrate near-perfect alignment with the exact classical frequency responses. Crucially, by encoding the grid's state logarithmically into quantum memory, this methodology bypasses classical RAM limitations. The successful implementation of the RVVQE framework not only bridges the mathematical topologies of dissipative electrical grids and open quantum systems but also provides a profoundly scalable architecture capable of diagnosing resonance instabilities in massive, continental-scale networks that currently exceed classical computational boundaries.

Qubit Loss Inference with Stabilizer Codes without Leakage Detection Units

Summary. This paper proposes an alternative to using dedicated Leakage Detection Units (LDUs) to handle qubit loss in fault-tolerant quantum computing. By analyzing the syndrome data obtained from standard stabilizer measurements, the authors derive a mathematical condition based on anticommutation relations that proves when and how qubit loss can be inferred. This method promises reduced space-time overhead while maintaining low logical error rates for near-term hardware.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a purely theoretical framework for mitigating physical errors in quantum computation by transforming hardware overheads (LDUs) into algorithmic/measurement-based diagnostics derived from fundamental algebraic properties of stabilizer codes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of qubit loss during quantum computation, aiming to infer the location of lost qubits directly from syndrome measurements without relying on dedicated hardware like Leakage Detection Units (LDUs).

Main result. It derives a sufficient condition for loss detectability based on the anticommutation of punctured stabilizer checks and shows that inference-based protocols can reduce logical error rates compared to LDU baselines in near-term regimes with less overhead.

Method. The authors develop theoretical criteria by analyzing the commutation relations between syndrome generators after accounting for lost qubits, formulating the problem as a maximum-likelihood/minimum set cover problem, and simulating performance on the surface code.

Model / system. The work is situated in the context of fault-tolerant quantum computation using stabilizer codes. The analysis models qubit loss under a non-entangling gate assumption for lost qubits, evaluating protocols on the rotated surface code.

Key observables. Logical error rate, anticommutation relations between punctured stabilizer checks ($s'_i s'_j = -s'_j s'_i$), and auxiliary qubit overhead.

Important parameters / regimes. Low-to-moderate loss rates relevant to near-term hardware; the structure of the underlying stabilizer code (e.g., surface code).

Assumptions / limitations. The gate model assumes non-entangling behavior for gates involving a lost qubit, and the analysis relies on establishing sufficient conditions rather than necessary ones.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate flow diagrams comparing LDU vs. inference protocols, and show how commutation/anticommutation relations change based on the number of lost qubits relative to stabilizer generators.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the hardware overhead of LDUs to deriving a mathematical condition for loss detectability via syndrome data (based on anticommutation), formalizing this into an inference problem, and finally validating the protocol through noise simulations on specific platforms.

Abstract

Qubit loss occurs when the physical carrier of a qubit leaves the computational system without directly revealing the event's location. Such errors are a major obstacle to fault-tolerant quantum computation on platforms including photonic, neutral-atom, and trapped-ion systems. Loss locations are commonly identified using additional hardware operations such as leakage-detection units (LDUs), which introduce space-time overhead and may themselves become a source of error. We investigate whether qubit loss on stabilizer codes can instead be inferred from syndrome data obtained through standard repeated stabilizer measurements. Under a non-entangling model for gates involving a lost qubit, we derive a sufficient condition for loss detectability in general stabilizer codes. The condition is based on the emergence of anticommutation between stabilizer checks after their support on the lost qubits is removed. By using that condition, we formulate the exact loss-inference problem using the observed set of non-deterministic checks together with its maximum-likelihood formulation. We then relax the problem to the minimum set cover problem with a greedy heuristic algorithm. We evaluate the resulting inference and loss-correction protocols on the rotated surface code via circuit-level noise simulations for trapped-ion and neutral-atom platforms. On both platforms, inference-based and adaptive protocols reduce the logical error rate relative to a noisy-LDU baseline in the low-to-moderate loss-rate regime relevant to near-term hardware, while requiring fewer space-time overheads.

Summary. This paper introduces the Transcorrelated Random-Phase Approximation (TC-RPA) to enhance electronic structure calculations by incorporating explicit three-body correlation effects via a Jastrow factor. It demonstrates that TC-RPA provides substantial accuracy improvements for ground-state energies in atoms and molecules, making it an efficient tool for ground-state energetics. However, the authors caution that state-specific optimization is needed to accurately model excited states.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles highly correlated electronic structure problems using advanced many-body techniques (RPA extensions), which is central to understanding chemical bonding and material properties relevant to AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is improving electronic structure calculations by addressing the slow basis set convergence of standard Random-Phase Approximation (RPA) due to short-range electron correlation effects.

Main result. The Transcorrelated RPA (TC-RPA) significantly improves accuracy and accelerates basis set convergence for ground-state correlation energies, but only shows marginal improvement for vertical excitation energies.

Method. The authors extend the RPA formalism to a non-Hermitian Transcorrelated (TC) Hamiltonian by incorporating three-body interactions via a Jastrow correlation factor. They apply this using both direct and exchange variants of the approximation.

Model / system. Calculations are performed for atoms (He, Ne) and small molecules (H2O, NH3, CH4, H2CO). The theory utilizes an Equation-of-Motion approach applied to the non-Hermitian TC Hamiltonian, requiring a biorthogonal formalism.

Key observables. Ground-state correlation energies and vertical excitation energies.

Important parameters / regimes. The Jastrow correlation factor, which is optimized for the ground state.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation noted is that optimizing the Jastrow factor only for the ground state limits its effectiveness in describing excited states.

Figures summary. Not specified by notes; however, a comparison figure mentioned suggests TC-RPA accelerates basis set convergence compared to standard RPA calculations for ground-state energies.

Paper structure. The paper develops the formalism by extending RPA to the non-Hermitian TC Hamiltonian. It then applies this method to calculate correlation and excitation energies for various molecules, culminating in a comparison of results showing differential improvements between ground and excited states.

Abstract

We extend the random-phase approximation (RPA) to the non-Hermitian transcorrelated (TC) Hamiltonian, which explicitly includes three-body interactions generated by a Jastrow correlation factor. We consider both the direct RPA (dRPA) and RPA with exchange (RPAx). We apply the resulting TC-dRPA and TC-RPAx methods to calculate ground-state correlation energies and vertical excitation energies for atoms (\ce{He} and \ce{Ne}) and small molecules (\ce{H2O}, \ce{NH3}, \ce{CH4}, and \ce{H2CO}). For ground-state correlation energies, the TC treatment substantially improves accuracy and accelerates basis set convergence, reducing errors by nearly an order of magnitude relative to conventional RPA calculations. By contrast, it yields only marginal improvements in vertical excitation energies. We attribute this limited effect to the ground-state optimization of the Jastrow factor, which does not adequately capture the distinct electronic character of excited states. These results establish TC-RPA as an accurate and computationally efficient approach to ground-state energetics, while highlighting the need for state-specific Jastrow optimization to achieve reliable descriptions of excited states.

Abstract

Abstract

Understanding the evolution of magnetic order in the two-dimensional limit remains a central challenge in van der Waals magnets, where thermodynamic measurements are constrained by the femtogram-scale mass of exfoliated flakes. Here, we use microsecond pulse-heating nanocalorimetry to measure the heat capacity and magnetic entropy of CrSBr flakes down to the monolayer limit. The measurements reveal the entropy landscape associated with magnetic ordering, uncovering a reduction of the interlayer transition temperature and an entropy-derived effective magnetic moment approaching the monolayer limit.
