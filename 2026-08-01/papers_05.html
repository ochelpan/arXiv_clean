Pauli Encodings & Unclonable Encryption

Summary. This paper analyzes the security of Pauli Encoding schemes designed for unclonable encryption by studying the associated cloning game. Using advanced mathematical tools like moment matrix hierarchies and representation theory, the authors derive tight lower and upper bounds on the adversary's winning probability. The work provides strong theoretical constraints on what quantum cryptography can achieve in preventing state cloning.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous application of representation theory (symmetric group characters, Specht modules) to bound quantum information security parameters is highly relevant for theoretical physicists working on fundamental limits in quantum communication.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the security of quantum encryption schemes against an adversary attempting to clone or distinguish encrypted states, addressing the Unclonable Bit Question.

Main result. It establishes universal lower bounds on the winning probability for Pauli Encodings and derives strong upper bounds (e.g., $\le 0.5556$) using advanced SDP relaxations for specific families of encodings.

Method. The authors employ techniques from quantum information theory, including analyzing the monogamy-of-entanglement game and utilizing moment matrix hierarchies (NPA) combined with representation theory to derive rigorous bounds.

Model / system. The core model is Pauli Encodings, where ciphertexts are normalized eigenspace projectors of Pauli strings acting on a quantum system. The analysis involves studying the security against adversaries in cloning games.

Key observables. Monogamy-of-Entanglement (MoE) winning probability ($P_{ ext{win MoE}}$), optimal cloning probability ($P^*_{ ext{win cloning}}$), and derived block matrices $B(K)_{\lambda,\epsilon}(g)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of Pauli strings ($K$), the system dimension ($2^n$), and the level of the moment relaxation (e.g., Level One, Two, Three).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on establishing equivalences between positive semidefiniteness of moment matrices and derived block matrices, and assumes certain structures for Pauli string families.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes; the structure suggests mathematical derivations leading to explicit SDPs at various levels.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the security problem (Unclonable Bit Question) using Pauli Encodings, deriving general lower bounds, establishing no-go results via pairwise marginals, and finally applying advanced moment/representation theory techniques (NPA hierarchy) to derive tight upper bounds.

Abstract

The unclonable bit question asks whether quantum encryption can prevent an adversary from producing two systems that both reveal the plaintext once the key is disclosed. We introduce and study Pauli Encodings, a simple class of one-bit encryption schemes whose ciphertexts are normalized eigenspace projectors of Pauli strings. For every Pauli Encoding with K Pauli strings, we prove a universal lower bound $1/2+1/(2\sqrt{K})$ on the optimal monogamy-of-entanglement winning probability, together with sharper bounds for several structured families. We then establish two limitations of natural approaches to unclonable security. First, if the Pauli strings are restricted to strings of X and Z of length n, the encoding is not secure. Second, we identify a universal 3/4 obstruction showing that arguments based only on pairwise guessing marginals cannot establish unclonable-indistinguishable security. When the Pauli strings all pairwise anticommute, the protocol becomes the one studied in [Quantum 10, 2157 (2026)]. We exploit the symmetry of this protocol to solve the third level of the natural semidefinite programming relaxation obtaining an asymptotic upper bound of approximately 0.5556 on the winning probability. Finally, we prove strong unclonable-indistinguishable security against bounded-local-dimension adversaries and strong indistinguishability security for several efficient Pauli families. First-level NPA computations provide additional numerical evidence towards the strong unclonable-indistinguishable security.

Summary. This work introduces a novel, computationally efficient spectral framework to model mechanical energy dissipation in large viscoelastic metamaterials. By optimizing the cross-sectional areas of the network rods, the authors demonstrate that maximum energy absorption is achieved not randomly, but through an engineered gradient structure dictated by the material's inherent attenuation length scale.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical mechanics, the use of spectral methods (Laplacian) and optimization principles in complex, disordered structures shares conceptual overlap with analyzing energy flow or correlation decay in quantum many-body systems or disordered quantum media.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is identifying optimal architectural designs within large, disordered viscoelastic truss networks to maximize mechanical energy dissipation for applications like vibration isolation.

Main result. Optimal architectures are not random but exhibit a gradient structure where material properties decay from the source according to the intrinsic attenuation length scale of the base material.

Method. The authors developed an efficient graph Laplacian-based spectral framework that retains full continuum dynamics, allowing optimization via gradient descent on cross-sectional areas.

Model / system. The system is a viscoelastic truss network modeled by interconnected rods, where each rod's dynamics are treated as a one-dimensional continuum element governed by linear viscoelasticity principles.

Key observables. Energy dissipation rate (Q), optimal cross-sectional area distribution ($ ho_{net}(z)$), and the intrinsic attenuation length scale ($\xi$).

Important parameters / regimes. The frequency of excitation ($\omega$), the material's intrinsic attenuation length ($\xi$), and the total available material cost/area ($ ext{A}_{total}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes linear viscoelasticity governed by the Boltzmann superposition principle, and that the problem can be efficiently mapped onto a spectral framework based on the network Laplacian.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison between energy dissipation profiles for random versus optimized cross-sections, showing the characteristic gradient decay in optimal designs. Later figures confirm the robustness of these scaling laws under geometric perturbations.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the spectral framework for viscoelastic networks using the graph Laplacian. It then uses optimization techniques to show that maximizing energy dissipation requires a specific, non-random distribution of cross-sectional areas governed by material attenuation lengths. Finally, it tests the robustness of these findings against geometric disorder.

Abstract

Mechanical energy dissipation in networked materials is relevant for applications from vibration isolation to impact protection, yet identifying optimal dissipative architectures in large disordered truss networks is computationally prohibitive with conventional finite element methods. We develop an efficient graph Laplacian-based spectral framework for viscoelastic truss networks, in which the full continuum dynamics of each rod are retained exactly and the problem size scales with the number of joints rather than element-level discretization points. Using this framework, we investigate how redistributing cross-sectional areas within a network (without changing material composition) controls energy dissipation. We find that random redistribution typically reduces dissipation relative to a uniform baseline, while gradient-based optimization yields nontrivial architectures whose form is governed by the intrinsic attenuation length of the base material. Focusing on driving frequencies near a global resonant mode of the network, we show that the optimal mass distribution decays from the source (driven joint) with the attenuation length scale, and at small attenuation lengths the optimal architecture is independent of the boundary conditions. These results motivate future studies of dissipation length scale based design principles on more complex disordered architectures and provide an efficient computational framework for exploring such structures at scale.

Summary. This paper proves two major obstacles to detecting nonclassical quantum effects from weakly coupled waves like axions or gravitational waves. First, realistic detectors average over fundamental modes, washing out the signal via coarse-graining. Second, the weak coupling itself suppresses these signatures by high powers of the coupling constant. The findings suggest that current experimental proposals are fundamentally limited in their ability to observe true quantum nonclassicality.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous theoretical bounds on experimental detectability, showing that even if quantum effects exist, practical limitations (detector bandwidth/coarse-graining) and fundamental physics constraints (weak coupling) severely suppress observable signatures for ultra-light dark matter searches.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental difficulty of detecting intrinsically nonclassical quantum effects from weakly coupled external waves, such as axion dark matter or gravitational radiation, using current experimental technology.

Main result. Two independent obstructions are proven: first, detector coarse-graining erases nonclassical signatures via the Central Limit Theorem; second, weak coupling suppresses these effects by extra powers of the coupling constant. Detecting them requires quantum resources beyond current capabilities.

Method. The authors analyze the evolution of the system's P-function using a coupled harmonic oscillator toy model and compare fully quantum treatments against classical approximations in the weak coupling limit.

Model / system. The system involves weakly coupled waves (e.g., axions, gravitational radiation) interacting with detectors modeled as resonant microwave cavities. The dynamics are governed by an interaction Hamiltonian involving lowering operators for both fields.

Key observables. Nonclassicality diagnostics include quadrature statistics, number statistics, entanglement measures, and Wigner function negativity.

Important parameters / regimes. The weak coupling constant ($g$) and the small conversion efficiency ($\eta = \sin^2(gt)$) are crucial parameters determining the suppression scale.

Assumptions / limitations. Key assumptions include the applicability of the Quantum Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for detector response and treating dissipation as negligible on relevant timescales.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates both obstructions: coarse-graining leading to an effective Gaussian state, and weak coupling suppressing nonclassical features. Figure 3 shows P-functions illustrating how negative regions are smoothed out by noise.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the general problem of detecting quantum effects in weakly coupled systems. It then develops a toy model Hamiltonian and analyzes the time evolution of the state's P-function, deriving explicit mathematical proofs for both the coarse-graining and weak-coupling suppression mechanisms.

Abstract

Precision experiments increasingly target weakly coupled waves, including axion dark matter and gravitational radiation. Such waves are commonly described as classical fields, yet they could exist in quantum states with no classical counterpart. We exhibit two severe obstructions to detecting nonclassical effects, both independent of the mode occupancy. First, realistic detectors cannot resolve the fundamental modes of a field; instead they couple to coarse-grained "effective" modes, which often washes out nonclassical effects. Second, all nonclassical effects are suppressed by extra powers of the weak coupling, making them much harder to detect than the waves themselves. We prove this in general, and explicitly show how the suppression arises for quadrature and number statistics, entanglement, and decoherence. The suppression can in principle be overcome given suitable quantum resources, such as highly squeezed detector states, but the required parameters are far beyond current experimental capabilities. We use the axion cavity haloscope as an explicit example, although our conclusions apply to many ultralight dark matter searches, and rule out proposals to establish the quantization of gravity from observations of gravitational waves.

Anomalous metal and superconducting phases in rhombohedral graphene

Summary. The authors report a detailed study of superconducting and anomalous metal phases in rhombohedral graphene. They map out distinct regions exhibiting zero-resistance superconductivity alongside finite-saturation resistance AM states using transport measurements under varying magnetic fields. This work provides strong experimental constraints on the nature of these correlated electronic phases.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides an experimental platform for studying unconventional superconductivity adjacent to exotic metallic states (AM), which often require strong electron correlations or novel pairing mechanisms beyond standard BCS theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the coexistence of zero-resistance superconducting (SC) phases alongside anomalous metal (AM) phases in rhombohedral graphene under gate tuning.

Main result. Distinct, adjacent SC and AM pockets are observed in the phase diagram, suggesting two fundamentally different electronic states rather than a simple crossover. The large difference in critical magnetic field responses between the two regimes suggests they are not trivially related.

Method. The research employs systematic transport measurements, including mapping resistivity ($ ho_{xx}$) vs. gate voltages and applying perpendicular ($\mathbf{B}\perp$) and parallel ($\mathbf{B}$) magnetic fields to characterize phase boundaries.

Model / system. The physical system is rhombohedral graphene grown on a WSe2 substrate, which serves as a highly tunable platform for studying correlated electronic states in 2D materials.

Key observables. Longitudinal resistivity ($ ho_{xx}$), transverse resistance ($ ho_{xy}$), and differential conductance ($dV/dI$) are used to diagnose the SC (zero resistance) versus AM (finite saturation resistance) phases.

Important parameters / regimes. Gate voltages ($V_b, V_t$), temperature ($T \approx 11 ext{ mK}$), perpendicular magnetic field ($\mathbf{B}_\perp$, critical $\approx 4 ext{ mT}$), and in-plane magnetic field ($\mathbf{B}_{\|}$, up to $3.5 ext{ T}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the observed phenomena are intrinsic to the material, though extrinsic origins for the AM state remain a point of debate.

Figures summary. Figures map $ ho_{xx}$ across gate space showing isolated SC and AM pockets; they also track phase evolution with varying magnetic fields ($\mathbf{B}_{\|}$) and detail non-monotonic current dependence in the AM regime.

Paper structure. The paper systematically maps out the phase diagram by measuring transport properties ($ ho_{xx}, ho_{xy}$) as a function of gate voltage and applied magnetic field, comparing the distinct phenomenology of the zero-resistance SC state against the finite-resistance AM state.

Abstract

Two-dimensional superconductivity is now well established in graphene-based systems, with many such realizations showing evidence for unconventional pairing. Yet in several of the gate-tuned phases that otherwise exhibit clear signatures of superconductivity, the resistance does not vanish as temperature is lowered, instead saturating at a finite value. Here we report a systematic study of this behavior in rhombohedral graphene on a WSe$_2$ substrate, finding regions of gate space with zero-resistance superconductivity alongside others with finite saturation resistance. At zero magnetic field, these regions appear as isolated pockets in gate space that otherwise exhibit strikingly similar phenomenology, including abrupt transitions to the normal state as temperature, perpendicular magnetic field, and current are raised above critical values. A small in-plane field expands and merges these pockets without qualitatively altering their behavior, producing a sharp boundary at millikelvin base temperature between states of zero or finite resistance. The finite-resistance state reproduces key phenomenology associated with the anomalous metal, a state that has been observed in thin-film superconductors for decades but lacks an accepted theoretical explanation. The tunability and reproducibility of ultra-clean rhombohedral graphene place strong constraints on extrinsic explanations and provide a new platform for understanding this behavior.

Summary. This paper develops a theory for quantum Bayesian networks by analyzing how conditional independence structures (defined via density operators) relate to graph structure on a DAG. It distinguishes between two ways of constructing these states—intrinsic and extrinsic—and provides necessary and sufficient conditions using logarithmic identities to characterize true directed Markov states in the quantum domain.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous mathematical framework for applying graphical model concepts, traditionally from classical statistics, to the non-commutative setting of quantum mechanics, which is crucial for understanding quantum causality.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper studies quantum analogues of Bayesian networks on a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), aiming to distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic constructions for positive definite density operators.

Main result. It proves that the intrinsic construction yields equivalent Markov properties, while the extrinsic one is highly dependent on the chosen topological ordering; furthermore, it characterizes directed quantum Markov states using logarithmic candidates.

Method. The authors employ concepts from quantum information theory, including partial traces, Von Neumann entropy, and defining specialized logarithmic conditionals to linearize complex non-linear Markov identities.

Model / system. The system involves quantum states represented by positive definite density operators ($ ho$) acting on finite-dimensional tensor-product Hilbert spaces, structured according to a DAG representing conditional independence relationships.

Key observables. Density operators ($ ho$), quantum kernels ($Q_{v| ext{pa}(v)}$), excess global information ($g_I(D)\sigma$), and the logarithmic candidate $TD(\sigma)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The structure of the DAG (transitivity, chordality) dictates the equivalence between different Markov characterizations.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on defining quantum conditional independence ($\perp\!\!\!Q$) via information measures and assuming the existence of positive definite density operators for factorization theorems to hold.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing definitions (e.g., quantum conditional independence using mutual information) to comparing two construction methods (intrinsic vs. extrinsic), culminating in necessary and sufficient conditions for Markov states via logarithmic identities and global information measures.

Abstract

We study quantum analogues of Bayesian networks on a directed acyclic graph (DAG), distinguishing two constructions for positive definite density operators on finite-dimensional tensor-product Hilbert spaces. The intrinsic construction starts from a joint state and its conditional-independence properties. The extrinsic construction assembles a state sequentially from prescribed local quantum kernels, following an ordering compatible with the arrows of the DAG. For the intrinsic construction, we prove the equivalence of the ordered, local, and global directed Markov properties, together with entropy, recursive-factorization, and logarithmic characterizations. The extrinsic construction always gives a normalized state and recovers each kernel as a conditional on all preceding systems. The same kernel, however, need not be recovered from the marginal on the vertex and its parents; a three-qubit example exhibits this obstruction. We prove that independence of the chosen topological ordering is sufficient exactly for transitive DAGs: every order-invariant kernel family then yields an intrinsically directed Markov state. Finally, we associate a logarithmic candidate with every positive definite state and DAG, prove that it is subnormalized, and show that the trace-one candidate is a directed Markov state. Both the candidate and the excess global information are invariant under DAG Markov equivalence.

Abstract

As quantum computing matures, it is critical to benchmark its real-world problem solving performance against competitive classical methods, such as tensor networks. In this work, we leverage the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) algorithm to compute ground state energies of the Lipkin Meshkov Glick (LMG) model as a comparative benchmark against popular noisy intermediate-scale (NISQ) algorithms like the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and Sample-Based Quantum Diagonalization (SQD) method. By running DMRG on the NERSC Perlmutter supercomputer, we provide one of the largest LMG ground state energy datasets in literature, containing accurate ground state energies for systems up to 1400 particles. We compare these results with VQE and SQD implementations on an IBM Eagle quantum computer for comparison. VQE achieved results within 1 percent error for 6 particles, while exceeding that threshold for all other values while SQD extended that range to 17 particles, suggesting that in a noisy intermediate scale quantum era, subspace-based approaches may strike the best balance between accuracy, circuit depth, and noise resilience.

Bose-Einstein condensation and superfluidity on a fuzzy sphere

Summary. This theoretical paper examines BEC and superfluidity for Bose gases confined to a fuzzy sphere, which models non-commutative geometry. The authors show that this inherent non-commutativity stabilizes ordered phases, increasing the critical temperature compared to standard spheres. A key finding is the linear temperature dependence of the normal fluid fraction, suggesting new physical insights relevant to high-temperature superconductivity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC) and superfluidity in two dimensions confined to a non-commutative, or 'fuzzy,' sphere geometry.

Main result. Non-commutativity enhances ordered phases, leading to a critical BEC temperature higher than on a commutative sphere. Crucially, the normal fluid fraction exhibits a linear dependence on temperature, contrasting with the usual T^3 behavior.

Method. The analysis involves adapting quantum field theory techniques for bosons on curved spaces, promoting fields to matrices, and calculating thermodynamic quantities like superfluid density using Bogoliubov quasi-particle approaches.

Model / system. The system is a non-relativistic Bose gas confined to a fuzzy sphere. The bosonic fields are treated as $N imes N$ matrices, where the matrix dimension $N$ quantifies the degree of non-commutativity ($ heta$).

Key observables. Critical temperature for BEC ($T_c$), superfluid density ($ ho_s$), and the normal fluid fraction dependence on temperature ($ ho_n(T)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Non-commutativity parameter (related to $N$), sphere radius ($R$), and temperature ($T$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on treating the fuzzy geometry as a suitable replacement for integration over the commutative sphere, and approximations are used when relating sums over angular momentum modes to integrals.

It then develops the formalism on the fuzzy sphere, analyzes BEC for ideal and weakly interacting gases, and finally investigates the superfluid response, particularly concerning vortex defects.

Abstract

According to Hohenberg's theorem, Bose-Einstein condensation (BEC) in two dimensions is impossible for any temperature $T>0$. By contrast, superfluidity does occur in two dimensions at finite temperatures; it emerges due to the breaking of Galilei invariance. Here we consider BEC and superfluidity on a compact two-dimensional space taking the form of a non-commutative ("fuzzy") sphere, where the scalar bosonic fields are promoted to $N\times N$ matrices. The dimension $N$ is related to the non-commutativity parameter of space and introduces an additional scale into the system. We find that non-commutativity favors ordered phases and so enhances BEC and superfluidity. We analyze BEC in ideal and weakly interacting Bose gases on a fuzzy sphere, finding in each case that the critical temperature of BEC is greater compared to that found in the case of a commutative sphere $S^2$. Then we investigate the superfluid response of weakly interacting Bose systems. To account for vortices in a superfluid, we show that, even on an ordinary sphere, the collective coordinates of vortices induce non-commutativity. With this in mind, we extend the definition of vortex defects to an inherently non-commutative sphere studied here, where the notion of a point is untenable. The non-commutativity is expected to be experimentally relevant to BEC and superfluidity since the fuzzy sphere has a thermodynamic limit distinct from the one defined over a plane, unlike the $S^2$ case. The significance of this difference is illustrated by the superfluid density calculation indicating that, in the large sphere limit, the normal fluid fraction on the fuzzy sphere yields a linear in $T$ dependence, while on a commutative $S^2$ it exhibits the usual two-dimensional $\sim T^3$ behavior. This linear dependence, arising directly from non-commutativity, is reminiscent of Uemura's law in cuprate high-$T_c$ superconductors.

Abstract

Whole-cell simulation, modeling all of a cell's functional systems over its life cycle, is an outstanding challenge in computational biology. Even the simplest living cell contains thousands of interacting proteins and metabolites (on the order of trillions of atoms) whose full functional dynamics spans roughly five orders of magnitude in space (nm to $μ$m) and nearly nineteen in time (fs to hours). Further, many of the governing physical and chemical properties remain incompletely characterized. Simulating such complex systems at fully atomistic resolution over a full cell cycle is computationally intractable on classical architectures, raising a central question: Can quantum computing offer a viable path to whole-cell simulations that integrate molecular- and systems-level complexity? This Perspective examines the potential of quantum computing across three hierarchical scales: atomistic-molecular modeling, metabolic and regulatory networks, and whole-cell spatial modeling. We present a complexity analysis comparing classical and quantum algorithms for representative biological problems, identifying regimes of substantial theoretical speedup under specified algorithmic assumptions. We highlight algorithmic developments designed to leverage both near-term exploratory and fault-tolerant quantum architectures, and discuss practical bottlenecks: data encoding overhead, system conditioning, measurement constraints, and hybrid quantum-HPC integration. Together, these results outline a roadmap for quantum-accelerated whole-cell modeling and the biological insights such multiscale frameworks may eventually enable.

Method. The authors use rigorous mathematical proofs based on properties of stabilizer codes, analyzing code susceptibility ($\Xi_G$), Bures distance bounds derived from quantum channels, and comparing required error correction weights against achievable scaling laws.

Abstract

Quantum metrology promises a quadratic speedup over the standard quantum limit (SQL), but signal-aligned noise is expected to preclude this advantage in realistic settings. A potential route around known no-go results is to encode the sensors in a quantum code where the physical signal acts transversally as a logical gate. Understanding restrictions on transversal non-Clifford gates is therefore central to both quantum metrology and fault-tolerant quantum computation. Here, we prove such restrictions and apply them to transversal sensing. For any stabilizer code of distance $d\ge 3$ supporting a transversal logical action in level $D$ of the Clifford hierarchy, every stabilizer generating set must contain a check of weight at least $2^D$. Moreover, any $r$-level concatenated realization satisfies $r\leq \lfloor \log_2 n/D\rfloor$, forcing $r=1$ and ruling out concatenation when applied to beyond-SQL metrology. We then show that transversal single-qubit rotations by a small angle $θ$ can only induce a nontrivial logical action on an $n$-qubit code if its checks include irreducible stabilizers of weight $Ω(1/(n|θ|^2))$. Here, many single-qubit errors commute with every stabilizer or logical Pauli below this weight and are only detected by a high-weight check, so their syndromes cannot be fault-tolerantly reconstructed from low-weight normalizer measurements. Since beyond-SQL transversal sensing requires $|θ| = o(n^{-1/2})$, the weight of checks required for syndrome extraction diverges with $n$. Finally, we prove a broader metrological no-go theorem that avoids the assumptions of the quantum Cramér-Rao bound: constant-strength signal-aligned noise rules out any asymptotic advantage over the SQL in AC or DC sensing, even with biased estimators, nonstabilizer or approximate encodings, quantum memory, intermediate measurements, or adaptive control.

Summary. This work reports observing third-order nonlinear electrical transport in a percolative superconductor ($ ext{MoTe}_2$), demonstrating that this nonlinearity is highly sensitive to the superconducting state. By correlating the measured cubic voltage response with theoretical predictions from Ginzburg-Landau theory, the authors establish higher-order transport as a powerful diagnostic tool for studying superconducting fluctuations in low-dimensional materials.

Why it may be interesting. The strong correlation between higher-order non-linear response and superconducting fluctuations provides an experimental handle to study pairing mechanisms and coherence in strongly correlated 2D materials without relying solely on transport measurements near zero resistance.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates third-order nonlinear transport as a sensitive probe for superconducting transitions occurring in percolative two-dimensional superconductors.

Main result. A clear cubic dependence of the third-harmonic voltage on current was observed, and this nonlinearity was found to be strongly correlated with the superconducting state, vanishing near the transition temperature.

Method. The research combines low-frequency lock-in measurements of nonlinear voltages ($V_{3\omega}$) with theoretical modeling based on time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau theory.

Model / system. The physical system is a trilayer $1T'$-MoTe$_2$ superconductor operating in its percolative transition regime. The analysis utilizes superconducting fluctuation models derived from the Ginzburg-Landau framework to explain the observed nonlinear response.

Key observables. Third-harmonic longitudinal voltage ($V_{3\omega\parallel}$), third-order transport coefficient ($\alpha^*$), and subcritical current scale ($I^*$).

Important parameters / regimes. Superconducting transition temperature ($T_c \approx 1.4$ K), excitation current ($I$), perpendicular magnetic field ($B_{\perp}$), and back gate voltage ($V_{BG}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The nonlinear response is assumed to originate from fluctuating Cooper pairs, which is semi-quantitatively captured by the time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau theory.

Figures summary. Figures demonstrate the cubic dependence of $V_{3\omega\parallel}$ on current at various temperatures and gate voltages. They also map out how $\alpha^*$ and $I^*$ change systematically with temperature, magnetic field, and doping (gate voltage).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the experimental setup and measures nonlinear transport across different regimes (T, B, V_BG). It then correlates the measured third-order coefficients ($\alpha^*$) with superconducting fluctuations predicted by GL theory, confirming its utility as a sensitive probe.

Abstract

Percolative superconductivity frequently arises in two-dimensional van der Waals materials due to reduced dimensionality, enhanced quantum fluctuations, and complex electron-phonon interactions, providing a unique platform where normal electrons coexist with Cooper pairs. We report the observation of substantial third-order nonlinear transport in a trilayer $1T^\prime$-MoTe$_2$ superconductor within its percolative transition regime. The third-harmonic longitudinal voltage ($V_{\|}^{3ω}$) exhibits a clear cubic dependence on excitation current below a threshold, with both its magnitude and nonlinear coefficient strongly correlated with the superconducting state. This nonlinear response is semiquantitatively captured by the superconducting fluctuation within the time-dependent Ginzburg-Landau theory, where nonlinear transport arises due to fluctuating Cooper pairs. Our results demonstrate that third-order nonlinear transport serves as a sensitive probe of superconducting transitions in percolative systems and establish a foundation for exploring higher-order transport phenomena in strongly correlated systems.
