Optimal local recovery cannot alter critical orthogonality exponents in quantum spin environments

Summary. This theoretical paper establishes fundamental bounds on how much quantum interference information can be recovered when only local measurements or controls are available. By analyzing critical quantum spin chains, the authors prove that the inherent critical orthogonality exponent remains invariant under optimal local recovery protocols. This work sets rigorous limits on the information extractable from a system when global control is impossible.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep insights into the robustness of critical quantum phenomena against local perturbations, which is crucial for understanding quantum error correction and the limits of measurement in complex quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the fundamental limit of interference recovery (quantum erasure) when only local control is available over a quantum system, specifically determining if optimal local recovery can alter critical orthogonality exponents.

Main result. The authors prove that optimal local recovery cannot alter the critical orthogonality exponent, demonstrating that this exponent governs the collective limit of interference loss even when local control is applied.

Method. The analysis employs rigorous mathematical bounds, including local amplitude-ratio bounds, comparison of full vs. recovered fidelity ($F_{ ext{full}}$ vs $F_R$), and techniques involving imaginary-time evolution and Cauchy product asymptotics.

Model / system. The study focuses on quantum spin environments, exemplified by a connected critical Ising chain. The system is described by Hamiltonians involving local bias terms and is analyzed in the context of finite-region erasure.

Key observables. Orthogonality exponent, fidelity ($F_{ ext{full}}$, $F_R$), recovery gain ($\Lambda_M$), and local component ratios.

Important parameters / regimes. The critical exponent (e.g., $1/8$ for the Ising chain), the size of the controlled region ($|M|$), and local energy scales ($\Lambda_M$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes positive ground-state Hamiltonians and uniformly bounded ratios of local longitudinal bias to transverse field, and requires no gap in the environment.

Figures summary. Figure 1 compares the bare overlap and optimized amplitude for a sign-defect chain, showing the preservation of the critical exponent. Figure 2 illustrates recoverable local records at finite mediator energy.

Paper structure. The paper develops bounds by comparing full state overlaps to locally recovered states, deriving local amplitude-ratio bounds, and applying these to specific models like the Ising chain to prove the invariance of critical exponents.

Abstract

We prove a local amplitude-ratio bound for positive ground states and an explicit reverse comparison for fidelity under finite-region erasure. An exact lattice calculation for a connected critical Ising chain gives amplitude exponent $1/8$, preserved by arbitrary optimal control on any fixed spin set and, at the level of logarithmic exponents, on $o(\log N)$ spins. We derive the finite Cauchy product and its uniform strip asymptotics, and document exact two-spin optimization and an interferometric protocol for measuring the recovery. A separate finite-mediator theorem quantifies local compression and a decoder while retaining the rest of the environment exactly. We also give a ground-preparation Ramsey bound, counterexamples to stronger projected-state inferences, and independent full-Hamiltonian calculations.

Summary. This work establishes a spectral theory for calculating large deviations in complex, non-Markovian systems. By using a WKBJ Ansatz on the Feynman-Kac equation, the authors transform the problem into an eigenvalue problem governed by Hamilton-Jacobi characteristics. This provides a powerful, efficient tool for quantifying rare fluctuations in systems with memory effects, such as self-interacting jump processes.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous mathematical machinery developed—linking spectral theory, WKBJ methods, and Hamilton-Jacobi dynamics—is highly relevant for analyzing non-equilibrium dynamics in open quantum systems where memory effects are crucial.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a spectral theory to analyze dynamical large deviations in complex, non-Markovian, self-interacting processes.

Main result. The problem is successfully recast as an eigenvalue problem for a tilted operator coupled to Hamilton-Jacobi characteristics, providing a computationally efficient framework for quantifying fluctuations.

Method. The authors employ a multiscale Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin-Jeffreys (WKBJ) Ansatz to solve the Feynman-Kac equation, reducing the dynamics to solving characteristic equations derived from a Hamilton-Jacobi framework.

Model / system. The theory applies to non-Markov jump processes (SIJPs) and non-Markov chains (SIMCs) whose evolution depends on the system's history via empirical observables (memory). An example used is the bistable self-induced East model.

Key observables. Empirical measure ($L_x$), empirical flux ($\Phi_{xy}$), Scaled Cumulant Generating Function (SCGF), and the rate function $I^*$.

Important parameters / regimes. The memory variable $a$ and its conjugate momentum $P$, which govern the slow evolution of the system.

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on the WKBJ approximation, which separates fast configurational relaxation from slow memory evolution, and assumes the existence of a compact interval $K$ for the memory variable.

Figures summary. Figure 1(a) compares the SCGF of the activity for the self-induced East model at various sizes ($N=6, 8, 10$) against the standard Markov East model.

Paper structure. The paper builds the theory by first deriving the Feynman-Kac equation for the MGF, then applying the WKBJ Ansatz to transform it into an eigenvalue problem. This is subsequently linked to a Hamilton-Jacobi equation, whose characteristics govern the slow memory evolution, culminating in the calculation of the rate function via the SCGF.

Abstract

We develop a spectral theory for dynamical large deviations in non-Markov jump processes and non-Markov chains, whose dynamics depends on the past through state- and jump-dependent empirical observables. We demonstrate that a multiscale Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin-Jeffreys (WKBJ) Ansatz separates fast configurational relaxation from slow memory evolution, reducing the Feynman--Kac equation for occupation and flux statistics to an eigenvalue problem for a new tilted operator coupled to Hamilton--Jacobi characteristics. This provides a computationally efficient framework for quantifying fluctuations in a broad class of non-Markovian systems. We illustrate our general results with a bistable self-induced East model.

Summary. This work proposes a defect-aware parallel protocol to efficiently reload atoms into the defect sites of neutral-atom quantum computers. This research is crucial for realizing fault-tolerant, continuous quantum computation by addressing resource depletion.

Main problem. The paper addresses the critical need to maintain high atom filling rates in neutral-atom quantum computers by developing an efficient protocol to reload atoms into defect sites.

Main result. The proposed defect-aware protocol significantly improves the average atom filling rate (e.g., from 98.61% to 99.94% in low-noise conditions) while maintaining planning times fast enough for real-time operation.

Method. The method combines a coherent atom reloading operation, which preserves quantum information, with a defect-aware planner that determines the optimal atom reloading pattern.

Important parameters / regimes. Array size (e.g., $36 imes 90$), noise level (low/high).

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem of atom loss, proposes the combined coherent operation and defect-aware planner, presents numerical results comparing performance against MIP solvers, and concludes on the feasibility of the protocol.

Abstract

In this work, we propose a defect-aware parallel atom reloading protocol that reloads atoms based on defect information to increase the atom filling rate. The proposed protocol is useful for neutral-atom quantum computers, where reloading atoms into the defects, or atom-loss positions, is mandatory for continuous operation against atom loss. Our protocol takes advantage of both parallelism and defect awareness to improve atom-reloading efficiency. The proposed protocol consists of the coherent atom reloading operation and the defect-aware planner. The former replenishes the defects with atoms preserving the quantum information coherently. This coherence allows us to adaptively choose the reloading strategy based on the current defect configuration. The latter determines the atom reloading pattern to efficiently replenish the defects. In numerical experiments assuming a low-noise environment, we confirmed that the average atom filling rate improved from 98.61% to 99.94% for a 36 x 90 atom array, matching the filling rate obtained with a mixed integer programming solver to within 0.001 percentage points while planning each reload in under 0.1 ms. These results demonstrate that defect-aware parallel reloading can achieve and sustain high atom filling rates while the performance of the defect-aware planner meets the real-time requirements of neutral-atom quantum computers.

Summary. This paper introduces a locality-based framework to accelerate the simulation of time-accumulated observables (dense outputs) in quantum many-body systems. By leveraging Lieb-Robinson bounds, the authors demonstrate that restricting the simulation to a local region around the observable is sufficient to achieve high accuracy. This significantly reduces computational resource requirements, making simulations of large, complex lattice models more feasible.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial theoretical tool for simulating large, complex quantum systems by proving that local information access is sufficient for calculating time-integrated observables, which is vital for simulating dynamics in condensed matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of efficiently estimating time-accumulated observables (dense outputs) in quantum simulations, which traditionally requires resources scaling with the full Hamiltonian norm.

Main result. By developing a locality-based truncation framework utilizing Lieb-Robinson bounds, the authors show that local simulation resources can recover the dense output, leading to query complexity that can be independent of the total system size.

Method. The core method involves spatially truncating the Hamiltonian to a local region and rigorously bounding the resulting error using Lieb-Robinson bounds, which are then translated into local-simulation query bounds.

Model / system. The analysis applies to quantum many-body systems on a lattice, covering free and interacting fermionic and bosonic models, as well as open quantum systems governed by Lindbladians.

Key observables. Dense Output: $\mathcal{J}_H(O) = \int_0^T \langle \psi(t)|O(t)|\psi(t) angle dt$; Local-simulation query bounds.

Important parameters / regimes. Evolution time $T$, target precision $\epsilon$, system size $N$, and the decay exponent $\alpha$ governing Hamiltonian locality.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of spatial locality bounds (Lieb-Robinson bounds) and the ability to truncate the Hamiltonian while maintaining controlled error bounds.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dependence of the required local region on simulation time and precision, and demonstrate the resource improvement by comparing local vs. full Hamiltonian norms.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem of dense output estimation, introduces the locality-based truncation framework using LRBs, provides theoretical bounds for various lattice systems (fermions/bosons/open systems), and concludes with numerical verification and complexity analysis.

Abstract

Estimating time-accumulated observables, or dense outputs, is central to extracting classical information from quantum simulations. Whereas most quantum-simulation algorithms aim to reproduce a final state or a single-time expectation value, many applications require the integrated signal $J_H(O)=\int_0^T \langle ψ(t)|O(t)|ψ(t)\rangle\,dt$. Previous Hamiltonian-simulation-based dense-output algorithms have query costs that depend on the norm of the full Hamiltonian, which can grow with system size. However, one can naturally optimize the simulation algorithm when the system structure is known. Consequently, we develop a locality-based truncation framework for the dense-output problem on lattice Hamiltonians. By truncating the Hamiltonian and tightening the resulting error with Lieb-Robinson bounds, we show that a local simulation can recover the dense output. We bound the quantum simulation error from spatial truncation and translate it into local-simulation query bounds. Our analysis includes free and interacting systems for both fermions and bosons, as well as open quantum systems governed by Lindbladians. The improvement from our truncation algorithm varies from constant to unbounded as the system size grows. We also give example applications and conduct relevant numerical experiments to support our findings.

CYNAR: a trajectory-based estimator of entropy production

Summary. This paper introduces CYNAR, a novel computational pipeline designed to estimate the entropy production rate ($\sigma$) from noisy, partially observed trajectories in non-equilibrium systems. It achieves this by decomposing $\sigma$ into a 'traffic' term derived from correlation function curvature and an 'inflow rate' from the steady-state score. CYNAR is shown to be significantly more robust and theoretically sound than existing methods, making it valuable for analyzing complex stochastic dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous statistical framework for estimating non-equilibrium quantities like entropy production is crucial for understanding dissipation in open quantum systems and quantum thermodynamics, providing a powerful tool for analyzing measurement data.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is accurately inferring the entropy production rate ($\sigma$) from recorded system trajectories, especially when the underlying physical forces are unknown or only partial measurements are available.

Main result. The proposed CYNAR estimator provides a more reliable, unbiased, and robust method for estimating $\sigma$ compared to standard current-based approaches, particularly under partial observation.

Method. CYNAR estimates $\sigma$ using a kinetic decomposition: $\sigma = 4T + G$, where $T$ (Traffic) is derived from the short-time curvature of correlation functions, and $G$ (Inflow Rate) is computed from the steady-state score function.

Model / system. The analysis is based on overdamped stochastic differential equations ($\dot{x}_t = a(x_t) + \sqrt{2D} \xi_t$) describing systems in non-equilibrium steady states (NESS).

Key observables. Entropy production rate ($\sigma$), probability current ($J$), local mean velocity ($v(x)$), connected correlation matrix $C(t)$, and the score function $s(x) = abla\ln ho(x)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Diffusion tensor ($D$), system dimensionality, and the degree of observation completeness (partial observation).

Assumptions / limitations. The dynamics are assumed to follow general overdamped diffusive processes, and the decomposition $\sigma = 4T + G$ is utilized.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the CYNAR pipeline, details the kinetic decomposition ($\sigma = 4T + G$), compares its performance against standard probability current estimation methods on various complex systems, and highlights the superior robustness and theoretical advantages of the CYNAR approach.

Abstract

Inferring the entropy production rate $σ$ from recorded trajectories is a key challenge in stochastic thermodynamics. In experiments, the forces governing the dynamics are usually unknown and often only some of the system's degrees of freedom can be observed. Building on a recently introduced method [I. Di Terlizzi, Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 237101 (2025)], we develop and validate CYNAR (computation yields nonequilibrium analysis and reconstruction), a pipeline that estimates $σ$ from trajectories. It exploits a kinetic decomposition of $σ$ into a traffic term ${\cal T}$, inferred from the short-time curvature of correlation functions, and an inflow rate ${\cal G}$, computed from the steady-state score, i.e., the gradient of the log-density. We show that the same decomposition, evaluated on any subset of observed coordinates, always bounds $σ$ from below, for any diffusion tensor and without knowledge of the hidden degrees of freedom. We then compare CYNAR with a more standard approach that estimates $σ$ from steady-state probability currents, on four systems of increasing complexity. CYNAR proves to be more reliable, with no detectable bias close to equilibrium, weak dependence on spatial discretization, and marked robustness to measurement noise. It is also readily applicable to high-dimensional systems, where the traffic alone provides a lower bound close to $σ$, and remains informative under partial observation, including cases where the flux-based estimate vanishes identically. CYNAR is provided as an open-source Python package.

Summary. This paper quantifies how thermal fluctuations in a solid substrate enhance the friction experienced by ions in an electrolyte confined at the interface. Abstract

In this article, we explore how transport at a solid--electrolyte interface is affected by the internal fluctuations of the solid and quantify the role of solid excitations on ionic friction. Combining molecular dynamics simulations with theoretical modeling, we show that the presence of ions enhances the dynamical response of the electrolyte compared to pure water. This leads to an ionic contribution to the fluctuation-induced interfacial friction. We show that collective ionic modes dominate the dissipation, such that ionic friction arises from many-body effects rather than from individual drag forces. Furthermore, high-frequency molecular modes are found to be ion-specific, suggesting possible routes toward spectral separation of ions, with implications for desalination, filtration, and energy conversion.

Reverse quantum state diffusion from differential geometry

Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 3/5 · quantum measurements 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5

Summary. This paper develops a method to reverse the stochastic diffusion process that describes open quantum system evolution. By deriving a backward stochastic differential equation on the manifold of pure states, the authors create a generative model for quantum states. This is significant because it allows for the controlled generation of quantum ensembles by reversing the effects of quantum dissipation.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, geometrically motivated framework for simulating quantum processes in reverse, which is highly relevant for understanding quantum state preparation and noise characterization in open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper derives the time reversal of quantum state diffusion, which is governed by a Lindblad master equation, and utilizes this reversal for generating new quantum state ensembles.

Main result. A physical, nonlinear backward stochastic differential equation (SDE) is derived that unravels the inverse Lindbladian evolution, allowing for the generation of new quantum states from a prior distribution.

Method. The authors employ general machinery for stochastic differential equations on Riemannian manifolds, specifically working on the complex projective space of pure states, and comparing discrete-time approximations to the continuous backward SDE.

Model / system. The system is an open quantum system whose dynamics are described by a Lindblad master equation. The evolution of the pure state vector is modeled by Gisin's quantum state diffusion SDE.

Key observables. The probability density on the manifold of pure states, the relative entropy (KL divergence) between generated and original distributions, and the expectation value of an operator.

Important parameters / regimes. The Lindblad operators ($L_k$), the time step ($\delta$), and the structure of the noise fields.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation assumes the manifold of pure states is compact and smooth, and the backward SDE is only valid for the specific ensemble from which it was constructed.

Figures summary. The notes do not provide a summary of figures, but mention numerical tests for invariant ensembles using Jacobi polynomials.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the forward diffusion process via the Lindblad equation, to deriving the general formalism for time reversal on a Riemannian manifold, and finally applying this to derive error bounds and compare discrete vs. continuous time results.

Abstract

Quantum state diffusion unravels a Lindblad master equation into stochastic trajectories of pure states. The ensemble of trajectories is described by a probability density on the manifold of pure states, which contains much more information than the density matrix, its first moment. We derive the time reversal of this diffusion. Writing the reversal of a general diffusion on a manifold in Stratonovich form, and passing through the manifold of pure states, we obtain a backward stochastic differential equation for the state vector, valid for any Hilbert space dimension and any Lindbladian. The backward stochastic equation provides a physical, nonlinear unravelling of the inverse Lindbladian that holds only for the ensemble from which it was constructed. Following score-based generative models, we then use the backward equation to generate a new ensemble close to the original. For the depolarizing channel the unravelling is a Brownian motion on complex projective space, so the uniform distribution of pure states is its stationary law in any dimension. Starting the backward equation from that prior, we bound the error of the resulting distribution compared to the original one in different settings: exact and learned scores, continuous and discrete time.

Efficient Learning of Fermionic Magic States under Free-Fermion Evolution

Authors: Jiwon Heo, Myeongjin Shin, Changhun Oh

Summary. This paper develops an efficient quantum information protocol to reconstruct a fermionic magic state $|\Psi angle$ that results from an unknown free-fermion evolution $\hat{U}$ acting on a structured input state $|\Psi_{ ext{in}} angle$. It achieves this by leveraging the spectral properties of particle reduced density matrices (RDMs) to decompose the state into its constituent blocks. The result demonstrates that the state can be learned with polynomial complexity, providing a powerful tool for characterizing complex quantum many-body states.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous theoretical framework for characterizing and simulating complex quantum states generated by free-fermion dynamics, which is highly relevant for understanding quantum simulation algorithms and resource estimation in quantum information.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper establishes efficient methods to learn a compact description of a fermionic magic state $|\Psi angle$ that results from applying an unknown free-fermion evolution ($\hat{U}$) to a structured input state $|\Psi_{ ext{in}} angle$.

Main result. The authors show that this learning can be achieved using single-copy measurements and polynomial complexity by exploiting the spectral structure of particle reduced density matrices (RDMs).

Method. The core method involves recursively analyzing and reconstructing the state by utilizing the $k$-RDM ($\Gamma^{(k)}$) to separate contributions from different input blocks, followed by classical post-processing like Gram-splitting.

Model / system. The system is a fermionic model described by creation and annihilation operators ($\hat{c}^\dagger, \hat{c}$) acting on $m$ modes. The state $|\Psi angle$ is generated by a passive Gaussian unitary $\hat{U}$ acting on a structured input state $|\Psi_{ ext{in}} angle$ composed of disjoint fermionic blocks.

Key observables. Particle reduced density matrices ($\Gamma^{(k)}_\Psi$), fidelity ($\epsilon_{ ext{fid}}$), and the reconstructed state $ ilde{\Psi}$.

Important parameters / regimes. The required RDM order ($r$) is bounded by the maximum particle number per block; complexity depends polynomially on $m$ and $1/\epsilon_{ ext{fid}}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The input blocks have a definite, bounded particle number, and the analysis relies on the spectral properties of the RDMs to decompose the state structure.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the input state family ($\vert\Psi_{ ext{in}} angle$) and the target state ($\vert\Psi angle$), showing how local mode sets define blocks and how the target state is obtained via the unitary $\hat{U}$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the problem (learning $\hat{U}|\Psi_{ ext{in}} angle$) to developing the mathematical tools (exploiting RDM spectral structure), proving the existence of efficient algorithms for both homogeneous and heterogeneous block structures, and finally bounding the preparation error and complexity.

Abstract

We establish efficient learning for a family of fermionic magic states under unknown number-conserving free-fermion evolution. Each input block has a definite particle number and is a superposition of Fock states, with occupied mode sets disjoint both within and across blocks. The key idea is to exploit the spectral structure of particle reduced density matrices (RDMs) to separate contributions from individual blocks from those involving several blocks, allowing us to reconstruct the hidden block structure. For a fixed upper bound on the particle number per block, our algorithm uses single-copy measurements and polynomial sample and classical computational complexity to recover a compact description of the state with prescribed fidelity and high probability, without prior knowledge of the block decomposition or the evolution. RDMs up to this upper bound suffice for reconstruction. We further show that this RDM order is necessary in general: two orthogonal states in the family can have identical RDMs at every lower order. These results show that an extensive number of non-Gaussian blocks can be compatible with efficient state learning.

To save experimental resources, it is challenging to achieve quantum double lock-in detection just via a single quantum interferometry while still extracting complete information about the signal's amplitude, frequency, and initial phase. Here, we present a general protocol for achieving a quantum double lock-in detection just via a single quantum interferometry under a sequential orthogonal periodic multipulse sequences. In particular, if the input state is a Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger state and two interaction-based operations are applied during interferometry, the measurement precisions for frequency, amplitude, and initial phase can both approach the Heisenberg limit. Our study paves a new way for measuring oscillating signals with a single quantum interferometry, and provides a feasible method for achieving Heisenberg-limited detection of alternating signals.

Summary. This paper presents a robust theoretical framework for generating random unitaries by repeatedly applying Hamiltonians that are conjugated by a global control operator. Abstract

Random unitary operations are a fundamental resource for quantum information processing, yet provably generating them with experimentally available controls remains challenging. Existing approaches often require calculating higher-order operator expectation values, relying on statistical assumptions about the experimental Hamiltonian's spectrum to make these calculations tractable. We show how to implement random unitaries by a sequence of evolutions generated by a fixed Hamiltonian conjugated by a global control operator. We give a geometric argument that establishes exponential convergence to the uniform random (Haar) measure on the accessible unitary group under uniform parameter sampling, beyond a finite sequence-length threshold that we bound. We show this holds when the connected Lie subgroup generated by the conjugated Hamiltonians is closed. Native dynamics and global control thus provide a constructive route to uniform randomness, with applications to many experimental platforms including Rydberg atoms, bosons and fermions in optical lattices, transmons, and trapped ions.
