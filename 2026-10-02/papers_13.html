Logical Operator Decomposition for Distance Analysis of Bivariate Bicycle Codes

Authors: Mohammad Rowshan, Simon Devitt
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39395v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops an algebraic method to determine the minimum distance of Bivariate Bicycle quantum codes by analyzing their logical structure using a short exact sequence. The method decomposes the distance into components derived from the annihilator and colon quotients, successfully calculating the distances for several standard codes. This provides a powerful, theory-driven tool for quantum error correction code analysis.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous algebraic approach to determining code parameters, especially distance, provides powerful tools for analyzing quantum error-correcting codes beyond standard syndrome decoding techniques.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to derive the minimum distance of Bivariate Bicycle (BB) quantum codes algebraically, rather than relying on purely numerical methods.

Main result. The authors established a distance identity $d_Z = \min(d_A, d_C)$ using a short exact sequence, and successfully calculated the distances for six standard BB codes.

Method. The analysis employs advanced algebraic techniques, specifically studying the Z-logical quotient $K/S$ over a group algebra, and utilizing a translation-anchored cluster search to establish lower bounds.

Model / system. The system is the Bivariate Bicycle (BB) quantum code, which is a finite-length family of quantum low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes defined over the group algebra $\mathbb{F}_2[x, y]/(x^\ell-1, y^m-1)$.

Key observables. Minimum distance ($d_Z$), logical dimension ($k$), and the component minima ($d_A, d_C$) derived from the annihilator and colon quotients.

Important parameters / regimes. Code lengths ($\ell, m$), the dimension $k$, and the specific parameters defining the six standard codes (e.g., $[[108, 8, 10]]$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the structure provided by the short exact sequence relating the logical quotient to its annihilator and colon components.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the block structure of the code checks and the overall workflow for bounding the distance, while tables summarize the calculated distances, structural dimensions, and census of logical classes.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by defining the algebraic structure of the code via the Z-logical quotient, establishing the short exact sequence, deriving the distance identity, and finally applying a structured search algorithm (cluster search) to compute the minimum distance for specific code instances.

Abstract

Bivariate bicycle (BB) quantum codes are a prominent finite-length family of quantum low-density parity-check codes, but their minimum distance is usually established numerically rather than read from the defining polynomials. We study the $Z$-logical quotient $K/S$ over $\mathbb F_2[x,y]/(x^\ell-1,y^m-1)$ and show that it fits into a short exact sequence with an annihilator quotient as kernel and a colon quotient as cokernel. The sequence gives an explicit logical basis, a dimension formula, and a componentwise distance identity $d_Z=\min(d_{\mathcal A},d_{\mathcal C})$. Using the Frobenius structure of the finite group algebra, we prove $r_{\mathcal A}=r_{\mathcal C}=k/2$ for every BB code, including repeated-root cases. The algebraic component of a logical class is distinct from the support shape of its lightest representatives: an annihilator class can have a lighter two-block representative, and a colon class can have a one-sided minimum. For lower bounds we show that every proper subset of a minimum-weight logical operator has nonzero syndrome, and that this property persists inside the colon component but not inside the annihilator component. A translation-anchored cluster search built on it proves the distances $4,6,10,10,12,18$ of the six standard BB codes of lengths $18$ to $288$ and enumerates every minimum-weight logical operator. The resulting census shows that $[[108,8,10]]$ is the only one of the six whose distance is attained in a single component, with $d_{\mathcal C}=10$ and $d_{\mathcal A}=12$.

MyTm: An Automated Melting Temperature Calculation Toolkit

Authors: Y. S. Huang, H. X. Song, Y. Sun, J. L. Li, Y. L. Xu, F. C. Wu, Y. C. Gan, Y. F. Wang, H. Wang, Hua Y. Geng

Summary. This paper introduces MyTm, a fully automated toolkit designed to calculate the melting temperature of crystalline materials using Molecular Dynamics simulations. It modularizes the process, integrating multiple established MD techniques and crucially employs a Machine Learning classifier to accurately distinguish between solid-like and liquid-like atomic environments. This automation makes high-throughput screening of phase transitions significantly more feasible.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical MD, the rigorous application of ML for phase identification and the development of a highly automated, modular framework for simulating complex phase transitions are relevant to developing robust computational tools for many-body dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop an automated, high-throughput computational method for accurately determining the melting temperature (Tm) of materials.

Main result. The proposed toolkit, MyTm, successfully automates various direct Molecular Dynamics (MD) approaches, yielding results comparable to literature values, especially using the solid-liquid coexistence method.

Method. MyTm is a modular toolkit that integrates MD simulations with advanced atomic classification, including a Machine Learning (ML) component, to automate the complex process of phase transition calculation.

Model / system. The toolkit is applicable to long-range ordered crystalline systems, simulating the solid-liquid phase transition using various models like perfect crystals, defective crystals, and two-phase solid-liquid coexistence models.

Key observables. Melting temperature (Tm), solid fraction, and thermodynamic quantities (Temperature, Pressure, Volume).

Important parameters / regimes. The choice of MD ensemble (NPT, NVE, etc.), solid/liquid cutoff thresholds, and simulation parameters are critical.

Assumptions / limitations. The method is limited to crystalline solids and cannot handle amorphous materials; direct-heating and void methods are noted to overestimate Tm.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the constituent components of the calculation methods, the various models used (direct-heating, void, coexistence), and flowcharts detailing the temperature adjustment procedures.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, details the modular components of MyTm (model construction, ensemble selection, temperature control, classification), presents the ML enhancement, validates the method on several systems, and concludes with a comparison of results across different methods.

Abstract

Melting temperature calculation is one of the important topics in computational materials science. In high-throughput in silico screening and artificial intelligence assisted design of materials, it usually requires a rapid and autonomous assessment of the melting temperature of the target. Unfortunately, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the melting point require many cumbersome and manual operations, making large-scale calculation of the melting point challenging. In this work, we introduce MyTm, a toolkit that employs MD to automatically determine the melting point. The method is fully modularized, and by combining these modules, the program enables fully automated melting calculations by using commonly adopted approaches, including the direct-heating method, the void method, the modified void method, the solid-liquid coexistence method, and the Z method. Moreover, a machine learning (ML) method is proposed and employed to recognize and classify the solid like and liquid like atoms, which effectively resolve the low accuracy issue in conventional classification approaches, thus making the automated high throughput pipeline of melting-point calculation possible. The robustness and efficacy of MyTm have been demonstrated by several well studied systems.

Natural Barriers to Quantum Extraction: On the Post-Quantum (In)security of (O)EKE and Masny-Rindal OT

Authors: James Bartusek, Jake Januzelli
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39844v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper analyzes the post-quantum security of widely used key exchange and oblivious transfer protocols. The authors prove that these protocols lose their strong Universally Composable security guarantees when faced with quantum adversaries. However, they provide positive results by establishing alternative, game-based security notions that can still hold in the quantum setting.

Why it may be interesting. While the topic is cryptography, the rigorous application of quantum computational models (QROM) to break classical security assumptions is a theoretical exercise in quantum information security.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the established Universally Composable (UC-) security of key exchange and oblivious transfer protocols translates when the adversary is quantum polynomial-time.

Main result. The authors prove that general families of (O)EKE protocols and Masny-Rindal OT are not UC-secure against quantum adversaries, although they establish that game-based security notions can still hold.

Method. The insecurity is demonstrated by devising a specific adversarial strategy that defeats the simulator's ability to extract input in the quantum setting. Positive results are shown via game-based security proofs.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on cryptographic protocols like Encrypted Key Exchange (EKE) and Masny-Rindal OT, which compile Password-Authenticated Key Exchange (PAKE) and Oblivious Transfer (OT). The security is analyzed in the Quantum Random Oracle Model (QROM).

Key observables. UC-security (failure), game-based security (success), and the ability of an adversary to extract secret inputs (failure).

Important parameters / regimes. Quantum polynomial-time adversaries, the structure of the KEM, and the specific instantiation of the protocols (e.g., 2-Feistel network).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis builds upon the assumption of ideal symmetric-key primitives and the use of random oracles, contrasting the classical ROM with the QROM.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of Masny-Rindal OT, and Figure 4 illustrates a typical instantiation of EKE using KEMs and ideal ciphers.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the failure of UC-security for (O)EKE and Masny-Rindal OT in the quantum setting. It then complements this negative result by establishing positive, game-based security notions for these protocols in the QROM.

Abstract

Encrypted key exchange (EKE), introduced by Bellovin and Merritt (IEEE S\&P 1992), and Masny-Rindal OT, introduced by Masny and Rindal (ACM CCS 2019), are highly-efficient methods for compiling essentially any KEM into advanced cryptographic protocols, namely password-authenticated key exchange (PAKE) and oblivious transfer (OT), by relying only on idealized symmetric-key primitives. They have become leading candidates for practically-implementable PAKE and OT due to (1) their simplicity, (2) their plug-and-play nature, allowing for flexibility in the choice of KEM, and (3) existing proofs of UC-security (in the classical adversarial model). Due to point (2) above, these compilers yield attractive candidates for efficient \emph{post-quantum} PAKE and OT, especially given the recent post-quantum KEM standardization efforts. This motivates the question of whether the (UC-)security of these compilers translates to the quantum adversarial model. In this work, we show that it does not. In particular, we prove that a general family of (O)EKE protocols, as well as Masny-Rindal OT, are \emph{not} UC-secure against quantum polynomial-time adversaries, even when instantiated with a post-quantum KEM. To establish UC-insecurity, we devise an adversarial strategy that provably thwarts any attempt by the simulator to extract its input (the password in the case of PAKE, and the receiver's choice bit in the case of OT). To complement these negative results, we establish that both compilers yield certain notions of \emph{game-based} security. Along the way, we establish a novel ``advantage-tight'' one-way to hiding lemma that may be of independent interest.

On quantum interactive proofs with a laconic prover

Authors: Zihan Hu, Yupan Liu
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39495v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper rigorously analyzes quantum interactive proof systems with a restricted (laconic) prover response. It characterizes the complexity class $ ext{QIP}_{\ell ext{-bit}}(2)$ using Multi-State Distinguishability, showing it sits slightly above $ ext{QSZK}$. Furthermore, it identifies precise conditions under which these powerful quantum protocols collapse to weaker, more understood classes like $ ext{QSZK}$ or $ ext{BQP}$.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep theoretical insights into the limits of quantum verification protocols, which underpins the security and computational power of quantum computation models.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the characterization and complexity of two-message quantum interactive proof systems ($ ext{QIP}_{\ell ext{-bit}}(2)$) involving a prover with limited (laconic) communication.

Main result. It establishes that $ ext{QIP}_{\ell ext{-bit}}(2)$ is completely characterized by Multi-State Distinguishability (MultiQSD), placing it just above Quantum Statistical Zero-Knowledge ($ ext{QSZK}$), and identifies specific conditions under which it collapses to $ ext{QSZK}$ or $ ext{BQP}$.

Method. The analysis uses techniques involving state distinguishability (Trace Distance, Quantum Jensen-Shannon Divergence) and reduction arguments to relate the QIP model to known complexity classes.

Model / system. The core model is the two-message quantum interactive proof system with a laconic prover, where the verifier interacts with a prover whose response length $\ell$ is logarithmic in the input size $n$.

Key observables. Quantum State Distinguishability (QSD), Multi-State Distinguishability (MultiQSD), and the acceptance probability $\omega(V(x))$ of the proof system.

Important parameters / regimes. The length of the prover's response ($\ell$), and the gap parameters $a(n)$ and $b(n)$ governing the distinguishability problems.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on specific conditions for collapse regimes, such as $a(n) - b(n) \ge 1/O(\log n)$ for $ ext{QSD} \in ext{QSZK}$, and the use of answer compression techniques.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first characterizing the $ ext{QIP}_{\ell ext{-bit}}(2)$ class using MultiQSD, then proving specific collapse regimes to $ ext{QSZK}$ or $ ext{BQP}$ based on parameter choices, and finally establishing relationships between different quantum distance measures.

Abstract

Interactive proof systems with a laconic prover, studied by Goldreich, Vadhan, and Wigderson (CC, 2002), capture problems verifiable with logarithmic prover communication in the classical setting. For two-message quantum analogs, even a single-bit prover response contains quantum statistical zero-knowledge ($\sf QSZK$), introduced by Watrous (FOCS 2002). However, restricting the verifier's question to classical public coins collapses the corresponding class to $\sf BQP$, as shown by Beigi, Shor, and Watrous (ToC, 2011). We further study two-message quantum interactive proof systems with a laconic prover. To this end, we introduce the class ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}(2)$, where $\ell$ is the length of the prover's response, and establish: 1. A natural complete characterization of ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}(2)$ by Multi-State Distinguishability. In particular, Quantum State Distinguishability (QSD) is ${\sf QIP}_{\rm bit}$-complete. Since QSD is $\sf QSZK$-hard, our result places ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}(2)$, for $\ell\geq 2$, in a landscape "just above" $\sf QSZK$. 2. Easy regimes for ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}(2)$ collapsing to $\sf QSZK$. We prove that QSD$[a,b]$ (and thus ${\sf QIP}_{\rm bit}[a,b]$) is in $\sf QSZK$ when $a(n)-b(n)\geq 1/O(\log n)$, and combine this with an answer compression from ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}[2,c,s]$ to ${\sf QIP}_{\rm bit}$ to obtain another easy regime when $2c>(1+2^{\ell/2})s$. Remarkably, our improved polarization applies to SD and $\sf SZK$, resolving an open problem in Sahai and Vadhan (JACM, 2003). 3. Quantum public coins also make the interaction useless: ${\sf qc}\text{-}{\sf QAM}[O(\sqrt{\log{n}})]$ with constant gap is in $\sf BQP$, where ${\sf qc}\text{-}{\sf QAM}[\ell]$ is a subclass of ${\sf QIP}_{\ell\text{-}{\rm bit}}(2)$ in which the verifier's question is exactly halves of EPR pairs.

On Removing Interaction from Quantum Proofs

Authors: Nicholas Spooner, Max Tromanhauser
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.38387v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper investigates the theoretical limits of creating non-interactive quantum proofs for QMA. By constructing a specific forgery attack, the authors prove that if a general quantum compiler exists to remove interaction from quantum protocols, it forces the collapse of the complexity class QMA down to BQP. This result has major implications for quantum cryptography and zero-knowledge proof systems.

Why it may be interesting. While highly theoretical, the collapse of QMA to BQP has profound implications for the computational power achievable with quantum proofs, which underpins quantum cryptography and verification protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is constructing publicly-verifiable Non-Interactive Zero-Knowledge Proofs (NIZKs) for the complexity class QMA.

Main result. The authors show that assuming the existence of certain quantum non-interactivity compilers (QNICs), the complexity class QMA collapses to BQP (QMA = BQP).

Method. The proof involves constructing a forgery attack that exploits the supposed compiler mechanism, demonstrating that the indistinguishability of 'real' and 'defer' quantum channels allows for the construction of a QMA-complete decision algorithm.

Model / system. The work operates within the theoretical framework of quantum computation, specifically analyzing $\Sigma$-protocols ($\Xi$-protocols) for QMA and the role of the Fiat-Shamir transformation in quantum settings.

Key observables. Acceptance probabilities for the proof system, soundness error ($\delta(n)$), and completeness probability ($\mu_x$).

Important parameters / regimes. The existence of quantum commitments and the nature of the compiler (straight-line vs. general).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is the existence of generic, small-error, straight-line Quantum Non-Interactivity Compilers (QNICs) in the QROM.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of a straight-line 'Fiat–Shamir-like' compiler. Figure 2 depicts the 'Real' and 'Defer' quantum channels used in the attack construction, and Figure 3 shows the overall attack flow on the Straightline QNIC.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the motivation by contrasting classical and quantum NIZK construction. It then develops the core argument by constructing a forgery attack that relies on the assumed compiler structure, culminating in the proof that this assumption implies QMA = BQP.

Abstract

An important open question in quantum cryptography is the construction of publicly-verifiable NIZKs for QMA. Classically, one can construct NIZKs for NP in the random oracle model (and sometimes in the standard model) by compiling an honest-verifier ZK (HVZK) $Σ$-protocol for NP using the Fiat-Shamir transformation. Broadbent and Grilo introduced a quantum analog of a $Σ$-protocol (which they call a $Ξ$-protocol) in which the prover's first message is quantum, and show that HVZK $Ξ$-protocols exist for QMA. However, it is not clear how to compile such protocols into NIZKs in the (Q)ROM, because the Fiat-Shamir transformation seems to be incompatible with quantum messages. In this work we give formal evidence that this is indeed the case: we show that if generic "Fiat-Shamir-like" compilers for quantum protocols exist in the QROM (with small completeness and soundness error) then QMA = BQP.

On the Relation Between the Boson Peak and the Vibrational Phases in Glasses

Authors: Philip Rasmussen, Søren S. Sørensen

Summary. This work investigates the Boson Peak in glasses by analyzing vibrational modes using multiple metrics beyond the standard density of states. The authors demonstrate that the BP marks a critical transition where vibrational modes achieve a unique combination of spatial extension, local phase coherence, and wave-like order. This provides a unified, microscopic picture of vibrational physics in disordered materials.

Why it may be interesting. The connection drawn between vibrational coherence (PQ, EP) and spatial extent (PR) at the BP provides a detailed, quantitative understanding of vibrational dynamics in disordered solids, relevant to understanding energy transport in complex materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to elucidate the microscopic origin of the Boson Peak (BP) in the vibrational spectra of glasses, which deviates from the Debye model.

Main result. The BP energy correlates with elastic properties, and its presence coincides with a transition where vibrational modes exhibit a combination of substantial spatial extent, maximal local phase coherence, and maximal wave-like order.

Method. The analysis involves normal mode calculations to determine the vibrational density of states, alongside calculating diagnostics like the Participation Ratio (PR), Phase Quotient (PQ), and Eigenvector Periodicity (EP).

Model / system. The study applies these vibrational analyses to a wide range of glassy and disordered systems, including oxides, chalcogenides, metallic glasses, and glassy water.

Key observables. Boson Peak energy (E_BP), Participation Ratio (PR), Phase Quotient (PQ), Eigenvector Periodicity (EP), and the reduced density of states g(E)E^-2.

Important parameters / regimes. Atomic number density (rho_a) and Debye energy (E_D) are used to establish scaling laws for E_BP.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the harmonic approximation for calculating the dynamical matrix and vibrational modes.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the BP variation across different glass families, confirm the scaling of E_BP with elastic properties, and plot PR, PQ, and EP showing characteristic crossovers at E_BP.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the BP using multiple mode descriptors (PR, PQ, EP) to show that the BP marks a crossover from localized, non-periodic vibrations to an extended, yet non-wave-like, regime.

Abstract

The boson peak is a vibrational feature commonly found in glassy and disordered systems described as the deviation from the scaling of the Debye model ($g({E}){\propto}{E}^2$). Despite much interest, the microscopic nature of the vibrations is only anecdotally discussed. Here we report how the boson peak appears in conjunction with a crossover from localized to extended but non-wave-like and coincide with a maximum in phase coherence and spatial periodicity. Our analyses provide new insights into the microscopic origin of the boson peak across a wide range of glasses.

Priority-Aware Routing for Quantum Networks:Integrating Coherence-Time Constraints into Scheduling

Authors: Sadhgun Ram Dasi, Aswath Babu H
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.38380v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a novel, coherence-aware routing protocol for quantum networks that explicitly accounts for decoherence caused by queuing delays. By integrating principles from Weighted Fair Queueing theory, the protocol prioritizes traffic to mitigate fidelity loss under high network load. The results demonstrate substantial improvements in quantum fidelity and throughput compared to existing loss-only or FIFO scheduling methods.

Why it may be interesting. It directly addresses the practical limitations of quantum communication by quantifying the impact of classical network congestion (queuing delay) on fundamental quantum information metrics like fidelity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Quantum networks suffer from decoherence due to finite memory coherence times, which is exacerbated by queuing delays in classical routing protocols.

Main result. The proposed coherence-aware routing protocol significantly maintains quantum fidelity and throughput under high load compared to standard routing methods, especially on scale-free topologies.

Method. The protocol integrates decoherence constraints into path selection using a priority-modulated aging model derived from Weighted Fair Queueing theory.

Model / system. The system is a quantum network modeled as a directed graph where nodes are quantum repeaters with finite memory, and edges are optical links subject to photon loss and propagation latency.

Key observables. Fidelity (e.g., 96.08% under 9x load increase), aggregate throughput, and tail latency (P2 P95).

Important parameters / regimes. Coherence time ($T_{mem} = 400 \mu s$), minimum fidelity threshold ($F_{min} = 0.70$), and network load (up to 180k QPS).

Assumptions / limitations. The fidelity decay is modeled using an exact simplification for pure dephasing ($F(t) = 1 - e^{-t/T_{mem}^2}$), and the queue estimation uses an EWMA model.

Figures summary. The results compare the protocol's performance against loss-only Dijkstra and FIFO scheduling across ER and BA topologies, showing quantitative gains in fidelity and throughput.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the physical problem (decoherence in queuing), develops the theoretical framework (WFQ-derived aging model and composite cost function), presents the custom simulator, and validates the protocol's performance across different network topologies under stress.

Abstract

Quantum networks face a fundamental challenge absent in classical networks; finite memory coherence times mean that queuing delay directly degrades information quality, causing decoherence that has no classical analog. Existing routing protocols optimize for hop count or channel fidelity while treating scheduling delays as a secondary concern, leaving fidelity vulnerable to congestion-induced decoherence. We present a coherence-aware routing protocol that integrates decoherence constraints into path selection via a priority-modulated aging model derived analytically from Weighted Fair Queueing theory, where high-priority traffic receives compressed effective queue delay. We build a custom discrete-event simulator verified against NetSquid (across 104 states), and evaluate across two structurally diverse topology classes under identical physical parameters, isolating topology structure as the sole independent variable. In all experiments, our protocol is benchmarked against loss-only Dijkstra routing and FIFO scheduling as baselines. On topologies, P2 traffic achieves 96.08% fidelity under a 9x load increase with only 0.04 percentage point degradation, versus a 13.51 percentage point collapse for the loss-only baseline; P2 tail latency holds stable at 0.055 ms while Dijkstra degrades 5.5x. On Barabasi-Albert scale-free topologies, our protocol delivers a +4.12 percentage point fidelity advantage with 97.8% higher aggregate throughput at operational loads over the baseline, with a clearly characterized hub-saturation boundary at 160k QPS. FIFO scheduling provides negligible priority differentiation on both topology classes, providing strong evidence that coherence-aware routing-not scheduling alone is responsible for the observed QoS gains.

Probing Collective and Individual Kondo Screening: Multi-Stage, Multi-Channel Kondo Effects in a $C_3$-Symmetric Four-Impurity Model

Authors: Danqing Hu, Jiangfan Wang, Zixiang Hu, Yi-feng Yang
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40109v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper investigates the nature of Kondo screening in a four-impurity Kondo model using the AUNRG technique. By coupling a probe impurity to three primary impurities, the authors successfully distinguish between collective and individual screening mechanisms. The results confirm the existence of emergent 2CK and 3CK effects, providing deeper insight into multi-impurity quantum magnetism.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a concrete theoretical framework (AUNRG) to analyze complex many-body quantum states in correlated electron systems, offering insights into how local interactions give way to emergent collective behavior.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of distinguishing between collective (multi-impurity) and individual (local) Kondo screening mechanisms in complex multi-impurity Kondo systems.

Main result. The study demonstrates that the four-impurity model exhibits emergent multi-stage, two-channel Kondo (2CK) effects from collective screening and unstable three-channel Kondo (3CK) effects from individual screening.

Method. The research utilizes the Auxiliary-Bath Numerical Renormalization Group (AUNRG) method, which is applied to analyze the complex spin interactions in the multi-impurity Kondo model.

Model / system. The primary system is a $C_3$-symmetric four-impurity Kondo (4IK) model, which extends the previously studied three-impurity Kondo (3IK) model. The Hamiltonian involves couplings between impurity spins and a shared electron bath.

Key observables. Impurity entropy ($ ext{Simp}$), effective moment ($\mu_{ ext{eff}}$), and various spin correlation functions are used to diagnose the nature of the screened state.

Important parameters / regimes. The key parameters are the Kondo coupling strength ($J_K$) and the coupling strength involving the probe impurity ($J_H$), analyzed across different temperature regimes.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the applicability and extension of the AUNRG method to systems with additional impurities decoupled from the bath.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the 4IK model geometry and numerical phase diagrams on the $J_K$-$J_H$ plane, while other figures show the temperature evolution of observables demonstrating the emergence of specific cluster states.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical framework using the AUNRG method, applies it to the 4IK model, and then analyzes the resulting phase diagram to distinguish between the signatures of collective (2CK) and individual (3CK) screening regimes.

Abstract

Collective screening has been proposed to underlie the basic physics of multi-impurity and lattice Kondo systems. But how to establish this picture and distinguish it from individual (local) Kondo screening remains a grand challenge. The recently developed auxiliary-bath numerical renormalization group (AUNRG) method provides a key step towards resolving this issue. Its application to the $C_3$-symmetric three-impurity Kondo (3IK) model with a shared electron bath reveals fully screened Fermi liquid ground states arising from collective screening of cluster spin degrees of freedom at small Kondo coupling $J_{\rm K}$ and individual (local) screening of local impurities at large $J_{\rm K}$. Here we design a four-impurity Kondo (4IK) model where the probe impurity couples only to the original three Kondo impurities to detect the nature of the screened states. We show that the collective and individual Kondo screenings give rise to emergent multi-stage, two-channel Kondo effect and unstable three-channel Kondo effect, respectively. This suggests a special helicity structure of the screened states and confirms the idea of collective screening in multi-impurity Kondo systems. Our work also demonstrates that the same auxiliary-bath construction can be readily extended to models with additional impurities to explore novel many-body quantum states with emergent cluster degrees of freedom.

Provable Classical and Quantum Local Algorithms for Max-$k$-Cut and Quantum Advantage at Moderate Girth

Authors: Anuj Apte, Abid Khan, Edward Farhi, Kunal Marwaha, Sami Boulebnane, Ruslan Shaydulin

Summary. This paper rigorously compares the performance of classical and quantum algorithms for the Max-$k$-Cut problem on structured graphs. By developing an improved local vector algorithm and analyzing QAOA using tensor networks, the authors provide provable guarantees. They conclude that QAOA surpasses the best classical bounds for sufficiently deep circuits, suggesting a concrete instance of quantum advantage.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly compares the scaling and performance of quantum algorithms (QAOA) against state-of-the-art classical methods (local vector algorithms) for a fundamental NP-hard optimization problem, which is central to quantum computation theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops and compares provable classical and quantum local algorithms for solving the Max-$k$-Cut problem on regular graphs.

Main result. The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) achieves a better cut-fraction guarantee than the best known classical algorithm for sufficiently deep circuits ($p \ge 9$) on graphs with high girth, suggesting an apparent quantum advantage.

As the radius \(ρ=(1-R)/2-γ\) approaches the quantum Singleton bound, we have, deterministically for FQRS codes and w.h.p. for balanced random CSS codes, \[ L^\star_{\rm QLR} = \ell^{Θ(R_1/γ)}, \qquad L^\star_{\rm QLD} = Θ\!\left(\frac{1-R}γ\right) \qquad (γ\downarrow0). \] The asymptotically exact QLD radius tradeoff is \[ ρ_L^\star = \frac{L}{L+1}\frac{1-R}{2}. \] These conclusions extend to the average-radius setting. For achievability, building on the work of Brakensiek, Chen, Dhar, and Zhang (STOC 2026), we establish a pairing lemma for joint \(X/Z\) candidate lists that preserves the one-sector coefficient and avoids a product loss in list size. For the QLD converse, we prove a quantum generalized Singleton bound based on the classical projection-and-patching argument with stabilizer distinctness. For the QLR lower bounds, we adapt the folded Reed--Solomon construction of Chen and Zhang (STOC 2025) so that the candidates remain stabilizer distinct. For random CSS codes, we show classical bad lists survive the stabilizer quotient with high probability.

Abstract

Every square matrix decomposes its underlying Hilbert space into generalized, nonorthogonal eigensubspaces. We show that a quantum computer can perform such an oblique eigenprojection $Π$ given block encoding access to the input matrix. Our approach has a query complexity nearly linear in the inverse gap and a normalization factor close to $\lVertΠ\rVert$ under a spectral-set condition on the input. This covers common assumptions on the numerical range or diagonalizability and matches known results for orthogonal eigenprojections. We achieve this with a two-sided block preconditioning that uses a discrete Fourier transform of the matrix resolvent. We describe applications to: (i) preparing eigenstates of matrices with complex eigenvalues, extending the quantum eigenvalue transformation algorithm of Low and Su beyond real spectra; (ii) solving continuous-time algebraic Riccati equations, cubically speeding up a prior solver of Rodenas-Ruiz, Zhao, and Lee; and (iii) solving ordinary Sylvester equations, quadratically improving a direct augmented method of Wang and Liu. Our result suggests a promising route to applying nonanalytic matrix functions on quantum computers.

Abstract

Pseudorandom error-correcting codes (PRCs), introduced by Christ and Gunn [CRYPTO 2024], are classical error-correcting codes whose codewords are computationally indistinguishable from uniformly random strings. We initiate the study of quantum analogues of PRCs. We define quantum pseudorandom error-correcting codes (QPRCs). Assuming that Learning Parity with Noise (LPN) is hard for $2^{O(\sqrt{n})}$-time quantum algorithms, we construct QPRCs whose encodings are indistinguishable from Haar-random isometries. We call these pseudorandom isometric error-correcting codes (PRICs), and our construction is robust to all $o(n \frac{\log \log n}{\log n})$-local quantum noise, where $n$ is the number of physical qubits. Under the same assumption, we also construct QPRCs whose encodings are indistinguishable from the completely depolarizing channel and that are robust to all $αn$-local quantum noise for some constant $α>0$. The latter QPRCs give a direct quantum analogue of classical PRCs. To construct PRICs, we develop two technical ingredients of independent interest. First, we introduce a new classical cryptographic primitive which we call pseudorandom functional error-correcting codes (PRFCs) and construct them under the same LPN assumption. Second, we develop a new efficient decoding procedure within the codeword-stabilized (CWS) framework introduced in [Cross, Smith, Smolin, and Zeng, IEEE ISIT 2008], which is a generic framework for constructing quantum error-correcting codes by combining (possibly nonlinear) classical error-correcting codes and graphs. This resolves the open problem of finding a general method for efficiently decoding such CWS codes based on nonlinear classical codes, raised in [Li, Dumer, Grassl, and Pryadko, Physical Review A 2010].
