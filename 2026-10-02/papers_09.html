Quantum State Routing and Perfect State Transfer on Signed Graphs under Environmental Noise

Authors: Nur Mohammad Sanfui, Supriyo Dutta
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39890v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · interference shaping light 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This paper proposes a novel, measurement-free quantum routing architecture using coined quantum walks on signed graphs. By engineering the graph structure with controlled phase shifts, the authors achieve deterministic Perfect State Transfer (PST) and demonstrate that the protocol maintains high fidelity over significant distances even when subjected to realistic amplitude and phase damping noise.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses quantum network communication, which is a major focus area for quantum information theorists. The use of topological invariants (edge signs) to achieve robustness against noise is highly relevant to building fault-tolerant quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is achieving measurement-free, deterministic routing of unknown quantum information across distributed networks while mitigating decoherence from environmental noise.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that using coined quantum walks on signed graphs enables Perfect State Transfer (PST) with unit fidelity, and they derive analytical limits showing the protocol's resilience against amplitude and phase damping noise.

Method. The research employs the formalism of coined Szegedy quantum walks on specially constructed signed graphs, treating edge signs as localized $\pi$-phase shifts to enforce destructive interference and zero back-scattering.

Model / system. The system is a quantum router modeled by coined quantum walks on edge-duplicated signed graphs ($\vec{\Sigma}$). Positive edges are standard waveguides, while negative edges introduce controlled $\pi$-phase shifts, enabling topological control over state propagation.

Key observables. Fidelity ($F$), Back-scattering suppression, Maximum achievable hop distance ($ au_{\max}$) under noise.

Important parameters / regimes. Control parameter $\alpha=0.5$, Amplitude damping rate ($\lambda$), Phase damping rate ($p$), Graph topology (e.g., dumbbell switch $D_{2m,0,2n}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The model assumes the physical feasibility of implementing localized $\pi$-phase shifts using external modulators, and the noise analysis assumes noise acts independently at each discrete time step.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the signed graph structure, the process of generating the signed directed graph from an undirected one, and examples of the topological archetypes used for routing.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the need for measurement-free routing, critiques conventional quantum walks, introduces the signed graph model to enforce zero back-scattering, proves deterministic PST on archetypes, and concludes with a detailed analysis of noise robustness.

Abstract

Routing unknown quantum information across distributed communication networks requires autonomous, measurement-free mechanisms to prevent wave-function collapse. Szegedy quantum walks provide a mechanism for spatial state transport. The conventional walks on unweighted graphs suffer from severe back-reflection, spatial dispersion, and channel crosstalk. In this paper, we introduce a deterministic topological quantum routing architecture based on coined Szegedy quantum walks on edge-duplicated signed graphs. By treating edge signs as localized phase shifts within a balanced coin reflection, we enforce an exact zero back-scattering condition across routing nodes. We demonstrate deterministic Perfect State Transfer (PST) with unit fidelity at exact arrival times across fundamental archetypes, including the signed dumbbell switch $D_{2m,0,2n}$ and scalable glued binary trees. Furthermore, we analyse routing performance under realistic open-system amplitude and phase damping noise channels.

Smite: A quasiclassical trajectory (QCT) program for bimolecular collisions and unimolecular dynamics on ab-initio, and machine-learned potential energy surfaces

Summary. This paper introduces Smite, a powerful Python toolkit for performing quasiclassical trajectory simulations of molecular collisions and chemical reactions. It allows researchers to model complex dynamics by coupling classical trajectory propagation with advanced potential energy surface methods, including machine learning potentials. This tool is crucial for testing the limits of statistical theories and capturing non-equilibrium chemical behavior in gas-phase and surface environments.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit focus on molecular dynamics, energy transfer, and the coupling between electronic structure methods (ab-initio/ML) and classical dynamics makes it highly relevant for AMO physicists studying non-adiabatic processes and complex molecular interactions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of modeling chemical reactions and molecular dynamics at the atomic level, especially when simple statistical theories fail to capture complex, non-equilibrium behavior.

Main result. The development of 'Smite,' a comprehensive Python toolkit, allows for detailed, explicit simulations of molecular collisions and reactions across diverse physical regimes.

Method. The core method is the Quasiclassical Trajectory (QCT) simulation, which propagates classical trajectories governed by potential energy surfaces derived from ab-initio or machine-learned calculations.

Model / system. The toolkit is designed for reactive and inelastic bimolecular collisions, unimolecular dynamics, and molecular collisions. It handles complex initial state preparation, including anharmonic sampling for vibration and rotation, and supports multiple potential energy surfaces for surface hopping.

Key observables. Product energy partitioning, stereodynamical correlations, time-resolved vibrational signatures, and cross sections.

Important parameters / regimes. Collision energy, temperature, and the specific nature of the potential energy surface (e.g., derived from DFT or ML potentials).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that explicit dynamics are necessary when statistical assumptions (like negligible recrossing or well-defined reaction coordinates) break down. Limitations include the absence of true quantum tunneling.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the Smite toolkit, detailing its capabilities for initial state preparation, trajectory propagation using various PES sources, and the calculation of multiple diagnostic observables.

Abstract

We present Smite, a Python toolkit for quasiclassical trajectory (QCT) simulations of reactive and inelastic bimolecular collisions, unimolecular dynamics, and molecular collisions with finite surface models. A central feature is the explicit control of reactant preparation: harmonic normal modes admit fixed quantum numbers, prescribed energies, thermal quantum populations, or ground-state Wigner sampling; rotation is prepared at fixed angular-momentum magnitude or from thermal distributions appropriate to diatomic and polyatomic rotors. Rotating-Morse sampling provides a coupled anharmonic treatment of diatomic vibration and rotation, while saved molecular-dynamics phase points provide an alternative source of initial conditions. The same nuclear propagators operate with on-the-fly electronic-structure calculations or user-supplied analytical and machine-learned potential energy surfaces. Additional modules provide constrained rigid-fragment dynamics, thermostat-based preparation, photoionization initial conditions with energy and recoil constraints, and fewest-switches surface hopping (FSSH) on multiple PESs with approximate curvature-derived couplings. Geometry optimization, transition-state searches, crossing-point optimization, intrinsic-reaction-coordinate following, and thermochemistry use the same energy and derivative interfaces. Analysis tools connect trajectories to product energy partitioning, stereodynamical correlations, time-resolved vibrational signatures, and independent-atom x-ray scattering.

Summary. This paper provides a comprehensive real-space study of Helical Trilayer Graphene, using STM and SNOM to image supermoiré lattice relaxation. The authors successfully linked the observed lattice domain structure to the emergence of topological flat bands, identifying a magic angle of 1.9° through both structural and spectroscopic measurements.

Why it may be interesting. The direct experimental mapping of structural relaxation to topological electronic features provides a concrete platform for studying correlated electron physics in tunable 2D materials, which is highly relevant to understanding emergent quantum states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to bridge the gap between the atomic-scale lattice reconstruction and the electronic properties, specifically the topological flat bands, in Helical Trilayer Graphene (HTG).

Main result. The research established a direct microscopic link showing that lattice relaxation dictates the domain structure, which in turn hosts narrow, topologically nontrivial moiré bands. Furthermore, a magic angle of approximately 1.9° was identified.

Method. The work combines experimental techniques like Scanning Near-Field Optical Microscopy (SNOM) and low-temperature Scanning Tunneling Microscopy/Spectroscopy (LT-STM/STS) with lattice-relaxation simulations.

Model / system. The physical system is Helical Trilayer Graphene (HTG), a moire quantum material formed by twisting three graphene layers. The study investigates the supermoiré lattice relaxation, which creates large domains separated by sharp domain walls.

Key observables. Supermoiré lattice relaxation patterns (triangular/stripe domains), flat bands with honeycomb texture, one-dimensional boundary states, and the local Density of States (DOS) measured via STS.

Important parameters / regimes. Twist angle ($ heta$), strain, and carrier density (gate-tunable). A key finding is the magic angle of $\approx 1.9^\circ$.

Assumptions / limitations. The study assumes that the electronic structure is governed by the underlying lattice relaxation and domain morphology, although it notes limitations in resolving correlated gaps at integer fillings.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the device architecture and supermoiré reconstruction via AFM/SNOM, the honeycomb moiré lattice structure, and the dependence of the DOS on the twist angle, confirming the magic angle.

Paper structure. Abstract

Helical trilayer graphene (HTG) has emerged as a highly tunable moire quantum material that hosts strong electronic correlations and nontrivial band topology. However, the atomic-scale lattice structure and local electronic properties have remained largely unexplored. Here we present a comprehensive real-space study of HTG using a combination of scanning near-field optical microscopy and low-temperature scanning tunneling microscopy. We directly image supermoire lattice relaxation, revealing large triangular domains separated by sharp domain walls, as well as stripe domains connected by smoothly varying boundaries. Atomic-scale spectroscopy uncovers flat bands with a honeycomb electronic texture and one-dimensional boundary states confined to domain walls. By systematically varying the twist angle, we identify a magic angle of approximately 1.9°, substantially larger than the 1.6 degree predicted by theory. Our results establish a direct microscopic link between lattice relaxation and flat bands in HTG.

Gibbs Sampling in the Shattered Phase by Decoded Quantum Interferometry

Authors: Leo Zhou, Noah Shutty, Mark Sellke, Stephen P. Jordan
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40345v1

Summary. This paper applies Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI) to sample from the complex energy landscapes of spin glass models. It demonstrates that DQI can overcome topological barriers that cause stable classical sampling algorithms to fail at low temperatures. While showing DQI's power, the authors also note that a classical algorithm (Prange's) can match this performance, suggesting the quantum advantage is not universal for this specific problem.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly addresses the limits of classical simulation techniques in complex, disordered quantum/classical systems, providing a quantum information-theoretic tool (DQI) to probe these limits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the ability of Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI) to sample from the Gibbs measures of classical Ising spin Hamiltonians in the highly complex 'shattered phase' of spin glass models.

Main result. DQI can successfully sample in the shattered phase at temperatures ($eta < eta_{ ext{ALG}}$) where stable classical algorithms fail due to topological barriers, although it is shown that Prange's algorithm can match this performance.

Method. The Gibbs sampling problem is mapped onto a quantum decoding problem, which is then solved using the DQI framework augmented with Unitary State Preparation (DQI+USD).

Model / system. The system is modeled by classical Ising $k$-spin glasses (or Max-$k$-XORSAT) defined on random Erdős-Rényi hypergraphs. The dynamics are governed by the Gibbs measure $\mu_{eta,H}(z) \propto e^{-eta H(z)}$.

Key observables. Inverse temperature ($eta$), total variation distance ($d_{ ext{TV}}$), and the achievable sampling temperature ($eta_{ ext{ALG}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The average degree $D$ and the spin interaction size $k$ are crucial parameters. The dynamical transition temperature $eta_{ ext{dyn}}$ is also key.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the reduction of Gibbs sampling to a quantum decoding problem and assumes the existence of topological barriers (shattering/disorder chaos) that impede stable classical samplers.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the relative positions of different phase transition temperatures ($eta_{ ext{OGP}}$, $eta_{ ext{dyn}}$, and the DQI/Prange sampling range) within the spin glass phase diagram.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the reduction of Gibbs sampling to quantum decoding. It then analyzes the failure of stable classical algorithms in the shattered phase, contrasting this with the success of DQI+USD and Prange's algorithm in reaching lower temperatures.

Abstract

We apply Decoded Quantum Interferometry (DQI) to sample from the Gibbs measures of classical Ising spin Hamiltonians. We show that this Gibbs sampling problem reduces to a quantum decoding problem, and the temperature achievable by DQI is determined by the performance of decoding algorithms. We then focus on the task of Gibbs sampling for classical Ising $k$-spin glasses (or Max-$k$-XORSAT) on random Erdős-Rényi hypergraphs with average degree $D\ge k$. In a temperature range beginning asymptotically at the predicted dynamical phase transition, $β_{\rm dyn}(k,D) = \sqrt{(2\ln k)/D}\times [1+o_{k\to\infty}(1)]$, we show that shattering and disorder chaos form a topological barrier that obstructs many algorithms, including Glauber dynamics and any algorithm whose output distribution is "stable" under perturbations of the input. In contrast, we prove that this barrier can be broken both by a classical algorithm based on Prange's method, and by DQI equipped with a quantum decoder. For example, when $D=αk$ with fixed $α>1$, both Prange's algorithm and DQI can sample at any inverse temperature $β< \tanh^{-1}(1/α)$ for sufficiently large $k$, well beyond the dynamical threshold $β_{\rm dyn} \sim \sqrt{2\ln k / (αk)}$. Therefore, our results show that DQI can overcome topological barriers that obstruct stable algorithms.

Impurity-induced Friedel oscillations and Wigner crystallization in Luttinger liquids via Unified Field Bosonization Technique

Authors: Soundarya P, Venkata Suryanarayana M, Joy Prakash Das
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40053v1

Summary. This paper provides an analytical study of density correlations in 1D Luttinger liquids containing impurities. Using the Unified Field Bosonization Technique, the authors show that the system exhibits a crossover between impurity-driven Friedel oscillations and interaction-driven Wigner-crystal correlations at a critical interaction strength g=1/3. This framework offers a unified description of density modulation in strongly correlated quantum wires.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous analytical treatment of correlation functions in 1D systems, especially the identification of distinct crossover regimes based on interaction strength, provides deep insights into emergent collective behavior in strongly correlated quantum matter.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the competition between impurity-induced Friedel oscillations and Wigner-crystal-like correlations in interacting one-dimensional Luttinger liquids.

Main result. The analysis reveals a critical crossover at the interaction parameter g=1/3, separating regimes where Friedel correlations dominate (g>1/3) from those where Wigner-crystal correlations dominate (g<1/3).

Method. The authors employ the Unified Field Bosonization Technique (UFBT) to derive closed analytical expressions for density-density correlation functions, overcoming limitations of perturbative approaches.

Model / system. The system is an interacting one-dimensional electron gas modeled as a Luttinger liquid, subjected to a localized static impurity potential and electron-electron interactions.

Key observables. Density-density correlation functions, specifically the asymptotic decay of the $2k_F$ (Friedel) and $4k_F$ (Wigner-crystal) components.

Important parameters / regimes. The Luttinger liquid interaction parameter, g, which dictates the crossover point at g=1/3.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis utilizes a reflection-coupled harmonic analysis to correctly account for left-right mixing caused by impurity backscattering, which is necessary for inhomogeneous systems.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the envelopes of the $2k_F$ and $4k_F$ correlations for varying g, explicitly mapping the crossover region around g=1/3.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by first establishing the Hamiltonian and the UFBT framework, then deriving separate scaling laws for the $2k_F$ and $4k_F$ correlations, and finally unifying these results to analyze the crossover behavior.

Abstract

We present an analytical study of impurity-induced density correlations in interacting one-dimensional Luttinger liquids using the Unified Field Bosonization Technique (UFBT). Unlike conventional approaches that treat impurities perturbatively, UFBT incorporates localized impurities exactly at the free-fermion level while treating interactions non-perturbatively. Closed analytical expressions for fermionic Green functions and density-density correlation functions are obtained for strongly inhomogeneous systems, enabling a unified description of the impurity-induced $2k_F$ Friedel oscillations and the $4k_F$ Wigner-crystal like correlations. The corresponding scaling behaviour is derived analytically, revealing a crossover in the dominant long-distance correlations at $g=1/3$: Friedel correlations dominate for $g>1/3$, whereas Wigner-crystal like correlations become dominant for $g<1/3$, corresponding to the regime of sufficiently strong repulsive interactions. A reflection-coupled harmonic analysis is developed to account for the left-right mixing induced by impurity backscattering and to consistently reproduce the resulting density correlations. The spinless limit is also obtained as a direct reduction of the spinful formulation. These results provide an analytically controlled framework for describing impurity-induced and interaction-driven density correlations in strongly inhomogeneous one-dimensional quantum systems.

Optical observation of interlayer spin correlation

Summary. This paper introduces two-magnon Raman scattering as a novel, direct optical probe for measuring interlayer spin correlations in layered antiferromagnets. By analyzing the spectral features and observing the signal's collapse under an external magnetic field, the authors confirm that the measured signal originates specifically from the interlayer magnetic bonds. This technique opens a new window into the fundamental magnetic physics of van der Waals magnets.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, non-invasive optical tool (magnon-pair Raman spectroscopy) to probe fundamental, hard-to-measure quantities like interlayer spin correlations in 2D magnets, bridging the gap between theoretical models and experimental observation in strongly correlated systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Interlayer spin correlation in layered quantum magnets is crucial for understanding their magnetic ground state and spin transport, but it has been experimentally difficult to measure directly.

Main result. The authors report the first direct optical measurement of interlayer spin correlations using two-magnon Raman scattering in vdW antiferromagnets, confirming its magnetic origin and field dependence.

Method. The study combines two-magnon Raman scattering spectroscopy with theoretical modeling based on the Fleury–Loudon interaction to probe the interlayer spin correlation function.

Model / system. The system is a van der Waals antiferromagnet (AFM) like CrSBr, modeled by a spin Hamiltonian incorporating interlayer exchange coupling. The physics is analyzed using two-magnon continuum theory, which splits into three distinct polarization channels.

Key observables. Two-magnon Raman intensity, which reports the integrated weight of the interlayer bond correlation ($\langle\hat{S}_A \cdot \hat{S}_B angle$), and its dependence on external magnetic field ($B$).

Important parameters / regimes. Interlayer exchange coupling ($J_{z2}$), magnetic field strength ($B$), and the energy gaps associated with optical and acoustic magnon modes (e.g., 46 meV, 82 meV).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the Fleury–Loudon light–matter interaction framework and assumes the two-magnon continuum can be accurately decomposed into distinct polarization channels.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the Raman setup, the theoretical decomposition of the two-magnon spectrum into three channels, and how the measured signal collapses or changes character when the magnetic field drives the system into different magnetic phases (AFM to FM).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical necessity of measuring interlayer spin correlation, introduces the two-magnon Raman scattering technique as the probe, presents the theoretical framework linking the signal to the spin correlation function, and validates the method by showing its sensitivity to the magnetic ordering state via field-dependent measurements.

Abstract

Spin correlations govern numerous collective behaviors of quantum materials, underpinning exotic phenomena such as unconventional superconductivity and topological magnetism. In layered materials, the interlayer spin correlation is important because it characterizes the magnetic ground state and determines spin transport across the interface. Yet interlayer spin correlations have remained hard to measure directly, leaving one of the most basic quantities of two-dimensional magnetism out of experimental reach. Here we provide the first direct optical probe of interlayer spin correlations, using two-magnon Raman scattering in the van der Waals antiferromagnet (AFM) CrSBr, in remarkable agreement with a microscopic spin-wave model without any fitting parameter. Moreover, the two-magnon channel switches on only in the AFM state and vanishes when a magnetic field takes the crystal to a ferromagnetic state. We furthermore establish an exciton-mediated variant of the technique, where tuning the laser near the exciton resonance enhances the signal roughly tenfold due to the exciton's large oscillator strength. Magnon-pair Raman spectroscopy thus opens a direct optical window into interlayer spin correlations in van der Waals magnets, extendable to twisted bilayers and proximity-coupled heterostructures.

Parallel classical simulation of noisy shallow circuits: no quantum advantage in 1D

Authors: Robert Koenig, Marco Tomamichel
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39448v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper provides a classical simulation algorithm for noisy shallow quantum circuits on a 1D lattice. By exploiting the structure imposed by depolarizing noise, the authors show that the problem can be mapped onto a randomized AC^0 circuit. This result demonstrates that for 1D systems, the quantum advantage previously claimed for noisy shallow circuits in higher dimensions does not hold.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, constructive classical simulation technique for a class of quantum circuits, directly challenging the premise of quantum advantage in a specific, physically relevant setting (1D).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether a quantum advantage exists for simulating noisy, shallow quantum circuits in one dimension (1D) compared to classical computation.

Main result. It shows that for 1D noisy circuits, the output distribution can be simulated by a randomized AC^0 circuit, ruling out the unconditional quantum advantage established for 2D/3D noisy shallow circuits.

Method. The authors develop randomized parallel algorithms and construct equivalent randomized Boolean circuits (AC^0) to sample the output distribution of the noisy quantum circuit.

Model / system. The model consists of quantum circuits with $d$ layers of nearest-neighbor two-qubit gates acting on $n$ qubits arranged linearly, subjected to independent depolarizing noise before each layer.

Key observables. Total variation error ($\delta$), parallel runtime, and classical circuit depth/size.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit depth ($d$), system size ($n$), noise probability ($p$), and error tolerance ($\delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The result is specific to 1D geometry and depolarizing noise; the simulation relies on the local effect of noise cutting the circuit into independent pieces.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates a three-layer brickwork circuit on 18 qubits showing the depolarizing noise channel at each location.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by analyzing the simulation complexity, first deriving bounds for general randomized Boolean circuits ($C_2$), then improving the structure to unbounded fan-in circuits ($C_\infty$), and finally achieving constant-depth simulation using lookup tables ($C_{lit}$).

Abstract

We consider quantum circuits consisting of $d$ layers of nearest-neighbor two-qubit gates acting on $n$ qubits arranged on a line, where every qubit is independently depolarized with a constant probability before each layer. We describe a randomized parallel algorithm which samples from the output distribution of any such circuit to within total variation error $δ$, with parallel runtime $2^{O(d)}\log\log(n/δ)$ and $n 2^{O(d)}$ elementary real-arithmetic operations. Without noise, the same approach gives an exact sampler with parallel runtime $O(\log n)$. For constant depth, we further show that the input/output behavior of the noisy quantum circuit is reproduced up to a constant error by a randomized $\mathsf{AC}^0$-circuit, that is, a Boolean circuit of polynomial size and constant depth with unbounded fan-in AND and OR gates and NOT gates. Consequently, every relation problem solved by a noisy constant-depth quantum circuit in one dimension is also solved, with essentially the same success probability, by a randomized $\mathsf{AC}^0$-circuit. This rules out, for noisy circuits in one dimension, the unconditional quantum advantage established for noisy shallow circuits in two and three dimensions, where polynomial-size classical circuits over the same gate set require depth $Ω(\log n/\log\log n)$. Our algorithm exploits the fact that depolarizing noise cuts a one-dimensional circuit into independent pieces of logarithmic width, each of which can be sampled exactly in parallel.

Predicting properties of Scrooge ensembles with high accuracy and low sample complexity

Authors: Yue Wu, Yuzhi Tong, Helen Propson, Yihui Quek, Liang Mao
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39992v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This work introduces efficient theoretical and computational tools to analyze the statistical properties of the Scrooge ensemble, which is key in quantum many-body physics. The authors overcome the difficulty posed by a non-polynomial integrand by developing a highly accurate polynomial approximation. This leads to a quantum algorithm that estimates ensemble moments with significantly reduced sample complexity.

Why it may be interesting. The development of efficient tools to calculate high-order moments for complex quantum ensembles provides crucial theoretical machinery for analyzing quantum many-body dynamics and quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of analyzing and implementing the 'Scrooge moments' ($\chi^{(k)}_{ ext{Sc}}( ho)$), which characterize the statistical properties of the Scrooge ensemble.

Main result. They developed a polynomial approximation for these moments with exponentially improving error bounds and constructed an efficient quantum algorithm to estimate expectation values using low sample complexity.

Method. The core method involves approximating a non-polynomial Haar integrand using a tunable polynomial expansion, which is then implemented via quantum algorithms leveraging Schur transforms and block encoding techniques.

Model / system. The study focuses on the Scrooge ensemble, $ ext{Sc}( ho)$, defined as the maximally random ensemble whose average density matrix is $ ho$. This has applications in quantum many-body dynamics.

Key observables. Scrooge $k$-th moment operator ($\chi^{(k)}_{ ext{Sc}}( ho)$) and observable expectation values $ ext{Tr}[\chi^{(k)} O_k]$.

Important parameters / regimes. Moment order $k$, target error $\epsilon$, and polynomial approximation order $\lambda$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on polynomial approximations derived from Taylor expansions and the efficient implementation of quantum subroutines like symmetric subspace projection.

Figures summary. Figure 1 compares the polynomial approximation $g_\lambda(r_\phi^{k-1})$ to the true inverse term $1/r_\phi^{k-1}$ for various orders $\lambda$, demonstrating convergence.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the mathematical obstacle (non-polynomial integrand) to proposing a polynomial approximation, followed by developing the quantum algorithm for estimation, and finally providing a detailed block-encoding construction for the moments.

Abstract

The Scrooge ensemble associated with a density matrix $ρ$ is the maximally random ensemble whose average density matrix is $ρ$. It has attracted growing interest for its applications to quantum many-body dynamics and quantum information. The intrinsic statistical properties of the Scrooge ensembles are encoded in their higher-order moments, yet they are nontrivial to analyze and prepare for they feature a non-polynomial Haar integrand, blocking direct use of Haar integration machinery. Existing physical mechanisms to sample multiple copies of states from the Scrooge ensembles, meanwhile, typically require costly postselection, rendering them inefficient for large system size. In this work, we develop efficient and accurate methods for analyzing and implementing Scrooge moments. We first introduce a polynomial approximation to the Scrooge $k$-th moments whose error is tunable down to a threshold that improves from inverse polynomial to exponential in $1/||ρ||_\infty$ over previous results. Building on our approximation, we then give the first efficient quantum algorithm that estimates observable expectation values using $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(k^3/ε^5)$ copies of $ρ$ for any target error $ε$ above this threshold. We further provide a construction of a block encoding of the Scrooge $k$-th moment when the preparation circuit of $ρ$ is known. Of independent interest could be a key subroutine in our algorithms, which efficiently implements projection onto the $k$-fold symmetric subspace for $ρ^{\otimes k}$ using $\mathcal{O}(k^3/ε^2)$ samples of $ρ$, avoiding the naive $k!$ postselection cost of such a projection. These results provide new tools for both the theoretical analysis and practical prediction of properties of Scrooge ensembles, with potential applications to quantum many-body dynamics and quantum information processing.

Abstract

Stabilizer operations describe a fragment of quantum theory that is known to be efficiently classically simulable, thanks to the Gottesman-Knill theorem. For this reason, nonstabilizer resources such as magic states are necessary for universal quantum computation. Interestingly, the operational and axiomatic approaches to the resource theory of magic differ: the set of free operations in the former, namely, stabilizer operations (SO), is strictly smaller than that in the latter, namely, completely stabilizer preserving operations (CSPO). A simple example showing the separation is given by a three-qubit stabilizer product basis whose states cannot be perfectly discriminated using SO, but which do admit perfect discrimination using CSPO. Such an ensemble of states is said to exhibit nonstabilizerness without magic (NSWM). Here we obtain a principled understanding of this phenomenon, proving necessary and sufficient conditions for its existence. We first derive a simple criterion to decide whether, given a stabilizer basis, its states can be perfectly discriminated using stabilizer operations alone. We then consider the case where the stabilizer basis contains only product states and use its link with process functions---classical models of paradox-free causal loops---to prove the following: the states in a stabilizer product basis require nonstabilizerness for perfect discrimination if and only if the corresponding process function violates a causal inequality. This provides a new operational meaning to causal inequality violations as witnesses of nonstabilizerness, a form of computational nonclassicality.

Abstract

The generation of certified random bits is an emerging near-term application of quantum computers. Potential applications, such as randomness beacons and CRS generation, require nearly uniform randomness, whose rate (the ratio of min-entropy to bitlength) is ~ 1. However, existing protocols for certified randomness either produce weakly random strings with rate o(1), or they require a trusted random seed. We show how to certify randomness with the optimal rate ~ 1 without requiring any trusted randomness from the verifier. Our protocol is secure unconditionally in the quantum random oracle model (QROM). This improves the result of Coladangelo et al., who certified weakly random strings unconditionally in the QROM, and answers a question posed by Aaronson and Hung. We also define and construct a proof of conditional min-entropy, which certifies optimal min-entropy even conditioned on an adversarially chosen transcript. We show an application of this primitive to randomness beacons, where each pulse should have high min-entropy conditioned on all previous messages.

Abstract

Secure position verification certifies that a remote prover occupies a claimed location, a task that is provably impossible with classical resources alone against colluding adversaries. Most existing quantum position verification schemes require transmitting quantum states between verifiers and provers, and the resulting photon loss makes long-distance verification impractical. Classical verifier position verification (CVPV) schemes with classical verifier-prover communication exist, but rely on complex quantum processes that generate certifiable randomness, placing them outside the reach of near-term hardware. Here we introduce a general compiler that maps any complete-support non-local game with the required quantum advantage into a multi-prover CVPV protocol. This addresses both limitations, with entirely classical verifier-prover communication and all quantum resources confined to shared entanglement and local measurements on the prover devices. Instantiating with the CHSH game enables near-term implementation on existing experimental platforms. We prove finite-size security in the quantum random oracle model for both simultaneous verification of multiple provers and verification of a single prover with multiple prover devices. Notably, security in the multi-prover setting is governed not by the certified randomness of the joint provers' outputs, but by certified blind local randomness that depends critically on the spatial arrangement of provers and verifiers.
