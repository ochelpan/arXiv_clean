Genuine Multipartite Nonlocality Is Fermionic Magic

Authors: Jamal Slim
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39411v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This work establishes that genuine multipartite nonlocality is the key resource for universal fermionic quantum computation, a property that cannot be captured by simple mean-field or Gaussian states. The authors prove this theoretically and experimentally certify this resource using measurements on superconducting quantum processors. It provides a deep link between fermionic physics, nonlocality, and the limits of quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. It bridges fundamental quantum information theory (nonlocality resource) with concrete physical implementations on superconducting qubits, providing a rigorous test of computational resource theory in fermionic settings.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the resource quantification of genuine multipartite nonlocality specifically within fermionic quantum systems, contrasting it with other quantum resources.

Main result. It proves that free fermions are fundamentally incapable of exhibiting genuine multipartite nonlocality, which is identified as the true resource for universal fermionic quantum computation.

Method. The authors use theoretical bounds derived from Mermin and Svetlichny polynomials, combined with experimental measurements on superconducting quantum processors, to test nonlocality.

Model / system. The system under study is free fermions, which can be simulated classically but require non-Gaussian states (like those involving controlled phases) to achieve universal quantum computation.

Key observables. Genuine multipartite nonlocality (measured via Mermin/Svetlichny polynomials), visibility (A), and the raw measured value ($M_{raw}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The number of parties ($n$), the angle $\varphi$ controlling the non-Gaussian element, and the number of qubits (up to 48 on IBM processors).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is primarily restricted to fermionic Gaussian states and specific local bilinear readout settings, though experimental results push beyond these theoretical limits.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates a physical setup using dual-rail logical qubits on six physical qubits to implement controlled phases and matchgates.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from theoretical bounds on fermionic Gaussian states (Theorems 1, 2, 3) to experimental demonstrations of nonlocality using controlled phases on superconducting hardware, culminating in benchmarking the monogamy law across multiple devices.

Abstract

A system of free fermions can be simulated on a classical computer, and a single non-Gaussian state makes it a universal quantum computer. We show that the same line bounds quantum nonlocality. Measured as fermionic qubits, free fermions carry the Bell nonlocality of one entangled pair, no more, however many parties share them, and are never genuinely multipartite nonlocal. Fermion parity is the reason. Genuine multipartite nonlocality is thus the resource of universal fermionic computation, cannot arise from any mean-field state, and we certify it on $48$ qubits of IBM processors.

Geometric estimation of NV charge-state contributions from a low-dimensional spectral representation

Type: both · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.38739v1

Summary. This work introduces a geometric method to accurately quantify the relative contributions of $ ext{NV}^0$ and $ ext{NV}^-$ charge states in diamond PL spectra. By mapping spectral variations into a low-dimensional space and using ZPL data for calibration, the authors achieve high accuracy and robustness against noise. This establishes a general, physically calibrated framework for spectral sensing in quantum materials.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous application of information theory (Fisher Information) to spectral decomposition provides a general framework for sensing, showing how much physical information can be reliably extracted from limited spectral measurements.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of quantitatively determining the relative photoluminescence (PL) contributions of $ ext{NV}^0$ and $ ext{NV}^-$ charge states from a mixed spectrum, overcoming limitations of existing methods.

Main result. A novel geometric method using low-dimensional spectral mapping (CIE XYZ space) successfully estimates the $ ext{NV}^-$ contribution, showing agreement with independent measurements and superior noise robustness compared to ZPL-only methods.

Method. The approach maps spectral variations into a low-dimensional manifold, calibrating the position along this manifold using Zero-Phonon Line (ZPL) information from two excitation intensities via geometric projection.

Model / system. The system is Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) centers in diamond, whose PL spectrum is a superposition of $ ext{NV}^0$ and $ ext{NV}^-$ emission. The spectral variation is modeled as a one-dimensional trajectory.

Key observables. $ ext{NV}^0$ and $ ext{NV}^-$ PL contributions, the charge-state ratio $r$, and the coordinates in the CIE XYZ color space.

Important parameters / regimes. Excitation intensity, Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), and the information retention ratios (e.g., 81% for CIE XYZ).

Assumptions / limitations. The spectral variation is assumed to lie on a low-dimensional manifold, and the method relies on physically calibrating the endpoints using ZPL features.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the geometric mapping of spectra to CIE XYZ vectors, the comparison of estimation results against independent methods (like Dual-Excitation Protocol), and the quantitative comparison of information retention across different spectral representations.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, details the geometric mapping methodology using CIE XYZ coordinates, validates the method against established techniques (DEP), and concludes with rigorous information-theoretic analysis comparing the fidelity of different spectral representations.

Abstract

Nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond exist in neutral (NV0) and negatively charged (NV-) states, and quantifying their respective photoluminescence (PL) contributions is important for charge-state-based measurements. Existing methods either require additional experimental control or may suffer from limited physical identifiability. Here, we introduce a geometric method for determining NV charge-state contributions from PL spectra acquired at different excitation intensities. Each spectrum is mapped into a low-dimensional space through broadband spectral weighting, where changes in the relative NV0 and NV- contributions trace a one-dimensional trajectory. Zero-phonon-line (ZPL) information from two spectra physically calibrates this trajectory, enabling the NV-PL contribution to be determined by geometric projection. Measurements on two bulk single-crystal diamond samples yielded NV- contributions in close agreement with those obtained using an independent dual-excitation reference-spectrum method, with root-mean-square errors of 1.11 and 0.31 percentage points. Under additive spectral noise, the proposed method exhibited more than an order of magnitude less variation than a ZPL-only method. Fisher-information analysis further showed that the three-dimensional CIE XYZ representation retained approximately 81% of the information about the NV- contribution available in the full spectrum. These results establish a physically calibrated approach to low-dimensional spectral estimation that combines reduced experimental overhead with robust parameter estimation and provides a general framework for spectral sensing governed by a small number of physical degrees of freedom.

How Shaped Waves Propagate in Scattering Media

Summary. This work introduces a Matrix Transport Equation (MTE) to describe coherent wave propagation in scattering media, overcoming the limitations of standard radiative transport theory. The MTE tracks phase information via a complex matrix, allowing for the derivation of analytical profiles for shaped wave channels. This framework provides a powerful tool for controlling wave energy in complex, disordered structures.

Why it may be interesting. The development of a matrix transport theory that retains phase information is highly relevant to open quantum systems and quantum optics, providing a framework to control wave dynamics beyond simple diffusive approximations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a transport theory for wave propagation in scattering media when the incident wavefront is deliberately shaped, a regime beyond the scope of conventional radiative transport equations.

Main result. A matrix transport equation (MTE) is established that governs coherent wave propagation, yielding analytical expressions for energy and current densities of transmission eigenchannels.

Method. The theory uses a complex two-by-two matrix transport equation (MTE) that propagates phase information, contrasting it with the scalar radiance used in the standard Radiative Transport Equation (RTE).

Model / system. The system models wave propagation in scattering media, analyzed using the MTE. The theory is applied to characterize transmission eigenchannels in disordered waveguides and slabs.

Key observables. Transmission eigenchannel intensity profiles ($I_T(\mathbf{r})$), transmission eigenvalue distribution ($ ho(T)$), and the matrix radiance ($g(u, \mathbf{r})$).

Important parameters / regimes. The degree of disorder, the mean transmission ($ar{T}$), and the boundary conditions of the scattering region.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the MTE is necessary when wavefront shaping occurs, and the theory can be extended to include absorption and leakage.

Figures summary. Figures compare plane wave propagation (RTE regime) versus shaped wave propagation (MTE regime), and show the derived intensity profiles ($I_T(r)$) against classical diffusion predictions ($ar{I}(r)$).

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the MTE as an extension to the RTE, derives the analytic forms for eigenchannel profiles, and then analyzes the distribution of transmission eigenvalues ($ ho(T)$) in both lossless and lossy regimes, concluding with numerical validation using alternative parametrizations.

Abstract

The radiative transport equation provides a powerful framework for describing wave propagation in scattering media. However, it cannot describe coherent waves whose incident wavefront is deliberately tailored. Here we establish a transport theory for wavefront-controlled waves based on a matrix transport equation. Unlike conventional radiative transport, which propagates a scalar radiance, our theory propagates a complex two-by-two matrix that retains phase information and obeys a nonlinear transport equation, despite the underlying wave dynamics being linear. We use this matrix transport equation to derive the spatial profiles of the energy and current densities of transmission eigenchannels, from closed to open channels, in arbitrary diffusive systems. Remarkably, these profiles can be expressed analytically in terms of the solution of the conventional radiative transport equation for a random wave in the same medium. The theory further extends to energy loss, including absorption and leakage into uncontrolled channels, revealing symmetry breaking of transmission eigenchannels in complex structures. Validated against numerical solutions of the wave equation, we establish matrix transport as a framework for describing and controlling coherent wave propagation in complex media.

Many-body topology in parity-preserving tensor networks

Summary. This work investigates the many-body topology embedded in planar parity-preserving tensor networks by showing its equivalence to several distinct physical models, including the Ising model and loop gas. By unifying these descriptions, the authors map out a comprehensive phase diagram centered around a robust 'topological island' phase. The study demonstrates the power of using complementary theoretical tools to characterize complex quantum critical behavior.

Why it may be interesting. The deep connection between topological order, critical phenomena (Ising model), and modern computational tools (tensor networks) provides a powerful framework for understanding quantum phase transitions in strongly correlated systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the many-body topology encoded within planar parity-preserving tensor networks (ppTNs) and unifies its description across multiple seemingly different physical models.

Main result. The phase diagram is shown to be unified by complementary descriptions (fermionic, loop gas, Ising model), revealing a central 'topological island' phase characterized by a robust $\mathbb{Z}_2$ invariant.

Method. The analysis employs fermionic perturbation theory, tensor-network numerics, and duality transformations to map the system's partition function onto several equivalent mathematical and physical forms.

Model / system. The core model is a two-parameter planar parity-preserving tensor network (ppTN) defined by parameters $a$ and $b$. This model is shown to be equivalent to a loop gas, a deformed toric-code state, and a quartic Ising model.

Key observables. The topological invariant ($\sigma_{\text{topo}}$), the Chern number ($C$), and the phase boundaries separating different ordered phases.

Important parameters / regimes. The two control parameters, $a$ and $b$, which govern the deformation away from the free-fermion limit. The special line $b = 1 - a^2$ is also crucial.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on mapping the interacting system to an effective free-fermion theory to predict phase boundaries, and the cancellation of fixed seam factors confirms the topological invariant's robustness.

Figures summary. Figure 1 provides an overview of the common phase diagram, illustrating the 'topological island' across the loop gas, deformed toric code, quartic Ising, and fermionic representations.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the equivalence between the ppTN and several other models, analyzes the topological invariant using fermionic methods, and uses numerical and analytical techniques to map out the complete phase diagram and critical lines.

Abstract

Planar parity-preserving tensor networks (ppTNs) admit a fermionic formulation in which the Gaussian, efficiently contractible limit is deformed by local interactions. We investigate how many-body methods can be used to analyze such contractions in a minimal two-parameter ppTN. Its contraction is simultaneously an interacting fermionic partition function, a loop gas, a deformed toric-code norm, and a quartic Ising model, allowing the same phase diagram to be approached with complementary tools. At its center lies a `topological island', characterized beyond the Gaussian limit by a boundary-twist $\mathbb Z_2$ indicator that reduces to Chern-number parity and admits a loop-winding interpretation under duality. Fermionic perturbation theory predicts the interacting phase boundaries, tensor-network numerics establish their critical behavior, and the loop and spin descriptions reveal a self-dual line with a $c=1$ four-state-Potts multicritical fixed point.

Single-particle detection of the Néel transition in LaCrO$_3$ microcrystals via widefield nitrogen-vacancy center magnetic imaging

Summary. This work uses single-particle nitrogen-vacancy (NV) magnetometry to image the magnetic behavior of $ ext{LaCrO}_3$ microcrystals, resolving the Néel transition at the particle level. By mapping stray magnetic fields, the researchers confirmed the antiferromagnetic ordering and provided evidence for structurally pinned magnetic domains. This technique establishes a powerful new tool for correlating local crystal morphology with bulk magnetic phase transitions.

Why it may be interesting. The use of single-particle magnetometry to probe phase transitions and domain structure in magnetic materials provides a highly localized, spatially resolved view of collective phenomena, which is valuable for understanding emergent behavior in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to understand how synthesis conditions and particle morphology influence the underlying magnetic ordering in rare-earth orthochromites, overcoming the limitations of ensemble averaging techniques.

Main result. Single-particle NV magnetometry successfully resolved the antiferromagnetic Néel transition in individual $ ext{LaCrO}_3$ microcrystals, revealing evidence of structurally pinned magnetic domains.

Method. Widefield nitrogen-vacancy (NV) magnetometry was employed to image the stray magnetic fields emanating from individual microcrystals as a function of temperature and applied field.

Model / system. The physical system is $ ext{LaCrO}_3$ microcrystals, which exhibit a temperature-dependent Néel transition from a paramagnetic to an antiferromagnetic state. The magnetic ordering is characterized by canted antiferromagnetism and domain structures.

Key observables. Temperature dependence of the stray magnetic field ($\langle|\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{V}| angle$), magnetic field patterns during thermal cycling, and the comparison of signals derived from different NV center measurements.

Important parameters / regimes. Néel temperature ($ ext{T}_ ext{N}$), temperature range (278 K to 298 K), and the magnitude of the weak ferromagnetic moment.

Assumptions / limitations. The structural scale is assumed to dominate the domain size over the magnetic correlation length, and the diamond temperature is used as a proxy for the particle temperature.

Figures summary. Figures show magnetic field maps across temperature sweeps, comparisons of magnetization curves derived from different NV measurements, and analysis of magnetic field patterns during thermal cycling and field variation.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the need for single-particle characterization, details the NV magnetometry setup and measurements on multiple particles, presents evidence for the Néel transition, and concludes by analyzing domain structure stability through thermal cycling.

Abstract

Rare-earth orthochromites exhibit rich, chemically tunable magnetic behavior, making them attractive for temperature sensing and temperature-dependent switching applications. Realizing this potential requires understanding how synthesis conditions and particle morphology influence the underlying Cr$^{3+}$ magnetic ordering. Orthochromite magnetism has traditionally been characterized by ensemble techniques such as neutron diffraction and SQUID magnetometry, which report only averaged magnetic properties and cannot access particle-level information. Here we use widefield nitrogen-vacancy (NV) magnetometry to image the stray magnetic fields of individual LaCrO$_3$ microcrystals produced via molten salt synthesis, resolving the antiferromagnetic Néel transition in single particles. Of the 18 particles measured between 278 and 298 K, five show a clear onset of magnetization near the ensemble Néel temperature, four show no measurable magnetic signal, and nine display temperature-independent signatures consistent with localized magnetic impurities undetected by ensemble measurements. Below the Néel temperature, particle magnetization remains fixed under a rotating in-plane field, consistent with canted antiferromagnetic ordering rather than paramagnetism, while repeated thermal cycling shows that the magnetic field pattern varies between cooldowns with certain features recurring at the same locations, consistent with micron-scale structurally pinned antiferromagnetic domains in which the sign of the weak ferromagnetic moment is set independently each time they pass through the Néel transition. These results establish single-particle NV magnetometry as a sensitive probe of magnetic ordering and domain structure in individual microcrystals, a key step toward correlating individual particle morphology and defect structure with magnetic ordering in orthochromites.

Summary. This work critically evaluates error mitigation techniques for near-term quantum computers, showing that methods like Zero-Noise Extrapolation can amplify errors, especially under miscalibration. The authors introduce budget-aware evaluation and demonstrate that the entire pipeline is vulnerable to attacks on calibration metadata. It serves as a crucial warning for the field regarding the need for robust, cost-aware validation protocols.

Why it may be interesting. It provides critical, practical guidelines for quantum algorithm development, warning practitioners that simply applying standard mitigation techniques without understanding their failure modes or cost implications can lead to worse results.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the reliability and potential pitfalls of error mitigation techniques in near-term quantum computing, demonstrating that these methods can sometimes amplify errors or fail under specific conditions.

Main result. Error mitigation techniques like ZNE can significantly amplify errors, and learned correctors are vulnerable to metadata attacks, necessitating budget-aware evaluation and rigorous calibration integrity checks.

Method. The authors compare multiple error mitigation strategies (ZNE, CDR, learned neural correctors) by evaluating them under fixed online shot budgets, analyzing tail risk, and stress-testing the calibration metadata using projected gradient descent.

Model / system. The study uses simulated quantum circuits (VQA ansatzes) on $n=4-16$ qubits, tested against both simulated noise and a small IBM Heron hardware platform. The core focus is on estimating expectation values $\langle H angle$ in the presence of noise.

Key observables. Mean error, median error, $p_{95}$ error, and the fraction of instances where the mitigation method performs worse than the raw, unmitigated estimate.

Important parameters / regimes. Online shot budget, offline training cost, coherent offset ($\delta$), and the integrity of provider-reported calibration metadata.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the mitigation layer is a classical post-processing step, and the study highlights the gap between simulation results and real-world hardware transfer.

Figures summary. Figures summarize comparisons of various pipelines across budgets and stress conditions; one figure illustrates the budget-conditioned neural error mitigation approach, and others show error vs. qubit count or miscalibration strength.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the problem of error amplification, then comparing various mitigation methods under fixed budgets, detailing the performance of a learned corrector, and finally conducting a security analysis via metadata stress testing.

Abstract

Error mitigation is expected to turn noisy quantum measurements into more useful estimates. We show that it can instead amplify error. Under control-offset miscalibration, the evaluated unconstrained zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) estimators are worse than no mitigation on 38-63% of simulated instances and produce extreme tail errors. A small IBM Heron study finds worsening on 80-88% of 18 instances across two depths, with mean error 3.0-4.1 times the raw error. In simulation, the median changes little, so median-only monitoring misses the failures. We then compare methods at the same online shot budget per evaluation and report offline training cost separately. After that cost is amortized, a budget-conditioned neural corrector occupies the low-budget end of the simulated accuracy-cost frontier and falls back to the raw estimate when an ensemble disagrees; its hardware transfer remains unsuccessful. Finally, we treat provider-reported calibration as an input that is not bound to the execution-time device state. A white-box projected-gradient stress test on this metadata increases the corrector's error 8.1 times within the feature ranges used for training and evades the disagreement monitor on half of the worsened cases. Together, the results connect budget-aware evaluation, tail risk, and calibration integrity in near-term quantum learning pipelines.

Decoupling of the QAOA into independent spin-boson systems and high-depth performance on pure and mixed spin glasses

Summary. This work develops a generalized spin-boson mapping to overcome the exponential computational barrier in analyzing the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) on large spin glasses. By showing that the system decouples into independent spin-boson units in the infinite-size limit, the authors enable the calculation of QAOA energies at previously inaccessible depths. This provides deep theoretical insight into the scaling and limitations of quantum optimization algorithms.

Why it may be interesting. The spin-boson mapping provides a powerful, analytically tractable framework to analyze complex, many-body quantum optimization algorithms like QAOA, connecting quantum computation performance to established models in condensed matter physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of studying the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) performance on large spin glasses, especially in the high-depth regime, where exact calculations become exponentially costly.

Main result. The authors show that for mixed spin glasses, the QAOA energy calculation can be mapped onto an effective, solvable spin-boson system, allowing for recursive computation at depths previously intractable.

Method. The core method involves establishing a generalized spin-boson mapping by demonstrating that spins approximately decouple into independent spin-boson systems in the thermodynamic limit, enabling the use of tensor network techniques for energy evaluation.

Model / system. The system studied is the spin glass, modeled by a Hamiltonian involving couplings $J(q)$ and coefficients $c_q$. The analysis focuses on the QAOA circuit structure applied to these spin glass Hamiltonians.

Key observables. QAOA energy density ($ u_q(p)$), the algorithmic threshold ($ u_{ ext{alg},q}$), and the relative gap between these two values.

Important parameters / regimes. QAOA depth ($p$), spin order ($q$), and the infinite-size limit ($n o \infty$).

Assumptions / limitations. The spin-boson mapping is an approximation for finite size, becoming exact only in the infinite-size limit, and the method only computes energy, not the full quantum state.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the approximate decoupling of spins into independent spin-boson systems in the thermodynamic limit, and show the decay of QAOA energy density ($ u_q(p)$) versus depth ($p$) for various spin orders ($q$).

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the spin-boson mapping for QAOA energy calculation, providing a recursive procedure. It then applies this to numerically optimize QAOA on pure and mixed spin glasses, analyzing the convergence trends as a function of spin order and depth.

Abstract

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is regarded as a promising candidate for near-term quantum advantage in combinatorial optimization, yet our ability to study it at scale is limited. Exact recursive formulas have been introduced for predicting QAOA performance on large spin glasses, but the computational cost of evaluating them grows exponentially with the number of QAOA layers, preventing the study of QAOA in the promising high-depth regime. In this work, we show that for the task of computing QAOA energy on any mixed dense spin glass problem, spins approximately decouple into independent spin-boson systems. In the infinite-size limit, the decoupling becomes exact, establishing the spin-boson mapping as a natural framework for the many-body physics of QAOA. This generalized spin-boson mapping gives a recursive procedure for computing QAOA energies that can be executed at modest cost using tensor networks. As a numerical application, we optimize QAOA on pure and mixed spin glasses at depths intractable for prior techniques, observing that higher-degree problems require more layers for a comparable approximation ratio while angle optimization becomes more challenging. While decoupling enables the evaluations of QAOA energies at large size and depth, it does not enable strong simulation of QAOA; a quantum computer is required to sample the bitstring corresponding to the predicted energy.

Summary. This paper introduces a quantum algorithm to calculate equilibrium correlation functions by encoding time into a quantum clock register. This technique allows all time lags to be computed simultaneously using a single circuit run, achieving an optimal resource scaling of $O(N_t \log N_t)$. This drastically improves upon previous methods, making the simulation of dynamic properties in many-body systems much more efficient.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a significant algorithmic improvement for calculating fundamental quantities in condensed matter theory, directly impacting how quantum simulations of thermal or equilibrium dynamics can be performed on near-term quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of efficiently calculating equilibrium correlation functions, $G_{AB}(t-t') = \langle A(t)B(t') angle$, which are crucial for describing measurable dynamic quantities in many-body physics.

Main result. The authors propose a novel quantum algorithm that achieves an optimal resource scaling of $O(N_t \log N_t)$ quantum resources, significantly outperforming conventional methods that require $O(N_t^2)$ gates.

Method. The core method involves encoding the time evolution history into a quantum 'clock register' using $\mathcal{O}(\log N_t)$ ancilla qubits, allowing all time lags to be computed simultaneously via a single circuit run followed by a Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT).

Model / system. The method is demonstrated using the Green's function calculation for a one-dimensional spinless Hubbard model, applicable to any system in a stationary state.

Key observables. Equilibrium correlation functions, $G_{AB}(t-t')$, autocorrelation functions, and the spectral function derived from the QFT measurement.

Important parameters / regimes. The time window size $N_t$ (number of time points) and the required accuracy $\epsilon$. The scaling advantage is most pronounced for large $N_t$.

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on the time-translational invariance of equilibrium correlation functions (Toeplitz form) and assumes the system is in a stationary state.

Figures summary. Figure 1 compares the circuit structures for the Hadamard test versus the clock circuit. Figure 3 compares the maximum estimation error for both protocols, showing the clock approach's superior scaling for large $N_t$.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the computational bottleneck of conventional methods. It then introduces the clock-register approach, detailing the circuit structure and the use of QFT for readout. Finally, it rigorously compares the resource scaling and demonstrates the superior performance using the Hubbard model.

Abstract

A majority of measurable dynamic quantities of a physical system is described as an equilibrium correlation function of the form $G_{AB}(t-t') = \left$, where $t'$ represents the impact time and $t$ represents the response time. Conventional quantum algorithms to compute such quantities via Hamiltonian simulation such as the Hadamard test, variational methods, or linear-response-based algorithms share one feature in common: each time point $t-t'$ is calculated by a different quantum circuit, leading to at least $O(N_t)$ total shots, and at least $O(N_t^2)$ total quantum gates. Here, we provide a different approach: utilizing $O(\log N_t)$ ancilla qubits to store time as a quantum variable, and effectively parallelize the computation of an equilibrium correlation function by requiring only a single quantum circuit for all time points. We show that this circuit needs to be run only $O(\log N_t)$ times, which in total requires $O(N_t \log N_t)$ quantum resources in the large system size limits. We prove that this scaling is optimal, and demonstrate our algorithm calculating the Green's function of a one-dimensional spinless Hubbard model.

For the annulene, the quantum-processing-unit (QPU) wall-clock times for hole-doped nonequilibrium dynamics became comparable to the estimated wall-clock time of two classical methods, Majorana propagation and matrix-product-state time evolution based on the time-dependent variational principle; this corresponds to tens to hundreds of compute nodes depending on the task (36 CPU cores per node), assuming near-ideal parallel scaling, bringing the quantum calculation into a wall-clock-time regime comparable to conventional parallel high-performance-computing resources. We also show a situation in which a hybrid quantum--classical calculation is a reasonable choice in terms of both accuracy and computational time. These results show that quantum hardware can become a realistic computational option for chemically relevant electronic dynamics.

Abstract

Nonstabilizerness is a critical resource for quantum advantage, but evaluating it, especially for mixed states, requires superexponentially many samples in system size, making the problem NP-hard. While symmetries are known to reduce this complexity, it is unclear whether they also restrict the amount of magic. In this work, we provide an explicit example of such a symmetry by proving that the Robustness of Magic (RoM) for N-qubit X-states is at most $\sqrt{3}$. Leveraging this symmetry constraint, we introduce a computationally efficient method to lower-bound the RoM of arbitrary many-body states, demonstrating its utility on the ground states of a spin-1/2 Hamiltonian with system sizes well beyond the reach of exact evaluation. Furthermore, for Hamiltonians whose equilibrium states are X-states, we analytically derive the critical temperature at which magic emerges. Our work shows why certain symmetries constrain magic while others do not, provides a scalable lower-bounding method, and identifies a nontrivial regime where many-body nonstabilizerness is analytically solvable.
