Information Potential: A Variational Approach to Quantum Information Complexity

Authors: Penghui Yao, Yifan Zhou
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39328v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · quantum measurements 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces the 'information potential,' a novel variational tool, to overcome the analytical difficulties associated with Quantum Information Complexity (QIC). By relating QIC to the potential's growth, the authors derive optimal lower bounds for communication tasks like AND and Set-Disjointness. This provides powerful new tools for quantifying the inherent information cost of quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on communication complexity, the rigorous mathematical machinery involving variational bounds on quantum divergences and relative entropy is highly relevant to understanding information flow and resource quantification in open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the difficulty of analyzing Quantum Information Complexity (QIC), a key measure for proving quantum communication lower bounds, due to its dependence on hard-to-estimate conditional mutual information terms.

Main result. They introduce the 'information potential' variational approach, which successfully bounds the cumulative potential increase to derive optimal lower bounds for QIC and quantum communication complexity for functions like AND and Set-Disjointness.

Method. The core method is the introduction and analysis of the 'information potential,' derived from the resolvent representation of quantum relative entropy, which allows bounding QIC by tracking potential increments along purification paths.

Model / system. The analysis is applied to quantum communication protocols ($\Pi$) designed to compute specific functions (AND, Set-Disjointness) using local isometries and message transmissions.

Key observables. Quantum Information Complexity (QIC), the information potential, and the total number of qubits communicated ($q_A, q_B$).

Important parameters / regimes. The number of rounds ($r$), the input size ($n$), and the error tolerance ($\epsilon$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the protocol is 'safe' and relies on variational representations and stability arguments to control information leakage.

Paper structure. The paper develops the variational framework using the information potential, applies it to derive tight lower bounds for the AND function, and then extends these techniques to establish strong lower bounds and tradeoffs for the quantum communication complexity of Set-Disjointness.

Abstract

Quantum information complexity (QIC), introduced by Touchette [Touchette, STOC 2015], has been shown to be one of the most powerful methods for proving quantum communication complexity and has also been shown to be equal to amortized quantum communication complexity. Unfortunately, QIC is generally hard to analyze because it is a sum of quantum conditional mutual information terms, which are difficult to estimate. In this work, we introduce a new variational approach, the information potential, for analyzing QIC and proving quantum communication lower bounds inspired by the resolvent representation for quantum relative entropy. This approach connects the QIC of individual messages to a quadratic form, making it more amenable and thus enables us to bound the cumulative positive increments of the potential throughout an interactive quantum protocol by its QIC. Lower bounds on the growth of the potential therefore translate into lower bounds on both QIC and quantum communication complexity. As an application, we give an optimal $Ω(1/r)$ lower bound on the QIC of the two-bit $\mathsf{AND}$ function as well as an optimal $Ω(n/r)$ lower bound on the quantum communication complexity of $r$-round Set-Disjointness, answering an open problem in~[Braverman, Garg, Ko, Mao, Touchette FOCS 2015]. Moreover, we further prove a direct-sum theorem for bounded-round quantum communication complexity of Set Disjointness. With the tight bound on the QIC of $\mathsf{AND}$ function, we further establish a nearly tight tradeoff for the asymmetric quantum communication complexity of $\mathsf{Set} \mathsf{Disjointness}$: $(q_A+1)(q_B+1)=Ω(n)$, where $q_A$ and $q_B$ denote the total numbers of qubits sent by Alice and Bob, respectively.

Summary. This paper introduces a method to solve hard combinatorial optimization problems, like finding the maximum clique, by reformulating them as continuous quadratic programs solvable on a photonic entropy computer. The authors benchmark this platform against leading classical solvers, showing it is highly competitive and establishes entropy computing as a promising new paradigm for tackling classically intractable problems.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles the computational bottleneck of NP-hard problems by proposing a novel, hardware-native approach (entropy computing) that bypasses traditional quantum circuit overheads, making it highly relevant to quantum computation paradigms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the NP-hard problem of finding the maximum clique (or maximum independent set) in a graph.

Main result. The proposed entropy computing platform (Dirac-3S) demonstrates performance matching or exceeding state-of-the-art classical solvers on most benchmark instances.

Method. It reformulates the discrete clique problem into a continuous quadratic program solvable via the Motzkin-Straus theorem, which is then implemented on the photonic entropy computer.

Model / system. The core system is the Dirac-3S photonic entropy computer, which solves the quadratic program subject to probability simplex constraints using photon number encoding. The problem is mathematically mapped to minimizing a quadratic form over the probability simplex.

Key observables. The primary observable is the size of the maximum clique, $\omega(G)$, derived from the minimum energy $f^*$ found by the optimization process.

Important parameters / regimes. The scaling parameter $R$ for the probability simplex, and the number of restarts/initializations used in classical benchmarking.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes that solving the continuous quadratic program on the entropy computer provides a viable pathway to the discrete optimal solution, although extraction methods (rounding, support-based) are necessary.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the mapping from the Max Clique problem to a quadratic optimization problem and show the entropy computing architecture using photon number encoding.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the framework, details the Motzkin-Straus mapping, presents the Dirac-3S platform's implementation, benchmarks its performance against classical solvers (Armijo-PGD, Hexaly) on DIMACS suites, and concludes by establishing the viability of entropy computing for combinatorial optimization.

Abstract

We introduce a framework for combinatorial optimization using sum-constrained continuous quadratic programs solvable by QCi's Dirac-3S photonic entropy computer. This is enabled by the Motzkin-Straus theorem which provides a powerful bridge between discrete clique problems and optimization over the probability simplex. We demonstrate this framework's versatility by solving constraint satisfaction problems, providing extensive benchmarks on the DIMACS suite. The Dirac-3S platform matches or outright leads two independently implemented classical baselines on more than four-fifths of the benchmark instances, reaching the best known solution on nearly all structured graph families, even outperforming both classical solvers on several of the largest instances tested. On the other hand, well-tuned classical continuous optimizers retain an edge only on the hardest planted-clique instances. This work establishes a viable pathway for solving combinatorial optimization problems using natively analog unconventional computing platforms, while positioning entropy computing as a competitive approach for navigating non-convex landscapes and providing rigorous baselines for an emerging computational paradigm.

Topological Transverse Transport without a Gap in Critical Topological Flat Bands

Authors: Chang-geun Oh, Tomoki Ozawa, Jun-Won Rhim
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40065v1

Summary. This work analyzes the thermal transport in Critical Topological Flat Bands (CTFBs), showing that these systems maintain quantized Hall conductivity at zero temperature without a spectral gap. However, the absence of a gap leads to algebraic-logarithmic temperature corrections rather than the exponential suppression seen in gapped insulators. This establishes a critical distinction between the requirements for zero-temperature quantization versus thermal stability.

Why it may be interesting. The findings provide a detailed, quantitative understanding of how topological protection breaks down in gapless systems, offering insights into non-equilibrium thermal transport in strongly correlated or topological materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the finite-temperature transverse transport properties of Critical Topological Flat Bands (CTFBs), challenging the traditional requirement of a bulk spectral gap for quantized Hall transport.

Main result. CTFBs show an algebraic-logarithmic hierarchy of low-temperature corrections ($T\ln(1/T)$, etc.), demonstrating that while a gap is unnecessary for zero-temperature quantization, it remains indispensable for exponential thermal protection.

Method. The authors developed a low-temperature theory using the cumulative Chern weight and analyzed the system under fixed particle number constraints to derive the temperature dependence of Hall responses.

Model / system. The study focuses on CTFBs, which are characterized by an exactly flat band touching a dispersive continuum while maintaining an integer Chern number. The analysis uses a lattice model (e.g., the 2pi-fluxed dice lattice) to calculate transport coefficients.

Key observables. Electrical, thermoelectric, and thermal Hall responses ($\sigma_{xy}, \alpha_{xy}, \kappa_{xy}/T$), and the chemical potential $\mu(T)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$), the spectral gap ($\Delta$), and the characteristic energy scales related to residual dispersion ($W_f$) and disorder ($\Gamma$).

Assumptions / limitations. The fixed particle number condition is crucial, leading to a singular Lambert-W drift of the chemical potential; the analysis also compares results to the grand-canonical ensemble.

Figures summary. Figures compare the band structure and cumulative Chern weight for gapped vs. CTFB systems, and show the temperature dependence of transport coefficients for both lattice calculations and continuum theory.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical framework by comparing gapped Chern insulators to CTFBs, derives the low-temperature expansions for the CTFB under fixed particle number, contrasts this with the grand-canonical case, and discusses the implications for thermal protection.

Abstract

Quantized Hall transport is traditionally anchored to a bulk spectral gap, which isolates the occupied subspace and exponentially suppresses thermal deviations. Recently discovered critical topological flat bands (CTFBs) challenge this paradigm: an exactly flat band touches a dispersive continuum while retaining a well-defined integer Chern number. However, their finite-temperature transverse transport properties remain entirely unexplored. Here, we develop a low-temperature theory of the intrinsic electrical, thermoelectric, and thermal Hall responses in CTFBs. At fixed particle number, the macroscopic flat-band degeneracy forces a singular Lambert-$W$ drift of the chemical potential, generating an algebraic-logarithmic hierarchy of low-temperature corrections: $T\ln(1/T)$ for electrical Hall, $T[\ln(1/T)]^2$ for thermoelectric Hall, and $T[\ln(1/T)]^3$ for thermal Hall conductivity, replacing the activated thermal protection of a gapped Chern insulator. By contrast, externally pinning the chemical potential to the flat-band energy locks the flat band to half occupation at any nonzero temperature, obstructing the recovery of the fully filled topological ground state as $T\to0^+$. Our results establish that a bulk spectral gap is unnecessary for zero-temperature Hall quantization, but indispensable for its exponential thermal protection.

Fault Tolerant Quantum Phases of Matter

Authors: Colin V. Coane, Shouzhen Gu, Aleksander Kubica
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39879v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work introduces a sophisticated classification of quantum phases of mixed states based on their resilience to noise during information transfer. By defining fault-tolerant phase equivalence, the authors provide a powerful tool to distinguish between merely connected states and those that can reliably maintain quantum information under realistic noisy operations. This has significant implications for the theory and implementation of topological quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous mathematical framework connecting topological order, error correction, and noise robustness is crucial for designing practical quantum hardware and understanding the limits of quantum memory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to classify quantum phases of mixed states based on their ability to preserve and fault-tolerantly transfer encoded quantum information.

Main result. It establishes a hierarchy of phase equivalences (LC, QL, FTQL) and proves that fault-tolerant state preparation is possible for qudit stabilizer codes with extended syndromes but not for point-like syndrome measurements.

Method. The authors use circuit-based definitions of phase equivalence, incorporating local channel circuits, measurements, and classical communication, while rigorously enforcing noise robustness.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on systems of qudits on regular lattices embedded in D-dimensional space, particularly examining topological codes like color codes and stabilizer codes.

Key observables. Phase equivalence (FTQL, LC, QL), recovery threshold for local noise, and the ability to perform fault-tolerant state preparation.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensionality of the lattice (D), the local dimension of the qudits (d), and the depth/range of the connecting quantum circuits.

Assumptions / limitations. The classification relies on the assumption that information transfer must be robust to noise, leading to the definition of FTQL equivalence.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the definitions of FTQL phase equivalence, the structure of noisy QL circuits, the hierarchy of phase equivalences, and the comparison of state preparation error scaling based on measurement dimensionality.

Paper structure. The paper develops definitions for different types of phase equivalence (LC, QL) and then introduces the stringent requirement of fault tolerance to define the FTQL phase, culminating in specific proofs regarding code equivalence and state preparation limits.

Abstract

We introduce a classification of quantum phases of mixed states based on the ability to preserve and fault tolerantly transfer information. We formulate phase equivalence between sets of states that encode logical quantum information and are connected by shallow local channel circuits assisted by local measurements and global classical communication of measurement outcomes, where all quantum operations, including measurements, must be robust to noise. We show that the existence of a recovery threshold for local noise is a property of an entire phase. We prove that any two sets of states with nonzero thresholds that are related by finite-depth local channel circuits belong to the same fault tolerant phase. Our approach leads to a classification of subsystem codes without favoring any state of the gauge subsystem, and we demonstrate phase equivalence between 2D and 3D color codes using dimensional jumps within the 3D gauge color code. Lastly, we study how syndrome structure affects fault tolerance in circuits with measurements. We show that qudit stabilizer codes with extended syndromes permit single-shot state preparation, whereas a broad class of shallow circuits measuring point-like syndromes, including state preparation for 2D topological codes and non-Abelian topological orders with solvable anyon theories, is not fault tolerant.

Optimal testing of fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states

Authors: Vishnu Iyer, Bryan O'Gorman, Ojas Parekh, Kevin Thompson, Andrew Zhao
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39113v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 2/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops optimal quantum property testers for several key classes of Gaussian quantum states, including fermionic and bosonic types. It proves that testing membership in these structured classes requires a sample complexity that is constant with respect to the system size, achieving a tight bound of $\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)$. This result is significant because it provides highly efficient verification tools for states common in quantum optics and condensed matter theory.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides fundamental, resource-efficient tools (constant sample complexity testers) for verifying the structure of quantum states relevant to quantum optics and many-body physics, which is crucial for quantum computation and simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the quantum property testing problem: determining if an unknown quantum state belongs to a specific class of states or if it is far from that class.

Main result. The authors establish a tight, constant sample complexity of $\Theta(1/\epsilon^2)$ for testing five important classes of Gaussian quantum states, independent of the system size.

Method. The testers are constructed using projections onto the 'top' irreducible representation of multiple copies of the state, with optimality proven by matching lower bounds derived from binary state discrimination.

Model / system. The focus is on characterizing and testing five structured classes of pure quantum states: Fermionic Gaussian states, Slater determinants, Bosonic Gaussian states, zero-mean bosonic Gaussian states, and bosonic coherent states.

Key observables. Trace distance ($ ext{dist}(\cdot, \cdot)$), spectral gap of the test projector, and the required sample complexity ($t$).

Important parameters / regimes. The error parameter $\epsilon$ (related to the distance) and the number of copies used in the test (2 or 3).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis extends to the tolerant setting, where the input state may be $O(\epsilon)$-close to the class $C$.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the property testing framework, analyzes the specific Gaussian state classes, develops the projection-based testers, and proves the optimal sample complexity by establishing matching upper and lower bounds.

Abstract

We study the problem of property testing quantum states: given a pure state $|ψ\rangle$ that either (A) belongs to some class $C$ of pure states, or (B) is $\varepsilon$-far in trace distance from all states in $C$, determine which is the case with high probability. It is known that there exists classes $C$ for which the sample complexity of property testing necessarily scales with the size of the system. In this work, we identify classes for which testing only requires a size-independent number of samples. We prove a tight sample complexity of $Θ(1/\varepsilon^2)$ for the property testing of five important classes of Gaussian quantum states: 1) Fermionic Gaussian states, 2) Slater determinants, 3) bosonic Gaussian states, 4) zero-mean bosonic Gaussian states, and 5) bosonic coherent states. While property testers for some of these classes have been studied previously, those analyses only gave upper bounds scaling polynomially in the number of modes. In each case, our tester is the projection onto the ``top'' irrep of two or three copies. We get to constant sample complexity by lower bounding how quickly the rejection probability increases with distance from the class; the main technical component involves bounding the spectral gap of symmetric extension of the test projector. We then establish optimality by proving matching lower bounds based on binary state discrimination for each class. Our testers and optimality claims also extend to the tolerant setting, wherein $ψ$ may be $O(\varepsilon)$-close to $C$ in case (A), rather than lying exactly inside.

Summary. This paper develops communication-robust self-testing protocols for the toric code, a key model in quantum error correction. It extends existing theory to account for limited classical communication between distant parties in a quantum network. The main result demonstrates that the code space can be verified using measurement statistics even when classical messages are exchanged up to a distance proportional to the network size.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a powerful generalization of quantum resource certification, showing how topological quantum error-correcting codes can be verified even in realistic, noisy quantum networks where limited classical communication is possible.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of developing device-independent self-testing protocols for multipartite quantum states, specifically relaxing the strict requirement that parties cannot communicate classically.

Main result. They successfully show that the code space of the toric code can be self-tested in a noise-robust way even when parties can exchange classical messages within a bounded distance proportional to the network diameter.

Method. The authors develop systematic algorithmic methods, involving the analysis of submeasurements and comparison between physical experiment statistics and reference expectations, to certify the required stabilizer properties.

Model / system. The primary model is the toric code, a topological error-correcting code defined on a lattice with periodic boundary conditions. The analysis is extended to a quantum network setting where communication is possible but restricted.

Key observables. Expectation values of products of Pauli operators ($\langle\Psi| O_i eO_{x_i} |\Psi angle$) measured in a Physical Experiment (PE) compared to a Reference Experiment (RE).

Important parameters / regimes. The robustness is quantified by the maximum communication distance $r$, which is shown to be proportional to the network diameter.

Assumptions / limitations. The classical messages exchanged are assumed not to depend on the outcomes of the quantum measurements, and the measurements in the PE are assumed to be projective.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the measurement patterns (certificates) required for self-testing the toric code for increasing rounds of classical communication ($r=2$ through $r=5$), detailing certificates for anticommutation and stabilizer generators.

Paper structure. The paper builds from the general concept of self-testing to the specific case of the toric code. It first establishes the necessary conditions for communication-robust protocols, then details the mathematical framework using submeasurements, and finally presents systematic constructions and results for increasing communication distances $r$.

Abstract

Device-independent self-testing certifies a multipartite quantum state from the correlations it produces alone, without assumptions on the inner workings of the devices. Most existing self-tests, however, break down as soon as the parties can exchange messages. We consider a relaxed setting in a quantum network, where in the honest version of the protocol, the parties never communicate, yet certification remains sound even in an untrusted case where the untrusted devices secretly exchange classical messages within a bounded distance in the network. We show that the code space of the toric code, distributed over a network with the geometry of a torus, can be self-tested in this setting in a noise-robust way, against communication up to distances proportional to the network diameter. We also develop an algorithmic method for constructing such protocols and extend our results to the surface code and cluster states, robust to one round of nearest-neighbor classical communication

Single-shot state preparation threshold: rigorous theorem and statistical mechanical mapping

Authors: Yuanchen Zhao, Yi Yuan, Zhengyi Han, Dong E. Liu
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39965v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper establishes new, weaker structural conditions—specifically linear confinement—that are sufficient to guarantee a non-zero threshold for single-shot state preparation in quantum error correcting codes. By developing a statistical mechanical mapping, the authors provide a powerful tool to analyze the logical error rate, suggesting that even sublinear soundness might suffice for fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides concrete, structural conditions (linear confinement) that guarantee fault-tolerance thresholds, which is crucial for designing practical, resource-efficient quantum computation protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental question of determining the threshold for performing single-shot state preparation in Quantum Error Correction (QEC) codes.

Main result. It proves that linear confinement, a structural property, is sufficient for a nonzero single-shot state-preparation threshold for CSS qLDPC codes, contradicting the prior belief that full soundness is necessary.

Method. The authors use rigorous mathematical proofs, developing a statistical mechanical mapping to relate the logical error rate to a classical partition function, and employing Monte Carlo simulations.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on CSS quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes and the process of preparing logical codewords using only a single round of noisy syndrome measurements.

Key observables. Logical error probability ($P_{ ext{fail}}$), residual data error, and the structure of the error channel $\mathcal{S}$.

Important parameters / regimes. Code distance ($d$), error parameters ($p, q$), and structural properties like linear confinement and linear soundness.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the state preparation is followed by an ideal final recovery, and the statistical mapping relies on maximum-likelihood final recovery.

Paper structure. The paper first defines the necessary preliminaries for CSS codes, then presents the main threshold theorem based on structural properties (soundness vs. confinement), and concludes with the development and application of a statistical mechanical mapping.

Abstract

Preparing logical codewords with a single round of noisy syndrome measurements can reduce the time overhead of fault-tolerant quantum computation. It remains unclear which structural properties of the code suffice to guarantee a threshold for single-shot state preparation. Here we prove that linear confinement suffices for a nonzero single-shot state-preparation threshold for CSS quantum low-density parity-check codes, in contrast to the common belief that soundness is necessary. When the distance is at least logarithmic, we prove that below nonzero local stochastic readout and data error thresholds, the logical error probability after a formal ideal final recovery is exponentially small and approaches zero when the code size approaches infinity. Under the stronger assumption of linear soundness, we show that the residual data error of the preparation protocol is local stochastic, directly allowing composition with other fault-tolerant gadgets. To investigate single-shot state-preparation threshold beyond the above assumptions, we further develop a statistical mechanical mapping that expresses the final logical error rate in terms of a classical partition function. We perform Monte Carlo simulations of this model for the three-dimensional toric code. The numerical results suggest that sublinear soundness can also support a single-shot state-preparation threshold.

Summary. This paper develops optimal, resource-efficient quantum protocols to test if an unknown quantum state is Gaussian, applicable to both fermionic and bosonic systems. Using a novel gradient-flow method, the authors achieve a sample complexity independent of system size, making these tests highly scalable and experimentally feasible. This advances the field of quantum state characterization by providing robust, few-copy measurement schemes.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous, resource-efficient quantum protocols for verifying fundamental structural properties (Gaussianity) in quantum states, which is crucial for validating simulations and understanding physical models in quantum optics and many-body physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental and practical challenge of efficiently testing whether an unknown quantum state possesses the structure of a Gaussian state.

Main result. Optimal and robust algorithms are provided to test fermionic and bosonic Gaussianity with a sample complexity independent of the system size or particle number.

Method. The authors develop a unified 'gradient-flow method' combining representation theory with the geometry of the rejection probability to derive these efficient, few-copy measurement protocols.

Model / system. The focus is on pure fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states, which are central to quantum optics and many-body physics. Specific families include fermionic Gaussian states, general bosonic Gaussian states, and coherent states.

Abstract

Efficiently testing whether a quantum state possesses a given structure is both a fundamental and a practical task in quantum information. Among the most important structured families are Gaussian states, which underpin quantum optics and many-body physics while defining paradigmatic regimes of efficient classical simulation. Despite the central role of Gaussian states, optimal tests of Gaussianity have remained unknown. Here we give optimal and robust algorithms for testing fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states, with sample complexity independent of system size, particle number, and energy. Remarkably, the protocols implement experimentally feasible measurements associated with known exact symmetry characterizations of Gaussianity, using Bell sampling on two copies for fermions and passive interferometry with photon counting on three copies for bosons. We further obtain tolerant testers requiring joint measurements on a larger, but still constant, number of copies. At the heart of our analysis is a unified approach---which we call the gradient-flow method---that applies across all structured families considered here and underlies our optimal testers: representation theory turns approximate satisfaction of the defining symmetry into quantitative control of the gradient of the rejection probability, allowing a continuous descent from the input state to the target family whose length bounds their distance.

Abstract

A recent work studied quantum two-prover one-round proof systems $\mathsf{MIP}^ψ[\text{poly},O(1)]$. It showed that when $ψ$ is a noisy EPR state with quantum maximal correlation $ρ_{\max}(ψ)<1$, the resulting complexity class is equivalent to $\mathsf{NEXP}=\mathsf{MIP}$. A basic example is dephasing noise: a dephased EPR pair retain quantum maximal correlation $1$. In this work, we remove the constant-answer restriction and establish a complete dichotomy for $\mathsf{MIP}^ψ[\text{poly},\text{poly}]$, where the provers share arbitrarily many copies of EPR pairs subject to unital noise. We prove that \[\mathsf{MIP}^ψ[\text{poly},\text{poly}]=\mathsf{MIP} =\mathsf{NEXP},~\text{if $ψ$ is separable or $ρ_{\max}(ψ)<1$};\]\[\mathsf{MIP}^ψ[\text{poly},\text{poly}]=\mathsf{MIP}^ψ[\text{poly},O(1)]=\mathsf{RE},\text{if $ψ$ is entangled and $ρ_{\max}(ψ)=1$.}\] Beyond the complexity classification, our proof develops two techniques that may be useful more broadly in quantum information and quantum complexity. Abstract

The $f$-routing protocol is a leading candidate for quantum position verification (Kent, Munro, and Spiller, 2011), but security guarantees for explicit functions remain limited. We prove unconditional resource lower bounds for uniform attackers; our new techniques bypass communication-complexity bounds central to previous works, which are inherently at most linear in the input length. We show that, for input length $n$ and sufficiently small constant $ε>0$, a uniformly generated strategy using $q$ qubits and having description length $\mathrm{poly}(q)$, with success probability at least $1-ε$ on every input, implies the following computational bounds on $f$: 1. If the strategies are arbitrary quantum channels, then $f\in\mathrm{QSZK}(\mathrm{poly}(nq))$, where $\mathrm{QSZK}(T)$ is the class of languages having quantum statistical zero knowledge proofs in which the verifier runs in time $T$ (and the simulator in time $\mathrm{poly}(T)$). 2. If the strategies are explicit Pauli-sparse unitaries on $q$ qubits that have at most $s$ nonzero Pauli coefficients, then $f\in\mathrm{DTIME}(\mathrm{poly}(nqs))$. 3. If the strategies are Clifford+T circuits using at most $t$ magic gates, then $f\in\mathrm{DTIME}(\mathrm{poly}(nq2^t))$. Time and space hierarchies then yield explicit functions secure against polynomial and even quasipolynomial qubits $q$ under our computational restrictions. These bounds exceed the $q\le\log n$ bound of Bluhm, Christandl, and Speelman (2022) for inner product function $f=\mathrm{IP}$, at the cost of restricting adversarial computation and increasing honest evaluation complexity.

Abstract

We prove maximal separations: $n$-qubit systems can have $Θ(n)$ ergotropy, while every efficient process extracts negligible work, even for Hamiltonians consisting of single-qubit terms. We establish an unconditional existential separation and give an explicit construction in the random oracle model. Assuming the existence of quantum-secure pseudorandom functions, this separation extends to the plain model. This work uncovers an important connection between ergotropy and the complexity of catalytic computation---computation where auxiliary qubits must be finally restored to their initial state. Relative to a random oracle, we establish relational and decision problems that: (i) can be solved efficiently with $λ$ catalysts; but (ii) cannot be solved by any algorithm with $cλ$ catalysts, for any $c<1$. We show this by proving query lower bounds for quantum-space bounded algorithms. As a consequence, for computational ergotropy, catalysts prove to be surprisingly powerful---there is a family of Hamiltonians and states for which catalysts enable efficient extraction of the full $Θ(n)$ ergotropy, while every efficient non-catalytic process extracts negligible work. Furthermore, catalysts also allow us to introduce and instantiate the notion of pseudoergotropy---analogous to pseudorandomness. On the other hand, we show catalysts do not change (information-theoretic) ergotropy. Finally, our work also sheds light on the classical aspect of the problem. First, most of our constructions rely on classical states and Hamiltonians and therefore imply analogous results for classical ergotropy. Second, we show that certain proof of quantumness protocols can be used to generically separate classical and quantum catalytic ergotropy.
