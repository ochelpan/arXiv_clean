Planted Cliques and Quantum Symmetry-Adapted Measurements

Authors: Vojtech Havlicek, Jordan Docter, Subhash Khot
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40310v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work analyzes the quantum feasibility of detecting a planted clique, a problem central to quantum advantage research. By comparing different quantum encodings, the authors demonstrate that symmetry-adapted measurements offer a highly efficient method, achieving near-perfect distinguishability using only one quantum sample. The findings are structural, highlighting the theoretical gap between current quantum capabilities and the conjectured hard classical regime.

Why it may be interesting. It provides deep theoretical insights into the limits of quantum distinguishability for graph problems, connecting abstract concepts from representation theory to concrete quantum computational advantages.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the potential for quantum advantage in solving the planted clique detection problem, which is conjectured to have a computational-statistical gap.

Main result. The authors show that while constant-advantage detection requires a high number of quantum samples using one encoding, symmetry-adapted measurements allow for near-perfect distinguishability using only one coherent quantum sample.

Method. The analysis relies heavily on quantum information theory, representation theory (Schur transform), and calculating distinguishability metrics (like trace distance) between quantum states representing the null and planted graph ensembles.

Model / system. The model is the detection of a planted clique in a classical graph, which is then encoded into quantum states using binary phase states or symmetry-adapted measurements on a full graph register.

Key observables. Distinguishability metrics (e.g., trace distance $D_{Sp}$), required number of quantum samples (copies), and the outcome probabilities derived from Schur sampling.

Important parameters / regimes. Clique size $k$, graph size $n$, and the required number of copies for constant advantage ($\Omega(n^{1+2\epsilon}\ln^2 n)$).

Assumptions / limitations. The results are structural and information-theoretic; the efficient detection from one classical graph in the conjectured hard regime remains an open problem.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes reference a Table 1 summarizing statistical guarantees for various measurement types at a specific clique size $k = \lfloor n^{1/2-\epsilon} floor$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by analyzing two quantum encodings (binary phase state vs. symmetry-adapted measurements). It first establishes the high cost for constant advantage using phase states, then details the superior performance of Schur measurements, culminating in showing that one coherent sample allows for an efficient quantum distinguisher.

Abstract

The planted clique problem is a promising candidate for quantum advantage with a wide computational-statistical gap and substantial evidence for classical hardness. We study two quantum encodings of classical samples, a natural binary phase state encoding and symmetry-adapted measurements, and determine if they preserve enough information for planted-clique detection, as well as discuss their potential towards algorithmic efficiency. For the binary phase state encoding, we show that constant-advantage detection requires $Ω(n^{1+2\varepsilon}\ln^2 n)$ copies, even under arbitrary joint measurements. Measurements on $\tilde{O}(n^2)$ copies suffice statistically above the logarithmic clique threshold. The symmetry-adapted measurements on the full graph register arise naturally from the Schur transform. We show that the outcome distribution of weak Schur sampling depends on the sampled graph only through its edge count and fails to distinguish the distributions; whereas retaining the representation label and Specht register after discarding multiplicity preserves distance $1-o(1)$. Near-perfect distinguishability survives even if the label is also discarded. We calculate the retained states, providing concrete targets for efficient measurement. Finally, we show that one supplied coherent quantum sample enables an efficient quantum distinguisher, which yields a conditional computational separation from one classical sample under quantum planted-clique hardness. Our results are structural and information-theoretic; efficient detection from one classical graph in the conjectured hard regime remains open.

Quantum state learning beyond approximate unitary designs

Authors: Gyungmin Cho, Changhun Oh, Dohun Kim
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39994v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work rigorously distinguishes between merely approximating Haar randomness and actually preserving the learning guarantees associated with that randomness. By analyzing shallow, low-depth Clifford circuits, the authors establish new, stronger bounds for quantum state estimation tasks like observable measurement and purity estimation. This highlights that the structure of the measurement circuit, rather than just moment matching, dictates the achievable performance.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous, circuit-level bounds on the performance of quantum state estimation, which is crucial for designing practical quantum hardware protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the limitations of using approximate unitary designs to guarantee the performance of quantum state learning protocols.

Main result. It proves that shallow, low-depth Clifford circuits can achieve measurement variance scaling matching global Clifford bounds, demonstrating that mere approximate design matching is insufficient for learning guarantees.

Method. The authors analyze the shadow channel and its inverse using circuit structure analysis, comparing results derived from approximate unitary designs against those derived from direct circuit properties.

Model / system. The study focuses on quantum state learning for unknown states $\rho$ on $n$ qubits, utilizing measurements based on two-layer block unitary ensembles (Clifford circuits).

Key observables. Observable expectation values, purity ($\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$), and the stabilizer structure of the state.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit depth (logarithmic vs. linear), design error ($\epsilon_{\text{des}}$), and the number of measurement shots ($N_U, N_S$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis contrasts the necessary conditions for learning guarantees (derived from circuit structure) versus the sufficient conditions provided by approximate unitary designs.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison between different measurement ensembles (e.g., Voroninski, Haar(n)) regarding the required number of samples needed to achieve certain estimation bounds.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general problem of state learning, then showing that approximate design conditions are insufficient, before deriving strong, direct circuit-based bounds for single-shot estimation, and finally analyzing multi-shot limitations.

Abstract

Approximate unitary designs reproduce the statistics of Haar-random unitaries to a given order and accuracy. Recent constructions realize such designs with logarithmic-depth circuits, enabling shallow measurement protocols for various quantum state-learning tasks while preserving performance. These results raise the question of whether approximate designs can replace exact designs more generally. We show that even exponentially small design error need not preserve the learning guarantees of exact designs. This motivates deriving learning guarantees directly from the measurement circuit structure. For observable estimation with classical shadows, we prove that logarithmic-depth two-layer Clifford circuits yield an unbiased estimator matching the global Clifford variance scaling for every state and Hermitian observable. Beyond observable estimation, this bound allows shallow measurements to retain global Clifford guarantees for other tasks, including setting-efficient tomography, mixed-state metrology, and stabilizer structure learning. We complement these statistical guarantees with a compact, exact tensor-network representation of the inverse shadow channel. When measurement bases are reused, approximate unitary designs of arbitrarily high order need not uniformly match the Haar variance in the many-shot limit. For the all-to-all random two-local circuits considered, doing so requires nearly linear depth. Together, our results reveal the capabilities and limitations of shallow quantum state learning, highlighting the distinction between approximating Haar randomness and reproducing its learning guarantees.

The Commuting Local Hamiltonian Problem: Relativized Evidence Against BQP-Hardness

Authors: Itay Shalit, Mark Zhandry
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.38957v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper analyzes the computational complexity of the Commuting Local Hamiltonian (CLH) problem, showing it is complete for the class QIMA. By constructing a specific classical oracle, the authors prove a separation between $ ext{BQP}^{\mathcal{O}}$ and $ ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$, providing strong evidence that the general CLH problem is not as hard as $ ext{QMA}$-complete.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly addresses the computational hardness of quantum many-body problems (like finding ground states), which is central to quantum simulation and quantum computation theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the computational complexity of the Commuting Local Hamiltonian (CLH) problem, a restriction of the local-Hamiltonian problem where Hamiltonian terms must pairwise commute.

Main result. The authors provide relativized evidence against the possibility that the general CLH problem is BQP-hard or QMA-complete by constructing an oracle separation ($ ext{BQP}^{\mathcal{O}} ot\subseteq ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$).

Method. The proof involves constructing a classical oracle $\mathcal{O}$ and utilizing the Forrelation problem to demonstrate that the complexity class $ ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$ is strictly different from $ ext{QMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$.

Model / system. The analysis is framed within quantum complexity theory, comparing the CLH problem (complete for $ ext{QIMA}$) against $ ext{QMA}$ and $ ext{BQP}$ using oracle constructions.

Key observables. The minimum eigenvalue ($\lambda_{\min}$) of the constructed commuting local Hamiltonian, which must be distinguished between $\le \alpha$ (YES) and $\ge eta$ (NO).

Important parameters / regimes. The promise gap ($eta - \alpha \ge 1/ ext{poly}(n)$) and the locality parameter $k$ of the Hamiltonian.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is the formal definition of $ ext{QIMA}$ and its oracle analogue $ ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$, which relies on commuting local gates.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the $ ext{QIMA}$-completeness of the CLH problem, then introduces the oracle model $ ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$, and finally uses the Forrelation problem and diagonalization techniques to prove the separation $ ext{BQP}^{\mathcal{O}} ot\subseteq ext{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$.

Abstract

The commuting local-Hamiltonian (CLH) problem is a restriction of the local-Hamiltonian problem, in which the terms of the Hamiltonian are required to pairwise commute. A long line of work has shown that the problem lies in $\mathsf{NP}$ for certain families of commuting local Hamiltonians. Nevertheless, there has been no formal evidence against the possibility that the general CLH problem is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete. CLH is complete for the complexity class $\mathsf{QIMA}$, defined through quantum verifiers whose local gates commute. Therefore, CLH is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete if and only if $\mathsf{QIMA}=\mathsf{QMA}$. In this work, we introduce a classical-oracle analogue $\mathsf{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$ and construct a classical oracle $\mathcal{O}$ such that $\mathsf{BQP}^{\mathcal{O}}\not\subseteq\mathsf{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$. Since $\mathsf{BQP}^{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$ for any classical oracle $\mathcal{O}$, this implies $\mathsf{QIMA}^{\mathcal{O}}\neq\mathsf{QMA}^{\mathcal{O}}$ for our constructed oracle. Thus, our result provides relativized evidence against the possibility that the general commuting local-Hamiltonian problem is $\mathsf{BQP}$-hard, and hence also against the possibility that it is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete.

The twisted convolution identity and ghost $r$-SICs from finite quantum dilogarithms

Authors: Marcus Appleby, Steven T. Flammia, Gene S. Kopp
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39192v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper proves a generalized twisted convolution identity and establishes the existence of 'ghost r-SICs' using advanced techniques involving the modular quantum dilogarithm. It connects abstract algebraic identities derived from modular forms to concrete structures in quantum information theory, particularly concerning optimal quantum measurements.

Why it may be interesting. The deep connection between number theory (modular forms, dilogarithms) and quantum information structures (SIC-POVMs) suggests underlying mathematical symmetries governing quantum measurement theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to establish the twisted convolution identity (TCI) and prove the existence of 'ghost r-SICs' (configurations of subspaces satisfying a non-Hermitian equichordal condition).

Main result. The authors establish the TCI for specific parameters and prove the existence of ghost r-SICs for any admissible tuple, with genuine r-SICs being conditional on the Stark conjectures.

Method. The proof extends a known pentagon relation from rank-1 to general rank-r tuples, utilizing character theory and explicit dictionaries between modular quantum dilogarithm conventions.

Model / system. The theory is rooted in the modular quantum dilogarithm and its relation to the Shintani-Faddeev modular cocycle. The physical manifestation involves configurations of rank-r subspaces in $\mathbb{C}^d$, relevant to quantum information theory (SIC-POVMs).

Key observables. The existence of ghost r-SICs ($\tilde{\Pi}_p$), the twisted convolution identity (Equation 7), and the modular quantum dilogarithm values.

Important parameters / regimes. Admissible tuples $(d, r, Q)$ satisfying $r < \frac{d-1}{2}$ and $\frac{d^2-1}{r(d-r)} \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation of genuine r-SICs from ghost r-SICs relies on the unproven Stark conjectures regarding the action of the Galois group.

Paper structure. The paper builds by first establishing relationships between the quantum dilogarithm and the modular cocycle (Prop 2.1), proving a pentagon relation for subgroups (Thm 3.1), and finally deriving the TCI (Thm 1.2) which leads to the existence proof for ghost r-SICs (Thm 1.3).

Abstract

Radchenko and Wheeler (RW) recently proved a finite pentagon relation for real quadratic special values of the modular quantum dilogarithm and used it to establish the rank-$1$ twisted convolution identity conjectured by the current authors. RW gave an explicit argument in the principal case and remarked that their proof holds for all rank-$1$ admissible tuples. We extend their proof to all rank-$r$ admissible tuples and provide an explicit dictionary between the modular quantum dilogarithm and the Shintani-Faddeev modular cocycle conventions in the respective papers. Thus, we establish that, if $d,r$ are positive integers such that $r<\frac{d-1}{2}$ and $\frac{d^2-1}{r(d-r)} \in \mathbb{Z}$, then there exist ghost $r$-SICs: i.e., configurations of $d^2$ rank-$r$ subspaces in $\mathbb{C}^d$ that satisfy a non-Hermitian equichordal condition. Under the Stark conjecture, these configurations are Galois conjugate to Hermitian equichordal configurations called $r$-SICs (or rank-$r$ SIC-POVMs).

Summary. This paper proposes a novel quantum optical magnetometry technique using noble gas atoms, specifically ${}^{129} ext{Xe}$, detected via direct ultraviolet laser excitation. By eliminating the need for alkali vapors, it promises long coherence times at room temperature. Theoretical analysis predicts record-breaking magnetic field sensitivities by optimizing laser power and noise suppression techniques.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly applies quantum optics and atomic physics principles to build a next-generation sensor, providing quantitative predictions for performance limits based on fundamental noise sources like laser intensity noise.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing a highly sensitive and precise quantum magnetic sensing technique using noble gas atoms.

Main result. A record-breaking sensitivity of 24 aT/sqrt(Hz) is predicted, achievable with high laser power and low relative intensity noise.

Method. The technique involves monitoring the oscillation of fluorescence power in ${}^{129} ext{Xe}$ due to Larmor precession, read out using a balanced detector scheme.

Model / system. The system uses noble gas atoms, specifically ${}^{129} ext{Xe}$, whose nuclear spin precession is optically detected via a two-photon transition using a UV frequency comb laser. This method avoids mixing alkali vapors, allowing for long spin relaxation times at room temperature.

Key observables. Fluorescence power oscillation amplitude, Larmor frequency ($\omega_B$), and magnetic field strength ($B$).

Important parameters / regimes. High laser power (10 W), low relative intensity noise ($-100 ext{ dBc}/ ext{Hz}$), and the spin relaxation time ($T_2$).

Assumptions / limitations. The initial spin polarization must be created externally, and the two-photon transition is used only for reading out precession, not for pumping.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the proposed experiment schematic for Doppler-free magnetometry, and Figure 1(b) shows the energy level diagram of ${}^{129} ext{Xe}$ detailing the two-photon transition and magnetic field interaction.

Paper structure. The paper analyzes the quantum sensing mechanism, derives the signal model based on decaying sinusoids, and performs a detailed noise analysis to determine the optimal experimental parameters for achieving maximum sensitivity.

Abstract

We analyze a novel approach for quantum magnetic sensing via optical detection of nuclear spin precession in a noble gas. The detection is carried out with an ultraviolet frequency comb laser, so no alkali vapor is required to be mixed with the noble gas during the sensing stage, allowing for hours-long spin relaxation time at room temperature. Our analysis reveals that combining high laser power and low laser intensity noise is crucial to achieving and exceeding state-of-the-art magnetometric performance, including that of cryogenic magnetometers. We find operational regimes where either fundamental or technical noise dominate, which directs experimental effort towards increasing laser power or lowering laser intensity noise, respectively. We also show that optimizing beam size is key to achieving optimal sensitivity. Our calculations predict that a record-breaking sensitivity of $24~\mathrm{aT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ is achievable in 10 minutes with a 10 W ultraviolet laser with a relative intensity noise of -100 dBc/Hz.

Summary. This work proposes a novel two-stage quantum-classical method to solve the difficult problem of optimizing power distribution network topology. It uses an LR-QAOA algorithm, constrained by a cycle-edge encoding, to sample promising network configurations, which then guide a classical optimization solver. The approach shows promise by significantly speeding up convergence on large test networks.

Why it may be interesting. The integration of graph theory constraints (spanning trees) into a quantum optimization framework, coupled with the use of trapped-ion hardware for implementation, provides a concrete example of applying quantum computation to a complex, real-world engineering optimization problem.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the combinatorial optimization problem of Distribution Network Reconfiguration (DNR) to find a low-loss network topology.

Main result. The quantum-guided classical solver significantly reduces the median solver time compared to unguided methods, especially for larger networks, although quantum overhead limits end-to-end speedup on tested systems.

Method. A two-stage quantum-classical approach is used: Stage 1 employs an iterative LR-QAOA using a novel cycle-edge encoding to sample feasible spanning-tree subspaces; Stage 2 guides a MISOCP solver with these samples.

Model / system. The problem is modeled on electrical power distribution networks, constrained by topological requirements (spanning trees). The quantum computation is implemented on a trapped-ion quantum hardware platform.

Key observables. Active power losses (minimized objective function), solver time (guided vs. unguided), and the quality of the resulting network topology.

Important parameters / regimes. The qubit budget (fixed at 29 qubits for testing), the size of the distribution network (33 to 417 buses), and the QAOA circuit parameters ($eta_j, \gamma_j$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes that the quantum sampling can effectively guide the classical solver, and the comparison is an empirical trend, not an asymptotic scaling claim.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the cycle-edge basis for the 33-bus network, the LR-QAOA circuit structure, and comparisons of solver runtimes and line-loss trajectories for different network sizes.

Paper structure. The paper introduces DNR as an MISOCP problem, proposes the cycle-edge encoding to restrict the search space to spanning trees, details the LR-QAOA quantum sampling stage, and concludes by evaluating the full two-stage quantum-classical workflow against unguided classical solvers.

Abstract

Distribution network reconfiguration (DNR) is a combinatorial optimization problem that seeks a low-loss network topology subject to topological and electrical operating constraints. In this work, we propose a two-stage quantum-classical method for DNR. First, an iterative linear-ramp Quantum Alternating Operator Ansatz (LR-QAOA) searches topologically feasible subspaces constructed using a novel cycle-edge encoding. The encoding guarantees that every represented configuration is a spanning tree while allowing the size and coverage of each subspace to be adjusted to the available qubit budget. Second, the resulting sample distribution is used to guide a mixed-integer second-order-cone programming (MISOCP) solver. The method is evaluated on six distribution networks ranging from 33 to 417 buses through simulation and execution on trapped-ion quantum hardware. Across all six test systems, the MISOCP solver guided by hardware-derived distributions reaches an incumbent within 1\% of the best-known solution in less median solver time than the corresponding unguided solver.

Verification Complexity and Extension of Classical Shadows

Authors: Georgios Karaiskos, Asad Raza, Dorian Rudolph, Dax Enshan Koh, Sevag Gharibian
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40107v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work analyzes the computational difficulty of verifying quantum states from classical measurement data (classical shadows). It proves that for many physical systems, verifying the state's validity is computationally hard (QMA-hard). Furthermore, it shows that predicting higher-order quantum correlations from lower-order measurements is generally intractable.

Why it may be interesting. The results establish fundamental computational limits on what can be inferred about a quantum state from limited, local measurements, which is crucial for quantum tomography and quantum machine learning.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the computational complexity of verifying if classical data (a 'shadow') originated from a genuine quantum state, and whether local measurements are sufficient to predict higher-order correlations.

Main result. Classical Shadow Validity (CSV) is shown to be QMA-hard for several physically relevant shadow types (real, fermionic, bosonic), and predicting 3-local properties from 2-local data is generally intractable.

Method. The authors employ complexity theory, relating the problem to known hard problems (QMA-hardness) and using advanced techniques like Schur-Weyl duality and quantum hierarchy analysis.

Model / system. The study is based on the 'classical shadow' framework, where a quantum state $\rho$ is compressed into classical data $S$ via measurements on $n$ qubits. Specific models include real, fermionic, and bosonic systems.

Key observables. Classical Shadow $S$, $k$-local observables (Pauli strings), 2-local marginals, 3-local marginals.

Important parameters / regimes. The complexity class hierarchy (QMA, coNP, $\text{qc-}\Sigma_2$), and the locality of the observables (2-local vs 3-local).

Assumptions / limitations. The hardness results rely on specific constructions for the shadow protocols (e.g., using all $n$-qubit Pauli strings).

Paper structure. The paper is structured around analyzing two main problems: 1) The complexity of CSV for various shadow types (permutation-invariant, real, fermionic, bosonic), and 2) The intractability of extending local measurements to predict higher-order correlations.

Abstract

Classical shadows are an influential framework for compressing copies of a given quantum state $ρ$ into classical data $S$, enabling many properties of $ρ$ to be predicted from relatively few copies. In this work, we study two natural questions involving shadows: (1) Given $S$, when can one efficiently verify that $S$ came from a genuine $n$-qubit state? This is called the Classical Shadow Validity (CSV) problem, introduced by Karaiskos, Rudolph, Meyer, Eisert, and Gharibian [ICALP 2026]. (2) Given $S$ that allows one to capture 2-local properties of $ρ$, can one fake or spoof a shadow $S'$ which predicts 3-local properties of some state? For (1), we show CSV is efficiently solvable for permutation-invariant shadows, QMA-hard for real, fermionic, and bosonic shadows, and both coNP-hard and QMA-hard when the observable family consists of all $n$-qubit Pauli strings. A result of independent interest along the way is a new upper bound qc-$Σ_2$ $\subseteq$ $\mathrm{P}^{\mathrm{PP}}$, where qc-$Σ_2$ is a quantum analogue of the second level of the polynomial hierarchy in which the first proof is quantum. For (2), we show intractability: Given the 2-local marginals $S$ of a quantum state $ρ$, estimating the 3-local marginals of $ρ$ is intractable unless QCMA $\subseteq$ BPP, even if the state $ρ$ is the unique state consistent with $S$.

Abstract

The input-output relation reduces the work needed to perturbatively expand the optical signal, allowing for cleaner derivations and clearer physical interpretations.

Summary. This work develops a theory showing that in coupled topology-geometry systems, structural information hidden from the average measurement can be recovered through the system's linear response (susceptibility). By analyzing microscopic correlations, the authors demonstrate that while the mean behavior is universal, the susceptibility uniquely encodes the underlying graph topology, providing a powerful diagnostic tool for complex networks.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of structural information surviving in linear response, even when removed from the mean, is a powerful general principle applicable to complex systems studied in AMO physics, such as characterizing hidden correlations in quantum many-body states or detecting subtle topological signatures in quantum measurements.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To understand how structural information (topology) embedded in physical networks survives when averaged out by normalization, but reappears through linear response measurements.

Main result. While the mean entanglement propensity is graph-independent under exchangeability, the susceptibility (linear response) retains a dependence on the specific graph structure, revealing hidden topological information.

Method. The authors use fluctuation-response theory applied to a coupled topology-geometry ensemble, decomposing correlations into graph-specific overlap classes.

Model / system. The system models physical networks (e.g., neuronal, vascular) embedded in 3D space, characterized by a graph G=(V, E) and vertex positions X. The coupling is introduced via a bias potential that links edge lengths to the ensemble average.

Key observables. Normalized Crossing-based Entanglement ($\langle A angle_0$), Mean Edge Length ($\langle h angle_\kappa$), and the Susceptibility ($S_G^{(0)}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The coupling parameter $\kappa$, the graph structure (e.g., ER, WS, BA), and the multiplicities ($\alpha_s, \alpha_ imes, \alpha_1$, etc.) derived from the graph structure.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the exchangeable ensemble as a statistical reference state, and the derived response is valid near $\kappa=0$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate that while the mean observables are universal across topologies, the susceptibility $S_G^{(0)}$ varies significantly between different network ensembles, and finite-size scaling shows architecture-dependent growth.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical framework by showing the mean is topology-independent, then derives the susceptibility $S_G^{(0)}$ using microscopic correlation decomposition, and finally validates this prediction by comparing it against measurements from various real and synthetic network ensembles.

Abstract

Topology and geometry are inseparable in physical networks. Here we show that, after factoring out the topology-dependent number of eligible edge pairs, exchangeability makes the mean crossing propensity of those pairs exactly graph independent while preserving topology in fluctuations. We derive a microscopic, parameter-free theory for the response when topology and geometry are coupled. Synthetic and real three-dimensional networks confirm that structure hidden from the normalized mean survives in correlations that determine response.

Entropy-Driven Altermagnetism from Thermal Magnons

Authors: Tanaya Halder, Ashis K. Nandy, Anamitra Mukherjee
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.40002v1

Summary. This work demonstrates that altermagnetism can be thermally induced in antiferromagnets through an entropy-driven instability in the exchange coupling. By showing that the entropic gain from magnon excitation favors an exchange modulation ($\delta$), the authors establish a finite-temperature phase where the system exhibits altermagnetic properties, measurable via transverse spin conductivity.

Why it may be interesting. The mechanism provides a clear, non-conventional route to magnetic ordering (altermagnetism) driven purely by thermal fluctuations and entropy, which is a key concept in understanding complex phase transitions in correlated materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper identifies a mechanism where entropy, rather than symmetry breaking, drives the emergence of altermagnetism in an antiferromagnet upon heating.

Main result. The system enters a finite-temperature altermagnetic phase characterized by an exchange modulation ($\delta$) when the entropic gain overcomes the energetic cost, leading to a non-zero transverse spin conductivity.

Method. The study employs variational spin-wave theory, spin-lattice Monte Carlo simulations, and cluster diagonalization to analyze the free energy landscape.

Model / system. The model is a minimal square-lattice antiferromagnet with nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor exchange interactions, incorporating an exchange modulation ($\delta$).

Key observables. Altermagnetism (momentum-dependent spin splitting), free energy difference ($\Delta f$), transverse spin conductivity ($\sigma_{zxy}/ au$), and the equilibrium modulation $\delta^*(T)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$), exchange coupling constants ($J_1, J_2$), and the instability criterion $\chi_{SW}^{ ext{nem}}(T) > K$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on spin-wave theory for magnon descriptions and treats the exchange modulation ($\delta$) as a static coordinate in some minimization steps.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the shift of the free energy minimum from $\delta=0$ to finite $\delta^*$ upon heating, confirming the entropic origin ($\Delta f < 0$), and present phase diagrams showing the finite-temperature altermagnetic window.

Paper structure. The paper develops the free energy functional, derives the instability criterion based on thermal susceptibility, and then uses both analytical (VSWT) and numerical (SLMC) methods to map out the temperature-dependent phase diagram, confirming the entropy-driven nature of the transition.

Abstract

We identify a route to altermagnetism driven by entropy upon heating and demonstrate it in a minimal square-lattice antiferromagnet. An exchange modulation is entropically favored because the resulting momentum-dependent magnon splitting increases the magnon entropy, thereby lowering the free energy and favoring altermagnetism. We derive an instability criterion for this exchange-modulated state, predict its temperature-driven reentrant behavior, and show that its overlap with Néel order defines a finite-temperature altermagnetic phase. Variational spin-wave theory, spin--lattice Monte Carlo, and cluster diagonalization establish the entropy-driven instability and its coexistence with magnetic order. The resulting state supports a transverse spin conductivity. The mechanism provides a route to thermally induced altermagnetism in compensated magnets coupled to sufficiently soft physical modes.
