Nodal obstruction to conditioned-diffusion representations of the weak momentum

Authors: Chon-Fai Kam, Kai-Wen Wong
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03242v1

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes the weak momentum in quantum systems, showing that standard conditioned-diffusion models fail when the system's two-state amplitude develops nodes. The failure manifests as a divergence in the osmotic component of the weak momentum, which is a key diagnostic distinguishing the quantum reality from purely current-based classical descriptions.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous mathematical constraint on how quantum weak measurements can be modeled using classical diffusion processes, highlighting where the underlying stochastic assumptions break down.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the limitations of applying conditioned-diffusion representations, derived from quantum Schrödinger bridges, to the weak momentum, especially when the two-state amplitude exhibits nodes.

Main result. The positivity hypothesis required by these bridges fails on a definite locus (the nodal curve), causing the osmotic part of the weak momentum to diverge while the current part remains bounded.

Method. The analysis uses the Madelung decomposition of the weak momentum and compares its behavior to established quantum stochastic frameworks like Doob's $h$-transform.

Model / system. The system involves pre- and post-selected quantum states ($\psi$ and $\phi$) and analyzes the weak momentum in the context of a two-slit geometry with separable transverse fields.

Key observables. Weak momentum components (current and osmotic parts), nodal locus of $\phi^*\psi$, and fringe visibility/contrast.

Important parameters / regimes. The order of the zero ($k$) of the two-state amplitude, and the small parameter $ heta$ in related context analyses.

Assumptions / limitations. The results are derived for one-dimensional systems covering separable transverse fields, and the analysis distinguishes between the current velocity and the Itô drift.

Figures summary. Not specified (Notes focus on mathematical derivations and conceptual failures rather than figure descriptions).

Paper structure. The paper builds by first establishing the failure of the positivity hypothesis required by quantum Schrödinger bridges when dealing with the two-state amplitude. It then analyzes the divergence of the osmotic part at the node, contrasting this with the bounded current part, and finally relates these findings to experimental diagnostics like fringe visibility.

Abstract

Quantum Schrödinger bridge constructions establish the existence of a conditioned process under a positivity hypothesis on the endpoint data, and three independent ones adopt it. We show that hypothesis fails on a definite locus once the conditioned object is the two-state amplitude $φ^*ψ$ of a pre- and post-selected pair on configuration space, and that the failure is testable on recorded data. Whenever $φ^*ψ$ has a moving zero of order $k$ across which probability flows, no diffusion of constant diffusion coefficient can both carry the conditioned density and avoid the nodal curve: the current velocity it would need grows as the inverse $2k$-th power of the distance to that curve and points toward it on one side, placing the curve at finite scale distance, so it is reached and not merely approached. Identifying the weak momentum with a bridge drift fails independently: for a free particle post-selected in position the current velocity is exactly minus the drift of the bridge to the target. The positive Doob $h$-transform and the Bernstein conditioning built on it exist wherever $φ^*ψ$ is nodeless and fail on the nodal curve. There the osmotic part of the weak momentum diverges as the inverse distance to the zero and changes sign across it with a coefficient set by $k$, while the current part stays bounded; that boundedness rests on the signed factorization of the real-envelope class and hides the obstruction from any picture built on the current velocity alone. A measured fringe visibility fixes a single length, which puts a Lorentzian of order $10^{-3}$ into the current channel reconstructed in the two-slit trajectory experiment if the contrast is limited by an off-axis zero, and nothing there if by incoherence. The results are one-dimensional, covering the separable transverse field of a two-slit geometry, not general two-dimensional vortices.

Abstract

Bilayer graphene (BLG) quantum dots (QDs) are a promising platform for semiconductor qubits. However, the low-frequency charge noise that may ultimately limit coherence has remained largely unexplored. Here, we systematically characterize charge noise in gate-defined BLG QDs using transport-based noise spectroscopy. We extract a median amplitude of $ S_μ^{1/2} (1~\text{Hz}) = 1.16~μ\text{eV}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$, placing BLG well within the range reported for established semiconductor quantum-dot platforms. Across variations in charge occupation, confinement, source-drain bias, and charge-sensor operating conditions, neither the noise amplitude nor the spectral dependence shows a reproducible trend in electrostatic tuning, indicating that we extracted the intrinsic semiconductor noise. Consistent noise levels are further observed in double QDs and confirmed using an independent superconducting resonator-based dispersive readout. Extending the study to BLG devices incorporating transition metal dichalcogenide layers reveals no measurable charge noise increase in weakly proximitized QDs. These results validate BLG as a viable platform for coherent quantum information processing.

Summary. This paper introduces Error Per Circuit Layer (EPCL), a novel overlap-based benchmark for characterizing noise in large quantum systems. EPCL measures the overlap between two independently evolved quantum registers, circumventing the need for computationally expensive classical simulations of ideal states. The method is validated on quantum hardware, providing a scalable metric for assessing aggregate register performance.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial, hardware-agnostic diagnostic tool for assessing noise in large quantum circuits, which is vital for developing fault-tolerant quantum computation protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing a robust, scalable circuit-level benchmark to characterize noise accumulation in large quantum systems without relying on classical simulation of ideal outputs.

Main result. The proposed Error Per Circuit Layer (EPCL) benchmark successfully measures aggregate register performance via output state overlap, showing clear decay on IBM hardware and remaining compatible with arbitrary gate sets.

Method. EPCL estimates effective layer polarization by measuring the overlap between the output states of two disjoint quantum registers after applying identical random circuits.

Model / system. The study analyzes quantum processors, using two copies of an $n$-qubit register to measure the overlap $ ext{Tr}( ho_A ho_B)$ as a function of circuit depth. The analysis is grounded in depolarizing channel models.

Key observables. Overlap between output states ($ ext{Tr}( ho_A ho_B)$), effective layer polarization ($p_{ ext{eff}}$), and the decay rate of this overlap with circuit depth.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit depth ($d$), number of qubits ($n$), and the effective layer polarization parameter ($p$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes an ensemble-averaged depolarizing model for deriving the expected overlap decay, and it is designed to overcome the classical simulation bottleneck of prior methods.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the overlap measurement using techniques like the Swap Test or Pairwise Bell Measurements, and show the resulting decay curve as a function of circuit depth.

Paper structure. The paper introduces EPCL as an overlap-based primitive, compares it to existing benchmarks (like XEB and EPLG), derives the expected decay under a depolarizing model, and validates the method through numerical simulations and experiments on quantum hardware.

Abstract

Quantum benchmarks provide compact measures of performance that are important for evaluating and comparing quantum systems. Circuit-level benchmarks are particularly valuable because they capture the accumulated effects of noise across interacting operations, but existing approaches may require structured gate sets and costly compilation, classical simulation of reference outputs, or subsystem decompositions that do not capture full-register behavior. We introduce Error Per Circuit Layer (EPCL), an overlap-based circuit-level benchmark that estimates an effective layer polarization by applying identical random circuits to two disjoint quantum registers and measuring the overlap between their output states as a function of circuit depth. EPCL avoids classical simulation of ideal output distributions and recovery to a known reference state, and is compatible with arbitrary gate sets, including non-Clifford gates. We derive the expected overlap decay under an ensemble-averaged depolarizing model and identify the assumptions under which the fitted decay parameter represents an effective layer polarization. Numerical simulations show that EPCL recovers the predicted polarization under weak local stochastic noise and remains well described by a single-exponential decay at stronger stochastic noise levels. The simulations further show that coherent errors associated with fixed entangling layers may require Pauli twirling or randomized compiling to produce the expected decay, while inter-register correlations contribute an additional covariance term to the measured overlap. Finally, experiments on IBM quantum hardware demonstrate clear EPCL decay in 8- and 16-qubit implementations. These results support EPCL as a method for measuring aggregate register performance without requiring classical simulation of ideal circuit outputs or restriction to structured gate sets.

Summary. This paper experimentally validates a compact, fault-tolerant quantum computing architecture using the C4-Helix code on a trapped-ion quantum processor. The authors successfully demonstrated error suppression for memory, executed Clifford group operations with low logical error rates, and established a high-fidelity interface between two different quantum codes. These results confirm the architecture's viability for early-stage fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. For a theoretical physicist, this provides concrete, hardware-validated benchmarks for resource estimation in fault-tolerant quantum computation, particularly regarding the overhead associated with implementing logical gates and state preparation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The challenge is to build a useful fault-tolerant quantum computation architecture that efficiently manages logical encoding, low-overhead logical operations, and access to non-Clifford resources.

Main result. The authors experimentally validated the C4-Helix code on a trapped-ion processor, demonstrating protected memory, low-overhead Clifford group operations, and a high-fidelity interface between two different quantum error-correcting codes.

Method. The work employs repeated Quantum Error Correction (QEC) cycles, randomized benchmarking (TQRB) for Clifford group testing, and benchmarking a heterogeneous interface to prepare entangled states between different codes.

Model / system. The physical platform is a trapped-ion quantum processor (Quantinuum Helios, 98-qubit). The primary theoretical tool is the C4-Helix code, which is tested for its ability to support fault-tolerant computation primitives.

Key observables. Logical error rates ($\epsilon_d$, $\epsilon_C$), GHZ state fidelity, and the efficiency of error suppression compared to physical measurements.

Important parameters / regimes. The target regime is the early fault-tolerant regime, aiming for logical error rates in the $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-8}$ range.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the assumption that the architecture can be validated by demonstrating protected memory, low-overhead Clifford control, and the ability to interface with other codes.

Abstract

Quantum error correction (QEC) is beginning to enable logical operations that outperform their unencoded physical counterparts, but useful fault-tolerant computation will require more than low-error quantum memory. An effective architecture must orchestrate efficient logical encoding, low-overhead logical operations, and access to the non-Clifford resources required for universal computation. Here, we introduce and experimentally validate such an architecture based on the $[[20,2,6]]$ $C_4$-Helix code, designed for the early fault-tolerant regime. Using Quantinuum Helios, a 98-qubit trapped-ion quantum processor, we experimentally demonstrate the principal components of this architecture: we perform repeated quantum error correction with an error of $4.6^{+6.2}_{-2.6}\times10^{-5}$ per logical qubit per QEC cycle. We benchmark the complete Clifford group on the two logical qubits of a single codeblock under active error correction, obtaining an error of $2.8^{+1.0}_{-1.6}\times 10^{-4}$ per two-qubit logical Clifford. We further demonstrate a fault-tolerant chain-map interface between $C_4$-Helix and a distance-5 surface code, preparing a heterogeneous three-logical-qubit GHZ state with a fidelity lower bound of $99.925^{+0.068}_{-0.245}\%$. In each case, the encoded implementation outperforms its corresponding unencoded physical baseline without relying on postselection. Circuit-level simulations indicate that improvements in physical fidelity bring the same architecture into the $10^{-6}$-$10^{-8}$ logical-error regime targeted for early fault-tolerant computation. Together, these results establish $C_4$-Helix as a hardware-validated fault-tolerant architecture rather than a bare quantum memory.

An interpretation-independent formulation of the measurement problem

Summary. This paper reframes the measurement problem not as a physical puzzle to be solved, but as a fundamental statistical inconsistency: quantum mechanics violates the axioms of classical probability theory. By establishing this non-Kolmogorovian nature of quantum statistics, the authors propose a framework that has profound implications for quantum gravity and the nature of objective reality.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a fundamental, statistical framework for understanding the measurement problem that transcends specific interpretations, which is crucial for developing a unified theory of quantum gravity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the quantum measurement problem by seeking an interpretation-independent formulation, rather than attempting a direct solution.

Main result. The core finding is that quantum matter cannot be described by a Kolmogorovian probabilistic theory, suggesting the problem lies in the incompatibility between quantum statistics and classical probability axioms.

Method. The methodology involves analyzing the empirical statistics of quantum measurements, comparing them against the axioms of classical probability (Kolmogorov theory), and drawing parallels with theory-independent theorems like Bell's theorem.

Model / system. The focus is on the abstract mathematical properties of quantum mechanical predictions and their empirical statistics, rather than a specific physical Hamiltonian or system.

Key observables. Violation of the Law of Total Probability (LTP), violation of Bell-type inequalities, and the non-Kolmogorovian nature of quantum interference statistics.

Important parameters / regimes. None specified; the focus is on fundamental axioms and statistical properties.

Assumptions / limitations. The authors assume that the conflict arises because the standard axioms of probability (Kolmogorov) are insufficient to describe quantum reality, leading to the conclusion that no 'God's-eye view' exists.

Paper structure. The paper moves from reviewing the limitations of existing interpretations to establishing the non-Kolmogorovian nature of quantum statistics through statistical diagnostics, and finally discussing the philosophical implications for quantum gravity and the nature of reality.

Abstract

In this paper, we do not try to solve the measurement problem, but rather to properly formulate it. One of the reasons why it still lacks a precise, agreed definition is that the problem may take very different aspects depending on the interpretation of quantum mechanics embraced. Inspired by the methodology of theory-independent results like Bell's theorem, we propose to identify the common root of the puzzle in an interpretation-independent way i.e. as a property of the empirical statistics only, before deriving its philosophical consequences. The key observation is that quantum matter can not be described by a Kolmogorovian probabilistic theory. Arguing that the Kolmogorov axioms of probability theory are the postulates of epistemic uncertainty leads us to reformulate the measurement problem as the impossibility to build an ontology independent of epistemology for quantum matter. Said differently, there exists no God's-eye view on quantum systems. Although it is meaningless to solve the measurement problem as defined in this way, insofar as it is a feature of the universe's statistics, such a formulation may on the other hand bring side benefits. In particular, we argue that it allows to: (i) shed a new light on the variety of interpretations; (ii) propose a fundamental reason why quantum mechanics and general relativity are so incompatible, not relying on purely mathematical or technical arguments; (iii) guide the quest for quantum gravity.

Summary. This theoretical paper tackles the mathematical challenge of describing measurements on separated parts of an entangled quantum system. It introduces a novel framework using free vector spaces to calculate these local measurement projections, successfully bypassing the need to invoke a random collapse model. The key finding is that quantum nonlocality emerges naturally from the way local measurements project the state's possible outcomes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the mathematical difficulty of describing measurement outcomes on physically separated subsystems of an entangled state when the state is represented in a tensor product Hilbert space.

Main result. It proposes a framework using the free vector space to calculate measurement projections, showing that nonlocality arises from the way these local measurements project the possible outcomes, eliminating the need for a random collapse postulate.

Method. The authors develop a mathematical formalism comparing the tensor product space to the free vector space, defining a canonical projection to relate global entangled states to local measurement outcomes.

Model / system. The system involves bipartite entangled quantum states ($\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$) that can be physically separated for measurement. The mathematical model contrasts the tensor product structure with the free vector space structure to handle measurement projections.

Key observables. Measurement outcomes on separated subsystems A and B, and the correlation between these paired outcomes.

Important parameters / regimes. The entanglement structure of the state, and the choice of measurement basis (contextual phase classes).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that measurement outcomes can be derived by projecting the state's equivalence class in the free vector space, rather than relying on a random collapse postulate.

Figures summary. Not specified (The notes detail mathematical derivations and comparisons between different classes of outcomes, rather than describing specific figures).

Paper structure. The paper builds from the initial problem of describing local measurements on entangled states, develops the mathematical machinery using free vector spaces, and concludes by demonstrating how the resulting correlated outcomes reveal the source of nonlocality.

Abstract

The entangled states of composite quantum systems are well studied. The particle-like nature of these systems also means that, while entangled, they can be physically separated and measurements performed on the separated subsystems. Measurements on the separated subsystems A and B should pertain to vectors in the local Hilbert space of each subsystem, but to date it has not been clear how to elucidate the relevant states of the separated subsystems because it is not obvious how to resolve them from states given in the tensor product basis, except for the separable states. Here it is shown that the projections of any general entangled state that are detected by measurements on the separated subsystems can be obtained considering the corresponding (cosets of) states in the free vector space from which the tensor product space is defined. The result eliminates the need to invoke random collapse, and from this perspective nonlocality arises because of the way measurements on each separated subsystem projects possible measurement outcomes.

Robust multi-hypothesis quantum-state discrimination under unknown common unitary perturbations via least favorable priors

Authors: Daichi Fujiki, Fuyuhiko Tanaka

Summary. This paper develops a robust framework for quantum-state discrimination under unknown unitary noise by solving a minimax decision problem. It introduces the Least Favorable Prior (LFP) and shows that the optimal measurement can be found by solving a dual SDP. The resulting LFP-based measurement significantly improves the worst-case success probability compared to standard methods.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, mathematically grounded method for designing quantum measurements that are robust against unknown environmental noise or systematic errors, which is crucial for practical quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses robust quantum-state discrimination when the candidate states are corrupted by an unknown, common unitary perturbation (a nuisance factor). The goal is to find a measurement that minimizes the worst-case error probability over all possible perturbations.

Main result. The measurement derived using the Least Favorable Prior (LFP) achieves a substantially improved worst-case success probability and flattens the success-probability profile compared to standard optimal measurements.

Method. The problem is formulated as a minimax decision problem, which is solved by relating it to a Bayes measurement problem using the LFP. This optimization is implemented via Semidefinite Programming (SDP) techniques.

Model / system. The study considers discriminating between $K$ candidate quantum states $\{ ho_a\}_{a=1}^K$ in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, where the actual states are perturbed by $ ho_a( heta) = U( heta) ho_aU( heta)^\dagger$. The perturbation $ heta$ acts as a nuisance parameter.

Key observables. Worst-case success probability ($V_{ ext{succ}}^{n,N}$), success-probability profiles $P_{ ext{succ}}(M, heta)$, and the weight distribution of the LFP ($\mathbf{w}^\star$).

Important parameters / regimes. The number of states $K$, the dimension of the Hilbert space, and the structure of the nuisance parameter space $\Theta$.

Assumptions / limitations. The theoretical framework handles continuous $\Theta$ but relies on discretizing it into a finite grid $\Theta_N$ for practical SDP computation.

Figures summary. Figures compare success-probability profiles for different measurements (LFP vs. Uniform-prior vs. Reference-point) in a qutrit model, showing the LFP measurement's superior worst-case performance. Table II summarizes these worst-case probabilities.

Paper structure. The paper first formulates the minimax risk and introduces the LFP concept. It then develops the mathematical machinery by showing the equivalence between the minimax problem and a Bayes problem over the LFP. Finally, it applies this framework to numerical examples (qubit and qutrit models) to demonstrate the performance gains.

Abstract

We study robust K-ary quantum-state discrimination when all candidate states are affected by the same unknown common unitary perturbation. The unknown perturbation does not represent the label to be identified, but acts as a nuisance factor that changes the performance of a fixed measurement. We formulate the problem as a minimax decision problem over the possible perturbations and propose using the Bayes-optimal collective measurement associated with a least favorable prior (LFP) on the nuisance-parameter space. For a finite discretization of this space, we show that the LFP can be computed by a semidefinite program and that the corresponding value coincides with the finite-grid minimax success probability. As numerical demonstrations, we consider a binary nonorthogonal qubit model and a nonorthogonal three-state qutrit model with an unknown common unitary perturbation. The LFP-based measurement substantially flattens the success-probability profile and improves the worst-case success probability compared with a reference-point optimal measurement and a uniform-prior Bayes measurement. The resulting LFP concentrates its weight on regions of the nuisance-parameter space that actively limit the robust discrimination performance, thereby providing both a constructive measurement design and a diagnostic description of the difficult nuisance-parameter regimes.

Abstract

We report the measurement of the critical velocity of a paraxial superfluid of light flowing past a mobile impurity, realized using a dual-wavelength optical scheme in a warm vapor of $^{87}\text{Rb}$. Abstract

Time-domain ultrafast and nonlinear terahertz spectroscopy techniques are recently applied to many condensed matter systems for investigating their collective excitations. In centrosymmetric systems, these collective modes are typically Raman-active and therefore do not couple directly to the terahertz electric field. The mechanism by which light-matter interaction realizes in these studies has not been explicitly discussed in detail. In this work, we perform terahertz pump - optical probe and terahertz third harmonic generation investigations on 1T-TaS2, a material exhibiting a rich charge-density-wave (CDW) phase diagram including the commensurate, nearly-commensurate and incommensurate CDW phases. The transition between these distinct states leaves a clear signature on the dynamical Raman response. We investigate how the Raman-active phonons couple to a broadband monocycle terahertz field as well as a narrowband multicycle terahertz field. Our results indicate that a modified impulsively stimulated Raman scattering mechanism involving two-photon absorption, also known as non-resonant Raman scattering, underlies the coherent excitation and observation of the lattice modes. These results are relevant for future spectroscopy investigation and coherent control of collective modes using low-energy terahertz field as well as cavity electrodynamical dressing of solids.
