A quasiparticle-protected superconducting qubit

Authors: Trond Hjerpekjøn Haug, Johan Kolvik, Joey Frey, Paul Burger, Fabian Resare, Witlef Wieczorek, Simone Gasparinetti, Per Delsing, Raphaël Van Laer
Type: experiment · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.12227v1

Summary. This research addresses the vulnerability of superconducting qubits to quasiparticle poisoning from optical fields. By engineering the superconducting material's energy landscape beyond the Josephson junction, the authors developed a Niobium-based transmon that resists optical power-induced relaxation significantly better than standard aluminum devices. This demonstrates a crucial pathway for building more robust, scalable quantum processors.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses hardware limitations in superconducting quantum computing by providing an experimental method to enhance qubit coherence against environmental noise sources like optical fields, which is critical for scaling quantum processors.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Superconducting qubits are highly susceptible to decoherence and errors caused by quasiparticle (QP) poisoning from high-energy radiation and optical fields.

Main result. Engineering the quasiparticle energy landscape beyond the Josephson junction, using a Niobium ground plane, increased the qubit's resilience to optical power by over an order of magnitude compared to all-Aluminum devices.

Method. The study involves fabricating and characterizing superconducting transmon qubits under varying levels of near-infrared optical illumination, measuring coherence times and frequency shifts.

Model / system. The platform is superconducting transmon qubits, comparing an all-Aluminum (Al) device against a novel Niobium (Nb)/Au device. The engineering focuses on controlling the QP energy landscape surrounding the Josephson junctions.

Key observables. Relaxation time ($T_1$), coherence time ($T^*$), QP-induced frequency shift ($\delta\omega_q$), and the onset of excess relaxation as a function of optical power.

Important parameters / regimes. Near-infrared optical power ($P_o$), gap engineering parameters ($\delta\Delta$), and the material choice (Al vs. Nb).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that engineering the QP energy landscape can physically separate the qubit from the sources of poisoning, although dephasing remains vulnerable.

Figures summary. Figures compare $T_1$ and $T^*$ degradation versus optical power for Al and Nb transmons, demonstrating the superior resilience of the Nb device. Simulations also model resonator quality factor ($Q$) dependence on QP sheet resistance.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem of QP poisoning, details the novel Nb/Au device structure, presents comparative measurements showing enhanced resilience, and concludes that gap engineering beyond the junction is crucial for robust quantum processors.

Abstract

Superconducting quantum circuits underpin large-scale quantum processors and are increasingly interfaced with optical systems, yet they are vulnerable to quasiparticle poisoning from high-energy radiation and optical fields. Gap engineering of Josephson junctions suppresses quasiparticle-induced relaxation, but quasiparticles near the junction can still shift the qubit frequency and cause phase errors. Here, we extend gap engineering beyond the junction using a flux-tunable transmon with a gold-on-niobium ground plane and island and strongly gap-engineered aluminum junctions designed to keep quasiparticles away from the junction leads. Compared with an all-aluminum transmon with nominally identical junctions, we demonstrate that the niobium device withstands more than an order of magnitude higher near-infrared optical power before excess relaxation appears. The Nb transmon shows no resolvable quasiparticle-induced frequency shift over the same power range. At the same time, we observe that dephasing and state-preparation-and-measurement infidelity increase with optical power even while relaxation remains protected. These results suggest that engineering the quasiparticle energy landscape beyond the junction can substantially increase qubit resilience, not only to radiation-induced error bursts, but also to nearby optical fields.

Sub-MHz SWAP spectroscopy of two-level systems in superconducting qubits

Summary. This paper reports on an advanced SWAP spectroscopy technique to study TLS defects in superconducting qubits. By using fast flux pulses, the researchers achieved unprecedented sensitivity, detecting coherent TLS dynamics at very low coupling strengths. This work significantly advances the understanding of decoherence mechanisms, which is crucial for building reliable quantum computers.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a highly sensitive experimental platform for characterizing decoherence sources in superconducting qubits, directly impacting the feasibility and fidelity of quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is mitigating energy loss from Two-Level System (TLS) defects in superconducting qubits while achieving high-resolution spectroscopy of these defects.

Main result. The authors successfully detected coherent TLS dynamics at swap rates as low as 0.1 MHz, revealing TLSs with a median energy-relaxation time of 10 microseconds.

Method. They introduced a fast flux pulse sequence for SWAP spectroscopy, allowing for fine frequency resolution and long interaction times to probe weak coupling regimes.

Model / system. The system involves flux-tunable superconducting qubits coupled to TLS defects. The dynamics are analyzed using a dressed-state picture, fitting the coherent swap oscillations to extract relaxation times.

Key observables. Rabi chevrons, swap rates (0.1 MHz), TLS energy-relaxation time ($T_{relax}$), and swap coherence time ($T_{osc}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Sub-MHz frequency resolution, detection of coherence at rates an order of magnitude lower than previously reported.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on fitting the time-domain population decay ($P_1( au)$) to a three-component model involving oscillation, relaxation, and background decay.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the SWAP spectroscopy technique, comparing previous detection limits ($\gtrsim 5$ MHz) to the new capability ($\downarrow 0.1$ MHz), and show the fitted time traces revealing oscillations and exponential decay.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from introducing the problem of TLS loss to detailing the novel fast-flux pulse sequence, presenting the measurements of low-rate Rabi chevrons, and finally analyzing the extracted coherence and relaxation times.

Abstract

An outstanding challenge in superconducting quantum circuits is mitigating loss from two-level system (TLS) defects. SWAP spectroscopy with flux-tunable qubits can detect individual TLSs, but the Rabi chevrons that signal coherent exchange have been observed only for the most strongly coupled defects. Here, we introduce a sequence with fast flux pulses only at state preparation and readout, switched off during the energy-relaxation delay for finer frequency resolution and longer interaction times. We detect Rabi chevrons at swap rates as low as 0.1 MHz, an order of magnitude lower than previously reported. In the dressed-state picture, we fit the coherent swap oscillations to extract the TLS energy-relaxation time. These Rabi chevrons reveal coherent TLSs with a median energy-relaxation time of 10 microseconds, at coupling strengths previously associated with incoherent TLSs. The swap coherence times exceed the qubit T2* limited by flux noise, indicating that the dressed gap protects them from low-frequency 1/f noise.

Quickly extracting fidelity decay rates in random circuit benchmarking experiments

Summary. This paper presents a new classical post-processing technique, the trace filter function, to overcome systematic biases in randomized benchmarking measurements. By suppressing transient decay modes, the method allows for accurate extraction of fidelity decay rates even when experimental circuits are too short for standard estimators to converge. This significantly extends the reliable characterization window for quantum hardware performance.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial algorithmic tool for characterizing decoherence in near-term quantum hardware, directly impacting the ability to benchmark quantum processors against theoretical limits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of obtaining accurate, unbiased estimates of fidelity decay rates from randomized benchmarking (RB) experiments when transient modes bias standard short-depth measurements.

Main result. A novel 'trace filter function' post-processing technique is introduced that provably suppresses these transient modes, enabling reliable fidelity characterization at circuit depths inaccessible to conventional estimators.

Method. The authors develop and analyze a one-parameter family of estimators, culminating in the trace filter function, which is applied to the measured expectation values from random circuits.

Model / system. The work is validated using experiments on a superconducting processor (Rigetti) for one-dimensional circuits up to 12 qubits, utilizing random Clifford circuits generated via brickwork patterns.

Key observables. Fidelity decay rate ($eta$), transient modes, and the variance of the estimators.

Important parameters / regimes. The filter parameter $\alpha$, the circuit depth ($m$), and the qubit number ($n$).

Assumptions / limitations. Assumptions include time-stationary Markovian gate-dependent noise and local-symmetry assumptions for the noise model.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental setup (simulated and physical circuits), the comparison of different methods for determining the mixing length, and the theoretical scaling of moment calculations.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the bias caused by transient modes in RB, introducing the interpolating estimator family, deriving the theoretical properties of the trace filter function (including its efficiency for local circuits), and validating the method with superconducting hardware experiments.

Abstract

Randomized benchmarking (RB) with structured circuits is experimentally scalable, but gives biased short-depth fidelity estimates because incomplete ensemble mixing introduces transient modes. Waiting for these transients to decay in deeper circuits can push the signal below the noise floor. This is a well-established obstacle, e.g. in linear cross-entropy benchmarking (LinXEB). Here we overcome this obstacle solely through classical post-processing. We introduce a one-parameter family of estimators that interpolate between the standard LinXEB-type post-processing and a new post-processing technique we call the trace filter function. Under practical assumptions, the trace filter function provably suppresses transient modes, exposing an interpretable fidelity decay at short depth, with theoretical guarantees comparable to standard RB, at the cost of an increased variance. We bound the variance, analyze the evaluation complexity of the new estimators, and develop practical post-processing algorithms. Experiments on a Rigetti superconducting processor validate the method for one-dimensional circuits of up to 12 qubits: where the standard estimator requires depth beyond a 40-layer experimental window, the trace filter function yields consistent estimates below 10 layers. Trace-filtered randomized benchmarking therefore extends reliable fidelity characterization to relevant structured circuit ensembles and short-depth regimes that are inaccessible to conventional estimators.

Residual-Guided Global-Drive Design in Trapped-Ion Quantum Simulators

Summary. This paper presents Residual-Guided Drive Design (RGDD), a novel optimization protocol for engineering arbitrary interaction graphs in trapped-ion quantum simulators. RGDD iteratively adds laser tones to synthesize a target Hamiltonian while minimizing infidelity and controlling motional excitation errors. This scalable framework significantly expands the programmable capabilities of trapped-ion quantum computing platforms.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the engineering challenge of controlling complex many-body interactions in trapped ions, providing a scalable, systematic method for designing the required laser fields.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central challenge is designing a compact set of global drive spectra to synthesize a target interaction Hamiltonian in trapped-ion quantum simulators with high fidelity.

Main result. The Residual-Guided Drive Design (RGDD) protocol systematically achieves near-theoretical fidelity bounds using a number of laser tones that scales nearly linearly with system size.

Method. RGDD is an incremental optimization strategy that iteratively adds global laser tones, guided by the residual error, to minimize the cosine infidelity between the synthesized and target interaction.

Model / system. The system is trapped-ion quantum simulators, where interactions are modeled using long-range Ising-type Hamiltonians generated by multi-tone global drive laser fields.

Key observables. Cosine infidelity ($I$), minimum number of tones ($M_{min}$), and residual motional excitation ($\alpha_k$).

Important parameters / regimes. Gate time ($t_g$), penalty coefficient ($\lambda_a$), and the ratio of gate time to baseline time ($s = t_g/t_{g0}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The accessible interaction space is fundamentally constrained by the native motional modes of the ion crystal.

Figures summary. Figures compare tone-count scaling (RGDD vs. NNLS) and closure error across different power-law targets; Figure 6 compares infidelity vs. tone count for RGDD versus equal-spacing placement.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the RGDD protocol, details the optimization objective incorporating a closure penalty, and then analyzes its performance by comparing it against baseline methods (like NNLS) and by investigating the trade-off between infidelity and closure error as a function of gate time.

Abstract

Trapped-ion quantum simulators are powerful platforms for exploring many-body physics, yet engineering of arbitrary interaction graphs remains a central challenge. To leverage the intrinsic structure of native motional modes and thereby enhance programmability, a residual-guided optimization protocol is introduced for the systematic design of multi-tone global drive spectra. While the achievable interaction fidelity is fundamentally bounded by the available motional modes, this adaptive strategy converges on the theoretical limit using a number of laser tones that scales nearly linearly with system size. The versatility of this method enables the synthesis of both long-range and traditionally difficult short-range coupling topologies. Additionally, by incorporating a penalty for residual motional excitation, the algorithm effectively mitigates errors associated with incomplete closure of phase-space trajectories. Overall, this scalable framework significantly expands the programmable capabilities of trapped-ion architectures while maintaining low computational overhead and high potential for experimental implementation.

Error-Corrected Memory and Logic on a Heavy-Hex Superconducting-Qubit Processor

Summary. This paper reports on advanced error correction techniques applied to a large superconducting quantum processor. The researchers successfully implemented the dynamic compass code while actively compensating for hardware imperfections like underperforming qubits. By using noise-informed decoding and defect mitigation, they demonstrated that high-fidelity fault-tolerant memory and logic operations are achievable on monolithic, imperfect quantum chips.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, hardware-aware roadmap for scaling quantum computation by directly addressing the practical limitations of superconducting chip fabrication, which is highly relevant to experimental quantum information scientists.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving high-fidelity, fault-tolerant quantum computation and memory using superconducting qubits while accommodating unavoidable component heterogeneity.

Main result. The authors demonstrated that by combining noise-informed decoding, defect exclusion, and leakage post-selection, they maintained high performance, achieving logical error probabilities improvements of up to 35% and Bell fidelities above 97% for lattice surgery.

Method. They employed advanced noise-informed quantum error correction strategies, including using ACES-derived Pauli noise models and implementing defect exclusion and leakage pre-selection filtering.

Model / system. The work is performed on a superconducting-qubit processor with 156 qubits arranged on a heavy-hexagonal lattice, utilizing the dynamic compass code for error correction.

Key observables. Logical error probabilities (for memory), failure probability (for stability), and Bell fidelities (for lattice surgery).

Important parameters / regimes. The performance improvements are quantified relative to baseline measurements, showing gains up to 56% in stability experiments.

Assumptions / limitations. The performance gains rely on the successful combination of multiple advanced noise-informed quantum error correction strategies to mitigate component imperfections.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the dynamic compass code layout on the 156-qubit processor, detailing the syndrome extraction schedule and how underperforming qubits are excluded.

Paper structure. The paper details the implementation of the dynamic compass code on the physical hardware, presents results comparing various noise-informed decoding strategies (including defect exclusion and leakage post-selection), and demonstrates high-fidelity operations like lattice surgery.

Abstract

Large quantum computations require high-fidelity error-corrected memory and fault-tolerant logical operations. To achieve these with superconducting circuits, quantum processors will need many components operating with low error rates. In practice, fabrication of monolithic superconducting devices produces unavoidable heterogeneity in component performance, which the quantum computer must accommodate. We implement strategies to operate a large dynamic compass code on an array of 156 superconducting qubits arranged on a heavy-hexagonal lattice, making use of the entire monolithic quantum processor and redesigning our measurement schedule to exclude underperforming components. We demonstrate improvements of up to $35\%$ in logical error probabilities for a memory experiment by using a noise-informed decoder combined with strategies to exclude the worst-performing qubits and couplers, along with post-selecting on shots with a low probability of leakage errors. In addition to these memory results, we also observe performance gains of up to $56\%$ when these strategies are applied to measurement-based logic, demonstrated by stability experiments. We furthermore demonstrate high-fidelity fault-tolerant logic through lattice surgery. Through pre-selection filtering techniques, we can obtain Bell fidelities above $77\%$, and further logical gap post-selection using exclusive decoding increases these fidelities to above $97\%$. Our experiments demonstrate that high performance can be maintained in key elements of fault-tolerant quantum memory and logic when working on monolithic superconducting quantum processors with underperforming components, by using a suite of noise-informed quantum error correction strategies.

Summary. This work characterizes a novel class of topologically distinct superradiant quantum critical points in cavity-mediated spin chains. By analyzing edge degeneracies and comparing bulk versus boundary critical behavior, the authors prove that this criticality is fundamentally different from conventional transitions. This has implications for understanding topological order in open quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. It bridges concepts from quantum optics (superradiance) with topological quantum matter, showing how topological invariants can characterize critical points in open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To investigate if superradiant phase transitions can exhibit topological universality classes distinct from conventional light-matter coupled systems.

Main result. The critical line separating the superradiant phase from the cavity topological phase is topologically nontrivial, evidenced by robust symmetry-protected edge degeneracies, and this criticality is generic for cluster-qudit chains.

Method. Numerical simulations, including Finite-size Density-Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) and analysis of entanglement entropies and energy spectra.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams showing three distinct phases (FM, Superradiant, SPT), and show finite-size scaling of entanglement entropies and correlation functions confirming topological differences between models.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the phase diagram, using DMRG and finite-size scaling to distinguish between topologically trivial and non-trivial critical points in the superradiant transition.

Abstract

Superradiant phase transitions are fundamental phenomena in light-matter coupled systems. Here, we uncover a novel class of superradiant criticality that is topologically distinct from previously known superradiant transitions. Using cavity-mediated cluster-Ising chains as an illustrative example, we numerically demonstrate that the critical line separating the superradiant phase from the cavity topological phase hosts robust symmetry-protected edge degeneracies, a feature absent in conventional superradiant transitions. Remarkably, although its local bulk critical properties are indistinguishable from those of the previously identified topologically trivial superradiant transition, the two criticalities cannot be smoothly connected without breaking the protecting symmetries or passing through a multicritical point. We further show that such topological superradiant criticality is generic, also occurring in cavity-mediated $\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{}$ cluster-qudit chains, as supported by complementary lattice simulations.

Summary. This theoretical study proposes a controllable method to generate remote nonreciprocal entanglement between magnons in a coupled cavity system. By leveraging the Barnett effect and implementing coherent quantum feedback, the authors show that the entanglement can be selectively generated for one direction of the applied magnetic field while suppressing it in the opposite direction. This offers a versatile platform for quantum information processing using magnonic circuits.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to AMO physics as it combines several advanced topics: cavity quantum electrodynamics (magnonics), non-Hermitian physics (via feedback), and the study of quantum correlations (entanglement) in solid-state systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the generation and control of remote nonreciprocal magnon-magnon entanglement in a coupled cavity magnonic system.

Main result. They demonstrate that nonreciprocal entanglement can be achieved by tuning the system parameters, specifically showing that entanglement exists for one direction of the bias magnetic field while vanishing for the opposite.

Method. The analysis uses Quantum Langevin Equations (QLEs) to model the system dynamics and quantifies entanglement using the logarithmic negativity of the magnon covariance matrix.

Model / system. The system consists of two microwave cavities coupled to YIG spheres (one rotating, one stationary) and driven by squeezed vacuum fields, incorporating the Barnett effect and a coherent feedback loop.

Key observables. Magnon-magnon entanglement ($E_N$), Asymmetry Contrast ($\xi_E$), and critical temperatures for entanglement vanishing.

Important parameters / regimes. Cavity detuning, feedback phase, relative squeezing phase, squeezing factors ($R_1, R_2$), and the sign of the Barnett shift ($\Delta B$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes a lossless feedback loop and neglects propagation delay.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dependence of entanglement ($E_N$) on the relative squeezing phase, feedback reflectivity ($r_b$), squeezing factors ($R_1$), and temperature ($T$) for both positive and negative Barnett shifts.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theoretical model using QLEs, derives the necessary steady-state covariance matrix equations, and then systematically analyzes the entanglement measure ($E_N$) as a function of control parameters to establish the nonreciprocal control scheme.

Abstract

We propose a scheme for generating and controlling remote nonreciprocal magnon--magnon entanglement in a cavity magnonic system with coherent feedback.The two microwave cavities are driven by independent single-mode squeezed vacuum fields, with a rotating YIG sphere in the left cavity and a stationary one in the right cavity. For a fixed rotation direction, reversing the bias magnetic field applied to the rotating sphere changes the sign of the Barnett shift, leading to nonreciprocal magnon--magnon entanglement. By tuning the cavity detuning and feedback phase, entanglement can be generated for one direction of the bias magnetic field while it vanishes for the opposite direction, yielding complete nonreciprocity. Coherent feedback enhances the entanglement at an appropriate reflectivity, whereas stronger feedback can reduce it. The relative squeezing phase provides additional control, and an appropriate choice of unequal squeezing factors improves the entanglement at fixed total input photon occupation. With the squeezed inputs kept fixed, moderate feedback also increases the critical temperature for magnon entanglement.Our results provide a controllable approach to preparing remote nonreciprocal quantum correlations in cavity magnonic systems.

Controlling transitions between nonequilibrium states through active bath engineering

Summary. This research introduces a method to control transitions between non-equilibrium steady states by actively engineering the noise correlations of the surrounding bath. Using a trapped particle model, the authors derive analytical protocols that allow for faster state changes than natural relaxation. The work is significant because it treats the bath's temporal correlations as a controllable parameter, enabling optimization of both speed and energy dissipation.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses non-equilibrium dynamics far from equilibrium, providing a quantitative method to steer system states using engineered environmental correlations, which is highly relevant for understanding open quantum systems and dissipation engineering.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops and demonstrates control protocols for achieving finite-time transitions between active nonequilibrium steady states (NESSs).

Main result. By actively engineering the temporal correlations (noise color) of the bath, transitions can be achieved substantially faster than natural relaxation times, while also allowing optimization of thermodynamic costs.

Method. The core method involves reverse-engineering the required time-dependent correlation rate ($\omega_c(t)$) by solving the coupled differential equations governing the system's second moments (variance and covariance) based on a desired trajectory.

Model / system. The system is modeled as a particle undergoing an Active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process (AOUP) confined in a harmonic trap. The active bath is controlled by modulating the noise correlation rate $\omega_c(t)$.

Key observables. Normalized variance ($\hat{s}(t)$), excess heat ($\hat{Q}_{ex}$), and the transition duration ($t_f$).

Important parameters / regimes. Trap relaxation rate ($\omega_0$), active diffusion coefficient ($D_a$), and the control parameter $\omega_c(t)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on polynomial fitting (e.g., third- or fifth-order) to define the admissible control trajectories, and the system dynamics are analyzed via second moments.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison between polynomial control protocols ($p_3$, $p_5$) and instantaneous step protocols for achieving faster transitions, and show the optimization of excess heat and minimum transition time.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical framework by deriving the moment equations and the required control law $\omega_c(t)$. It then develops and analyzes polynomial protocols ($p_3, p_5$) to achieve specific trajectories, followed by optimization procedures to minimize time or excess heat, culminating in experimental validation using a trapped colloidal particle.

Abstract

We develop a family of control protocols for finite-time transitions between active nonequilibrium steady states using the noise-color (correlation rate) as the sole control parameter. By reverse-engineering the second moment dynamics of an active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, we determine the time-dependent correlation rate required to realize a prescribed evolution of the system's state. We experimentally implement these protocols with a micrometer-sized optically trapped particle coupled to an engineered active bath, demonstrating transitions substantially faster than the natural relaxation while keeping the confining potential, noise amplitude, and temperature fixed. Our approach extends engineered swift-equilibration methods to far-from-equilibrium steady states by directly controlling the temporal correlations of an active environment. We show how physical constraints impose a speed limit on finite-time transitions, while the freedom in choosing the prescribed system evolution can be exploited to eliminate control discontinuities, minimize the admissible transition time, or optimize a thermodynamic cost.

Abstract

Orbital angular momentum (OAM) modes have emerged as an important platform for high-dimensional quantum communication but remain vulnerable to environmental noise and channel imperfections. Here we introduce a logical-qubit representation of OAM propagation in which modes evolve as qubits through noisy quantum channels. In this representation, turbulence manifests as leakage from the logical OAM subspace into turbulence-induced leakage components, providing a unified description of degradation in both single-photon and coherent-beam regimes. We show that turbulence-induced leakage exhibits systematic dependence on turbulence strength and OAM order, revealing structured signatures of information redistribution beyond the logical subspace. Leveraging this insight, we develop a distributed quantum feature-extraction scheme that encodes high-dimensional OAM states into quantum representations and employs quantum convolutional operations to extract turbulence-robust features. Simulations and experimental measurements demonstrate robust extraction of these features across a broad range of turbulence strengths, OAM orders, and propagation conditions. Our results establish a direct link between OAM-channel dynamics, structured leakage, and quantum learning, providing a physics-informed framework for characterizing turbulence-degraded high-dimensional optical states and extracting robust signatures of turbulence-induced information redistribution.

Abstract

Witnessing entanglement between electrons propagating in quantum circuits requires accessing their two-electron coherences, which encode the two-particle quantum correlations generated by Coulomb interactions. Yet, experimental reconstruction of two-electron coherence remains a major challenge. We introduce photo-assisted two-electron tomography, a quantum coherence reconstruction protocol from dc-currents and time-averaged current correlations, routinely measured in electron quantum optics experiments. Driving an energy filter with a small rf tone encodes off-diagonal coherences in the frequency domain into the dc-current response, enabling single-electron tomography without resorting to current-noise measurements. Extending it to two channels, current correlations after two photo-assisted filters reconstruct the two-electron Wigner distribution and isolate its irreducible two-body part. When applied to Coulomb-coupled quantum Hall edge channels, the protocol reveals interaction-induced energy entanglement. Signatures of this entanglement are visible through the violation of an entanglement witness based on Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities, as well as Wigner function negativities, which remain resolved above the thermal background for realistic device parameters. A generalized Franson geometry further extends the approach to intra-channel two-electron coherences. This opens a direct pathway to detect and characterize electronic entanglement in ballistic quantum circuits.
