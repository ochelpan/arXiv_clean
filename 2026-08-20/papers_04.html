Zero Point Density Fluctuations and Electron Brownian Motion

Authors: L. H. Ford, G. O. Heymans, N. F. Svaiter
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17230v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5 · correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This theoretical paper calculates the motion of an electron near a dielectric surface caused by zero point density fluctuations in the material's phonon vacuum. The resulting quantum Brownian motion is found to be significant, potentially surpassing thermal or standard quantum limits. This suggests a measurable quantum noise source for nanoscale electronics and a method to sense vacuum fluctuations.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects vacuum fluctuations (a quantum field effect) to measurable transport phenomena (Brownian motion) at interfaces, suggesting a novel source of quantum noise in nanoscale devices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the quantum Brownian motion of a test charge (electron) induced by zero point density fluctuations within a dielectric material.

Main result. The calculated mean squared velocity due to these quantum fluctuations can be non-zero in both normal and transverse directions, potentially exceeding both thermal motion and standard quantum momentum uncertainty.

Method. The authors calculate the mean squared velocity by deriving the fluctuating electric field from density fluctuations and solving the resulting Langevin equation approach.

Model / system. The model involves a test charge placed near a dielectric interface whose local dielectric properties fluctuate due to zero point phonon vacuum fluctuations. The force on the charge is derived from these fluctuating dipole moments.

Key observables. Mean squared velocity components, $\langle (v_z^{zp})^2 angle$ and $\langle (v_x^{zp})^2 angle, \langle (v_y^{zp})^2 angle$.

Important parameters / regimes. Separation distance $d$, dielectric constant $\kappa$, and the magnitude of the density fluctuation $\hat{ ho}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes small fractional density fluctuations and treats the dielectric constant as static, ignoring dispersion and dissipation effects.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the physical setup: a test charge above a dielectric where local density fluctuations induce a fluctuating electric field, causing Brownian motion.

Paper structure. The argument progresses by defining the density fluctuation source ($\hat{ ho}$), calculating the resulting fluctuating field ($\mathbf{E}_f$), deriving the velocity correlation function, and finally solving the time integral to find the steady-state mean squared velocity.

Abstract

The fluctuations in the phonon vacuum state can lead to zero point density fluctuations in a material, which in turn lead to local zero point fluctuations of the dielectric properties of the material. We argue that the density fluctuations lead to a fluctuating force on a test charge, such as an electron, located a short distance outside of the material. This force is due to fluctuating dipole moments inside the material, and produces Brownian motion of the electron. We calculate the mean squared velocity of the electron in both the normal and transverse direction relative to the boundary of the material. The result is nonzero in both directions, but larger in the normal case. We estimate the magnitude of this quantum Brownian motion and find that, in some cases, it can exceed the effects of both thermal motion and quantum momentum uncertainty. This suggests that the effect may be observable, and could constitute a source of quantum noise in nanoscale devices. It also potentially offers a means to remotely sense zero point density fluctuations in a material.

Finite-range Lattice Momentum Operators for Quantum Field Theory

Authors: Jan C Olivier, Etienne Barnard
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17327v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops a novel, finite-range approach to constructing momentum operators for lattice QFT using a Z-transform framework. By reinterpreting fermion doubling as an aliasing artifact, the authors derive a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) operator that maintains spectral accuracy without resorting to infinite-range terms. This provides a mathematically rigorous and computationally tractable alternative for simulating lattice field theories.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on QFT, the mathematical machinery—using spectral analysis (Z-transform) and constrained approximation to build local operators that mimic non-local continuum behavior—is highly relevant to developing effective Hamiltonians or lattice representations for complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of constructing lattice momentum operators for Quantum Field Theory (QFT) that are finite in range while accurately reproducing continuum physics and avoiding fermion doubling.

Main result. The authors propose a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) operator derived from a Z-transform framework, which achieves spectral accuracy with a strictly finite coupling range, unlike infinite-range approximations.

Method. The core method involves mapping lattice operators to functions on the unit circle (Z-transform) and solving a constrained least-squares approximation problem in the frequency domain to construct the FIR coefficients.

Model / system. The study is set in Lattice Quantum Field Theory, specifically analyzing the momentum representation of fermion fields ($\psi$) in the context of QED or general gauge theories.

Key observables. Dispersion relations, group velocities, and the presence/absence of unwanted zeros (aliasing) in the discrete momentum operator on the unit circle.

Important parameters / regimes. Lattice spacing ($a$), number of taps ($N$), and the momentum variable $ heta$ (angle on the unit circle).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the problem can be reformulated as a constrained approximation problem in the frequency domain, leading to the FIR structure. The gauge-covariant extension remains an open problem.

Figures summary. Figure 1 shows dispersion functions for FIR operators, illustrating spectral gaps for small N. Figure 2 shows the dispersion function for 10 taps.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the Z-transform framework, reformulates fermion doubling as an aliasing problem, derives the FIR operator via constrained approximation, and validates its spectral accuracy against continuum limits and existing lattice methods.

Abstract

We propose a Z-transform framework for the analysis and synthesis of finite range lattice momentum operators in quantum field theory. In this formulation, translation-invariant lattice operators are represented as functions of the complex variable $z$ in the unit circle, allowing their spectral properties to be analyzed using tools from digital signal processing and rational approximation theory. Within this framework, the fermion doubling problem is reinterpreted as the appearance of unwanted zeros of the discrete momentum operator on the unit circle --- an aliasing phenomenon in the sense of the Nyquist sampling theorem --- and the conditions for ghost suppression are expressed as precise constraints on the zero structure of the operator's transfer function. It is proven that no rational function can satisfy all required conditions simultaneously, motivating the finite impulse response approach developed here. This reframing naturally suggests a class of finite-range momentum operators, constructed by solving a least-squares approximation problem in the frequency domain. The resulting finite impulse response (FIR) operator approximates the continuum derivative across the full Brillouin zone, with ghost suppression achieved through the accuracy of the spectral approximation rather than through the addition of a symmetry-breaking Wilson term or the infinite-range nonlocal SLAC derivative. Numerical investigation confirms that near $θ= π$ only plane waves propagate coherently, and these exhibit group velocities far exceeding the speed of light, further distinguishing them from physical low-energy excitations. No ghost wave packet solutions exist near $θ= π$.

Astrophysical Graviton Squeezing Can Be Hidden in the Far-Field

Summary. This theoretical work analyzes the observability of quantum squeezing in astrophysical gravitational waves at a distant detector. It finds that due to kinematic conflicts in same-cone measurements, the expected quantum signatures are severely suppressed, causing the signal to appear classically thermal. This demonstrates a crucial limitation: global quantum properties do not automatically translate into local, measurable quantum effects.

Why it may be interesting. The core mechanism of suppression—where geometric constraints (like single-line-of-sight detection) destroy quantum correlations—is a powerful concept applicable to any field where global quantum resources are mapped onto local measurements, such as quantum sensing or quantum communication.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the global quantum squeezing of gravitons, generated by astrophysical sources, can manifest as measurable local quantum coherence at a far-field detector.

Main result. A severe kinematic conflict in same-cone measurements causes local quantum coherence to be highly suppressed, meaning global squeezing does not guarantee local observability; the signal appears effectively classical/thermal.

Method. The analysis employs spatial quantum optics methods, calculating two-time correlation functions ($g^{(2)}$) and analyzing the far-field expansion of the graviton field using pole extraction techniques.

Model / system. The system involves gravitons (gravitational waves) propagating from localized astrophysical sources to a far-field detector. The state is modeled as a multi-mode squeezed state, analyzed via an Effective Field Theory (EFT) Hamiltonian.

Key observables. Second-order correlation function ($g^{(2)}$), local quantum coherence, and the anomalous moment ($M_D$) relative to the normal moment ($N_D$).

Important parameters / regimes. The suppression factor $\eta \sim \exp(-dE/k^*)$, the far-field distance $r$, and the angular separation $\delta heta$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the far-field limit ($r \gg L_D$) and relies on the detector sampling only a single far-field line of sight (same-cone measurement).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the far-field projection onto the outgoing branch ($I_+$) and compare overlap integrals for normal and anomalous moments, highlighting the kinematic conflict.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the far-field expansion of the field (pole extraction), then calculating the relevant correlation functions ($g^{(2)}$) using overlap integrals, and finally demonstrating the exponential suppression factor arising from the same-cone measurement geometry.

Abstract

While localized astrophysical sources can generate macroscopic graviton squeezing, their observable quantum signatures at far-field detectors remain unresolved. In this work, we investigate the propagation dynamics of the squeezed states using spatial quantum optics methods to evaluate correlation functions accessible to a local observer. Crucially, we reveal a severe kinematic conflict in same-cone measurements, which highly suppresses local quantum coherence. Consequently, these macroscopically squeezed states appear classically thermal to a single detector. Our results demonstrate that global squeezing does not guarantee local observability, and the measurable quantum signatures may be significantly weaker than what would be expected from the overall squeezing parameter of the state.

Summary. This theoretical work develops a 'connectivity-order law' to solve the problem of identifying the physical mechanism responsible for a measured many-body geometric phase. By employing advanced algebraic and combinatorial methods, the authors prove that the phase value constrains the underlying interactions to those that form a connected cover. This provides a powerful diagnostic tool for characterizing quantum system dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous mathematical tools to distinguish the physical origin of quantum phase accumulation, which is crucial for designing robust quantum gates and characterizing quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining the generating mechanism (direct vs. connected sequence of lower-body interactions) of a measured many-body geometric phase ($\Phi_S$) from its value alone.

Main result. The paper establishes a 'connectivity-order law' stating that a nonzero connected response must involve an active set that connects and covers the target support, leading to the bound $ u_S \geq au_E(S)$.

Method. The authors use advanced mathematical tools including Möbius inversion on subset lattices, joint-support projectors, and developing a 'minimal-cover order law' to analyze the structure of Abelian holonomies.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on multiqubit systems exhibiting Abelian holonomies, derived from Hamiltonians structured with coupling terms, applicable to settings like two-spin NMR or semiconductor spin-qubits.

Key observables. The multiqubit geometric phase ($\Phi_S$), locally irreducible phase ($\Phi_S$), and confidence bands for detecting mixed responses.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis is restricted to analytic, gapped, nondegenerate Abelian holonomies; the separation condition $|\Phi_S| > 2\Delta$ is key for identifying exact minima.

Assumptions / limitations. The results are derived under the assumption of analytic, gapped, and nondegenerate Abelian holonomies, and the method cannot resolve uncalibrated virtual paths.

Figures summary. Figure 1 summarizes the physical logic, showing irreducible phase extraction, additivity for factorized blocks, and joint logical–coupling resolution enforcing connected covers.

Paper structure. The paper builds from identifying the mechanism-identifiability problem, developing the connectivity-order law via joint support projectors, and concluding with necessary and sufficient conditions for minimal-cover response identification.

Abstract

The value of a multiqubit geometric phase at a single operating point does not reveal whether it was generated directly or through a connected sequence of lower-body interactions. For analytic, gapped, nondegenerate Abelian holonomies, we jointly resolve logical support and independently calibrated coupling support. Every nonzero connected response must involve an active set that connects and covers the target and must contain one factor of each active coupling, yielding the sharp onset bound $ν_S \geq τ_E(S)$. The same selection law applies locally to projected Berry curvature. Conversely, an unrestricted finite-dimensional construction realizes all responses allowed by the connected-cover condition simultaneously, making the condition necessary and sufficient within this theorem class. Operationally, simultaneous confidence bands certify detected mixed responses while controlling false positives; a factor-of-two separation condition additionally recovers exact library-relative response minima. In a complete dictionary, nontriviality of every minimal-cover response is generically necessary and sufficient for these minima to coincide with the minimal calibrated mechanisms. Three-qubit Wilson calculations distinguish direct and pair-mediated routes with the same endpoint phase, and a synthetic Ramsey audit locates the finite-resolution boundary.

Universal quantum theory from dynamical consistency

Authors: Chiara Marletto, Vlatko Vedral
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17688v1

Summary. This work uses dynamical consistency to argue that quantum theory is universal by showing that any attempt to model quantum systems coupled to classical fields violates fundamental principles like exact conservation laws or canonical commutation relations. The authors demonstrate that for consistency, all coupled components must obey quantum rules, thereby ruling out viable hybrid quantum-classical models.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a fundamental, principle-based argument for the necessity of quantum mechanics, which is relevant for understanding the foundations of open quantum systems and quantum gravity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper argues for the universality of quantum theory by testing the consistency of hybrid quantum-classical models.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that maintaining exact conservation laws and canonical commutation relations forces any coupled system to be fully quantum, ruling out consistent hybrid classical-quantum descriptions.

Method. The analysis uses dynamical consistency arguments, examining the preservation of commutation relations and exact conservation laws under joint unitary time evolution.

Model / system. The models include coupled quantum harmonic oscillators, the linear coupling of gravity to quantized EM fields, and a general framework for bipartite quantum-classical systems.

Key observables. Commutation relations (e.g., $[x(t), p(t)]$), exact conservation laws ($[O, U] = 0$), and the preservation of the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis focuses on the structure of the coupling Hamiltonian and the regimes where semiclassical approximations fail.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumptions are that the combined dynamics must be Hamiltonian and unitary, and that the canonical algebra must be preserved under joint unitary dynamics.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by analyzing simple quantum field couplings, extending the argument to gravity/EM coupling, and concluding with a detailed comparison to quantum measurement theory and the failure of classical approximations.

Abstract

We argue for the universality of quantum theory using a dynamical consistency argument, within a specific Hamiltonian setting. We analyse two different types of coupling between simple quantum harmonic oscillators. Each illustrates an aspect of the free and interacting quantum fields and shows the inadequacy of semiclassical models. In particular, we establish that requiring the canonical algebra to be preserved under joint unitary dynamics rules out specific hybrid classical-quantum models. We apply our reasoning to the gravitational field in the linear regime, coupled to the quantised electromagnetic field and, separately, to quantised matter. We conclude with a comparison to DeWitt's analysis of quantum measurement, in which the apparatus, if classical, must be at least stochastic to preserve the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle. We also note that stochastic models are inconsistent with the strict version of conservation principles, even if they comply with a probabilistic (on average) conservation.

A Complete Classification of Complex Hadamard Matrices of Order Six

Summary. This paper provides a complete mathematical classification of complex Hadamard matrices of order six, which are crucial for quantum measurements and interferometry. The authors advance existing methods by proving that all such matrices can be systematically reconstructed from a small initial corner block. This resolves a long-standing open problem in the field by providing a rigorous, exhaustive algebraic framework.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous classification of these matrices provides a deep mathematical toolset for designing optimal quantum measurement schemes and understanding the limits of quantum state tomography in multi-mode systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to provide a complete and exact finite-incidence classification of complex Hadamard matrices of order six ($d=6$).

Main result. The authors successfully classify all such matrices up to standard equivalence, proving that every class can be reconstructed from a suitable initial $3 imes 3$ corner via a finite, branch-complete procedure.

Method. The classification relies on refining Szöllősi's dilation method by proving that every matrix possesses a 'finite-corner witness' and solving the resulting constraints using algebraic elimination and solving coupled quadratic/cubic equations.

Model / system. The core model is the complex Hadamard matrix $H \in T^{6 imes 6}$, which encodes perfectly balanced unitary transformations and is fundamental to understanding mutually unbiased quantum measurements and multiphoton interferometry.

Key observables. The classification is determined by analyzing the structure of the matrix corners, the rank of the Gram matrix, and the existence of finite-corner witnesses.

Important parameters / regimes. The order of the matrix ($d=6$), the structure of the corner blocks, and the parameters defining the continuous families (e.g., Karlsson's family).

Assumptions / limitations. The classification relies on the assumption that the finite-corner witness approach covers all cases, and that the algebraic methods used for reconstruction are exhaustive.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the proof architecture, showing the flow from an arbitrary class to the conclusion that it must have a finite-corner witness, leading to the complete classification $ ext{H}6 = A^6$.}

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by defining the matrices and known families, introducing the 'branch-complete finite-dilation extension' of Szöllősi's algorithm, and finally studying the geometry of the reconstruction process using fixed-Gram constraints.

Abstract

Complex Hadamard matrices encode perfectly balanced unitary transformations. They underlie mutually unbiased quantum measurements and multiphoton interferometry. Their classification is complete through order five, but order six -- the first dimension in which several continuous families coexist with an isolated solution -- has remained open for decades. Here, we give a complete and exact finite-incidence classification of order-six complex Hadamard matrices up to standard equivalence. We first prove that every such matrix can be constructed from an initial dephased $3 \times 3$ corner by a finite, branch-complete procedure. This supplies the global step missing from Szöllősi's dilation method and proves his conjecture: up to standard equivalence, every class outside Karlsson's three-parameter family and Tao's isolated matrix is recovered algebraically from a suitable corner. We then describe the geometry of the reconstruction from four initial phases and show that, except for Tao's isolated matrix and a single explicit Karlsson matrix, every class admits a representative obtained by solving one quadratic and one cubic equation in both the horizontal and vertical directions. Our work resolves the classification problem and provides a rigorous framework for further investigating order-six Hadamards, with applications to balanced six-mode interferometers and the study of mutually unbiased bases.

A solid-state theory for dense cylindrical packings of balls

Summary. This paper provides a comprehensive analytical theory for determining the maximum packing fraction of hard spheres inside a cylinder. It establishes a rigorous lower bound using lattice masking and improves this bound through systematic optimization and the addition of 'sheaths.' The work is significant for providing a general theoretical framework applicable across all cylinder sizes.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical geometry, the rigorous development of analytical bounds and optimization procedures for confined systems mirrors techniques used in analyzing quantum confinement effects or calculating ground state energies in geometrically constrained quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops an analytical theory to calculate the dense packing fraction of hard spheres (balls) confined within a cylinder, covering all aspect ratios.

Main result. The authors derive an exact analytical lower bound for the packing fraction using a lattice-masking approach, and further improve this bound using an Optimized Cylinder Orientation (OCO) procedure and a 'sheathing' construction.

Method. The methods involve deriving the packing fraction using Fourier transforms of the cylindrical indicator function, optimizing the cylinder orientation via gradient conditions, and geometrically constructing successive annular sheaths.

Model / system. The system is monodisperse hard spheres packed inside a cylinder of specified diameter and height. The theory models the packing by assuming the spheres adhere to a perfect crystal lattice (FCC or HCP).

Key observables. Packing fraction ($\phi$), which exhibits oscillatory (step-like) behavior as a function of cylinder dimensions.

Important parameters / regimes. Cylinder diameter ($2R$), cylinder height ($H$), and sphere diameter ($\sigma$). The analysis covers regimes from narrow to wide cylinders.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the packing can be modeled by masking a finite volume from a perfect, regular crystal lattice. Later derivations assume $H \gg R_0$ for sheath calculations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the masking process, compare predicted packing fractions (lower bound vs. numerical data) across varying dimensions, and detail the geometric construction of added annular sheaths.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the basic lattice-based lower bound via Fourier analysis to introducing optimization techniques (OCO) to tighten this bound, and finally detailing a recursive 'sheathing' construction to further improve the predicted density.

Abstract

We develop an analytical theory for the dense packing of hard spheres in cylinders. Physically, our theory consists of a finite cylindrical masking of a close-packed three-dimensional solid and covers the entire range of cylinder aspect ratios, thus going beyond efforts that are focused on very tall cylinders in a narrow range of widths. We explicitly derive an exact equation for resulting packing fractions, valid for any regular lattice, and it provides a basis to understand the oscillations and scaling of volume fractions that have appeared in previous works. Our analytical relation serves as a rigorous lower bound and to tighten it we derive, and implement, an efficient mathematical procedure to optimize the orientation of the cylinder. Furthermore, we suggest simple techniques to improve on the predicted packings. Overall, we provide a general theoretical foundation for the packing of balls in cylinders, valid for all container sizes.

Gisin's Argument and the Limits of Causal Explanations in Relativistic Spacetime

Summary. This paper extends Gisin's argument by developing frame-indexed causal models to handle the probabilistic nature of quantum correlations. It proves that no model satisfying key principles like Independent Settings and No-Retrocausality can be Lorentz covariant while reproducing Bell test statistics. The work thus places strong theoretical constraints on any relativistic causal interpretation of quantum theory.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly about AMO systems, the rigorous development of causal inference frameworks and their incompatibility with quantum correlations provides deep theoretical constraints on any physical theory attempting to explain quantum mechanics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the conflict between quantum nonlocality (implied by Bell correlations) and the principles of special relativity when constructing causal explanations for quantum mechanics.

Main result. The authors establish two no-go theorems showing that no empirically adequate frame-indexed causal model can simultaneously satisfy Independent Settings, No-Retrocausality, and Causal Lorentz Invariance.

Method. The methodology involves extending Gisin's original deterministic argument to the probabilistic domain using a novel framework of frame-indexed causal models.

Model / system. The system under study is the bipartite Bell experiment, analyzed using hidden variable models parameterized by causal structures associated with different inertial reference frames.

Key observables. Bell correlations (e.g., CHSH inequality violation), and the agreement of operational variables across different reference frames (runwise agreement).

Important parameters / regimes. The assumptions include Independent Settings, No-Retrocausality, and the degree of Lorentz invariance assumed for the causal structure.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis critically examines the assumptions required for Gisin's argument, particularly the assumption of determinism, and introduces the Independent Explanation principle.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates a simple Markov chain used to critique probabilistic notions of no-retrocausality. Figure 7 illustrates the maximally allowed causal structure compatible with Independent Settings and No-Retrocausality in two frames.

Paper structure. The paper first critiques the limitations of generalizing Gisin's deterministic argument to the probabilistic case. It then introduces frame-indexed causal models to formalize the constraints, leading to the derivation of the two main no-go results based on invariance principles.

Abstract

Gisin has provided an argument for the conclusion that no covariant nonlocal model can reproduce the operational statistics observed in Bell experiments. Gisin's argument applies only to deterministic models and we argue that proposed generalizations of the argument beyond determinism based solely on statistical notions are unconvincing, as they lack the resources to formulate a satisfactory notion of no-retrocausality. We introduce the framework of frame-indexed causal models in order to extend Gisin's argument to the genuinely probabilistic case. Frame-indexed causal models associate potentially different causal models with different reference frames while requiring corresponding operational variables to agree runwise. Within this framework, we establish two no-go results based on Gisin's argument. First, we show that no empirically adequate frame-indexed causal model can jointly satisfy Independent Settings, No-Retrocausality, and Causal Lorentz Invariance. Second, it is established that this result continues to hold when Causal Lorentz Invariance is weakened to Lorentz Invariance of Causal Connections, which requires only the skeleton of the causal structure to remain invariant, thereby allowing the direction of causal influence to depend on the choice of reference frame. These results are discussed in the context of relativistic interpretations of quantum theory and nonclassical causal inference.

Abstract

Textbook quantum superposition refers to the feature that certain linear combinations of Hilbert space rays, each representing a valid quantum state, are themselves valid states. This notion is not operational, and it relies on the underlying Hilbert space formalism. Recent proposals for experimental tests of indefinite causal order, as well as tests probing the non-classicality of gravity, pivot on superposition, thereby calling for a theory-independent, operational formalisation of the concept. Here, we define superposition within the framework of Generalised Probabilistic Theories, based on observed statistics in prepare-and-measure experiments. Using this, we formulate three superposition principles to investigate which structural features of quantum theory carry over to other theories. We study conditions under which these principles carry over from subsystems to their compositions; to this end, we show that the quantum tensor product emerges as the largest composition rule for quantum systems respecting all three principles. Furthermore, we show how non-classical features such as entanglement and preparational uncertainty can be viewed as special forms of superposition.

Summary. This paper rigorously proves the weak typicality of entanglement entropy in Gaussian Boson Sampling models. It shows that the measured entropy is highly concentrated around its expected value, establishing a quantitative concentration bound. This result is significant for understanding the typical scaling laws governing entanglement in large-scale quantum optical devices.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous mathematical tools for analyzing entanglement measures in complex quantum optical circuits, which is crucial for understanding the resource scaling and fundamental limits of photonic quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the weak typicality of the von Neumann entanglement entropy generated by a Haar distributed passive interferometer acting on squeezed input modes.

Main result. It proves that the ratio of the entanglement entropy to its expectation value converges in probability to 1, establishing proportional weak typicality for this specific Gaussian Boson Sampling setup.

Method. The proof involves representing the entropy as a singular value statistic of a principal block of the interferometer's unitary matrix and applying concentration inequalities on the unitary group.

Model / system. The system is Gaussian Boson Sampling (GBS) using an ideal, lossless interferometer acting on $n$ equally squeezed input modes. The evolution is governed by a Haar distributed unitary $U$.

Key observables. Von Neumann entanglement entropy ($S_{1,n}$), the ratio $S_{1,n} / \mathbb{E}S_{1,n}$, and the variance $ ext{Var}(S_{1,n})$.

Important parameters / regimes. Fixed nonzero squeezing strength ($s$), and a proportional bipartition ratio ($k_n/n o r \in (0, 1)$).

Assumptions / limitations. The result assumes fixed equal squeezing and a proportional bipartition; it does not cover unequal squeezing or vanishing squeezing.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mathematical framework by relating the entropy to singular values, proves the weak typicality bound using concentration on the unitary group, and concludes by deriving the implications for convergence and variance.

Abstract

We study the von Neumann entanglement entropy generated by a Haar distributed passive interferometer acting on $n$ equally squeezed input modes with fixed nonzero squeezing strength $s$. Previous work established proportional weak typicality for integer R'enyi orders $α\geq 2$ and stated a sublinear von Neumann result, while the proportional von Neumann case remained open. For a subsystem of $k_n$ modes satisfying $k_n/n\to r\in(0,1)$, we prove that, for every $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$, $\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{S_{1,n}}{\mathbb{E}S_{1,n}}-1\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq2\exp\left[-\frac{c_{s,r}\varepsilon^2n^2}{\log^2(en)}\right].$ The proof represents the entropy as a singular value statistic of a principal block of $UU^{\mathsf T}$, where $U$ denotes the unitary interferometer. It regularizes the logarithmic singularity at the endpoint corresponding to a pure Gaussian mode and applies concentration on the unitary group. The result establishes proportional von Neumann weak typicality and further implies almost sure convergence of $S_{1,n}/\mathbb{E}S_{1,n}$ to $1$, a typical volume law, and the variance bound $\mathrm{Var}(S_{1,n})=O_s(\log^2 n)$. An accompanying Lean 4 development verifies the proof chain.
