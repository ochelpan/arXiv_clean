Influence of laser chirp and interferometer delay and imbalance on the performance of a time-bin BB84 quantum key distribution system

Summary. This paper analyzes the limitations imposed by optical imperfections—specifically laser chirp, interferometer delay, and imbalance—on time-bin BB84 QKD systems. By simulating and measuring visibility degradation, the authors quantify how these factors reduce the achievable Secret Key Rate. The work is important for developing robust quantum communication hardware by providing concrete metrics for required component stabilization.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it bridges fundamental quantum optics characterization (chirp measurement) directly into the practical performance limits of a quantum communication protocol (QKD), which is crucial for building real-world quantum networks.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how imperfections like interferometer delay, imbalance, and laser chirp degrade the performance of a time-bin BB84 quantum key distribution (QKD) system.

Main result. System visibility is highly sensitive to these parameters; specifically, laser chirp introduces significant degradation in the Secret Key Rate that must be accounted for beyond simple visibility loss calculations.

Method. The authors combine simulations of QKD performance with experimental measurements using advanced techniques like PROUD (ultrafast optical differentiation) to characterize the chirp.

Model / system. The physical system is a time-bin BB84 QKD protocol implemented using an imbalanced interferometer setup. The analysis models pulse propagation and interference visibility under varying delay, imbalance, and frequency chirp.

Key observables. Secret Key Rate (SKR), Interference Visibility (V), Intensity Imbalance (U), and the Chirp parameter ($\Delta\omega$ or $eta$).

Important parameters / regimes. System visibility ($V$), relative time delay ($ au$), intensity imbalance, and chirp coefficient ($eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that linear chirp is the dominant source of phase variation; discrepancies between simulated unchirped results and experimental data are used to quantify the chirp effect.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate SKR vs. visibility (Fig. 1), setup schematics for visibility measurement (Fig. 2) and chirp retrieval (Fig. 4), and contour plots showing visibility dependence on imbalance/delay (Fig. 3, Fig. 6).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the QKD performance metric (SKR vs. V), then measuring how delay and imbalance affect V, and finally introducing chirp analysis using PROUD to quantify an additional, significant degradation factor.

Abstract

We investigate the effect of interferometer delay and imbalance on the performance of a BB84 time-bin quantum key distribution system. We simulate the impact of interference visibility on system performance and measure the visibility of a pair of interferometers as a function of their relative time delay and intensity imbalance. In addition, our analysis highlights the effect of laser chirp on system performance.

Summary. This paper rigorously determines the conditions under which exotic dimer states are guaranteed to be the ground state in generalized spin chains and ladders with long-range and anisotropic interactions. By combining theoretical semi-positivity arguments with numerical validation, it maps out precise phase boundaries that extend the known understanding of quantum magnetism.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous mathematical criteria for identifying specific quantum phases (dimerized) in complex, interacting many-body systems, which is crucial for understanding magnetic materials where long-range correlations are important.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining precise, explicit conditions on coupling constants for long-range interacting spin chains and ladders to guarantee that the exact dimer state is the ground state.

Main result. The authors derive sufficient conditions (e.g., involving non-negativity of auxiliary coefficients $\alpha_n$ and $eta_n$) under which the dimer state is guaranteed to be the ground state, often extending beyond previously known necessary conditions.

Method. The methodology combines theoretical derivation using semi-positivity arguments on the Hamiltonian ($ar{H} \ge 0$) with validation via exact diagonalization (ED) for finite systems and analysis of anisotropic quantum top models.

Model / system. The study focuses on spin-1/2 interacting quantum spin chains and ladders, described by generalized Hamiltonians including long-range interactions ($J_n$ and $K_n$) and anisotropy terms ($\gamma_\mu^n$).

Key observables. Ground state energy (EGS), Dimer entanglement entropy ($\Delta S_{ ext{dimer}}$), and the non-negativity of derived coefficients ($\alpha_n, eta_n$).

Important parameters / regimes. Coupling constants $\{J_n\}, \{K_n\}$, anisotropy parameters $\{\gamma_\mu^n\}$, and system size $L$ (often assumed even).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of a ground state characterized by semi-positivity, and some derivations rely on finite-size approximations or specific structural assumptions about coupling ranges.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams showing boundaries between Dimer, Ferromagnetic (FM), and Partially Magnetized states for both isotropic and anisotropic chains/ladders, comparing theoretical predictions with ED results.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing general Hamiltonian forms to deriving necessary conditions via semi-positivity arguments ($\alpha_n \ge 0, eta_n \ge 0$). It then tackles the complexity of anisotropy by relating it to quantum top spectra and concludes by comparing theoretical sufficient conditions against numerical ED results on phase diagrams.

Abstract

Interacting spin chains and ladders are known to support a plethora of quantum phases with complex ground-state phase diagrams. In this work, we study a large family of such models and determine precise, explicit conditions under which an exact dimer state is guaranteed to be the ground state. These general conditions are validated for various generalizations of the Majumdar-Ghosh model using exact diagonalization. Our results provide exact reference points in the phase diagrams of a wide class of spin chains and ladders, including those with anisotropic and arbitrary-range interactions.

Exploiting Symmetry in Quantum Reservoir Computing

Authors: Markus Baumann, Michael Poppel, Thomas Gabor, Maximilian Zorn, Claudia Linnhoff-Popien, Jonas Stein
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01187v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: measurement-induced transitions 3/5 · quantum measurements 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5 · non-equilibrium dynamics 1/5

Summary. This paper establishes that exploiting symmetry in Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC) requires enforcing consistency across all computational interfaces: encoding, dynamics, measurement, and readout. By developing 'observable-orbit completion,' the authors demonstrate that aligning these four components yields significant performance boosts over models where symmetry is broken at any single point.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous architectural guide for designing quantum machine learning models, showing that the physical implementation details—especially measurement design—are as critical as the underlying Hamiltonian dynamics for extracting useful information.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is ensuring that symmetry present in time-series data (like cyclic patterns) is preserved and visible throughout the entire Quantum Reservoir Computing pipeline, as mere symmetry in one component is insufficient.

Main result. Achieving strong predictive gains requires aligning symmetry constraints across all four interfaces: input encoding, reservoir dynamics, measurement observables, and classical readout. This alignment mechanism proves crucial for successful data-driven quantum modeling.

Method. The authors employ a systematic 'four-interface audit' comparing fully aligned models against baseline configurations to quantify the necessity of symmetry preservation at every stage, particularly focusing on observable-orbit completion in measurements.

Model / system. The model is Quantum Reservoir Computing (QRC) applied to cyclic forecasting tasks on periodic rings. The dynamics involve mapping inputs through fixed quantum evolution and measuring expectation values before training a classical readout layer.

Key observables. Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE), percentage gain in forecasting accuracy, and the measured feature map $\Phi_M(x)$ restricted to the span of measured observables.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of sites on the ring ($K$), fixed feature budget (e.g., 61 features/site), and the specific symmetry group action ($\mathbb{Z}_K$).

Assumptions / limitations. A critical limitation is the 'measured-span obstruction': if a symmetry-carrying channel is not explicitly measured, it cannot be recovered by the classical optimization process.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate performance gains comparing baseline vs. fully aligned models across various tasks (e.g., Advection, L96), and tables detail the systematic audit results for the E/H/O/R switches.

Paper structure. The paper systematically develops the necessity of symmetry alignment by first defining the QRC framework, then introducing observable-orbit completion to enforce measurement symmetry, followed by a comprehensive factorial audit across all four interfaces (E, H, O, R), and finally validating these findings on diverse tasks including real weather data.

Abstract

Symmetry is a powerful inductive bias, but in quantum reservoir computing (QRC) it cannot be imposed only by making the reservoir symmetric. QRC maps inputs through fixed quantum dynamics into nonlinear expectation-value features and trains only a classical readout, so the relevant symmetry must be visible in the measured feature map. We study cyclic forecasting tasks, such as sensors around a turbine or weather stations along a latitude circle, where the same local pattern should be forecast by the same rule wherever it appears on the ring. Thus, rotating the input by one site should rotate, not change, the predicted field. We show that a symmetric Hamiltonian is not enough: even large Pauli measurement sets can fail if their channels do not match the data symmetry, since optimization cannot recover channels that were never measured. We address this through observable-orbit completion, which measures symmetry-related observable channels and aligns encoding, dynamics, measurement, and readout. The strongest gains arise from aligning all four interfaces together, with matched spin-ring, real-weather, and IBM hardware checks supporting the same measured-span mechanism.

Summary. This work develops a powerful Monte Carlo technique to calculate free energy differences between distinct global sectors of lattice models. Applied to the 3D Ising model, it yields precise values for symmetry-twisted Casimir effects and spectral gaps by analyzing finite-size corrections in various geometric limits.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical statistical mechanics, the methodology of using complex sampling techniques (like flat histograms) to extract subtle spectral information (gaps, boundary effects) is highly relevant to modern quantum many-body simulations where direct measurement of ground state properties is difficult.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to estimate partition function ratios between distinct global sectors of a lattice theory, specifically for the critical 3D Ising model.

Main result. The method successfully yields the symmetry-twisted thermodynamic Casimir difference $\Delta Z_2 = 0.327(2)$ and estimates the $Z_2$-odd energy gap $\kappa_{\sigma} = 1.230(3)$.

Method. It employs a Monte Carlo strategy using an enlarged configuration space to sample an interpolating family of lattice systems, allowing for direct reconstruction of free energy differences via flat histogram methods.

Model / system. The system studied is the three-dimensional Ising model simulated on a slab geometry ($R^2 imes S^1_{L_z}$) at its bulk critical point. The analysis compares the untwisted (periodic) sector with the $\mathbb{Z}_2$-twisted (antiperiodic) sector.

Key observables. Sector free energy difference ($\Delta F_{\mathbb{Z}_2}$), symmetry-twisted thermodynamic Casimir difference ($\Delta Z_2$), and spectral gap estimates ($\kappa_\sigma$).

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis involves extrapolations in the large-$q$ limit ($q=L/L_z o \infty$) and the small-$q$ limit ($q \ll 1$), as well as finite-size scaling parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes the existence of a suitable interpolating family for sampling and relies on extrapolations to infinite volume and specific limits (e.g., $q o\infty$).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate large-$q$ evaluations of slab data versus $L_z$, showing convergence towards $\Delta Z_2$, and stability checks for both the Casimir difference and the gap exponent $\kappa_\sigma$.

Paper structure. The paper details the general Monte Carlo framework using an enlarged configuration space, applies it to the 3D Ising model on a slab geometry, performs finite-size scaling analysis by extrapolating results in both large-$q$ and small-$q$ regimes, and concludes with universal values for sector differences.

Abstract

We introduce a Monte Carlo strategy for directly estimating partition function ratios between distinct global sectors of a lattice theory. It enlarges the configuration space to sample an interpolating family whose endpoints are the desired sectors, and uses flat histogram methods to reconstruct the corresponding free energy difference. Although the construction is more general, we focus here on the three-dimensional Ising model on the slab $\mathbb{R}^{2}\times S^{1}_{L_{z}}$ at the bulk critical point, comparing the untwisted periodic sector with the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-twisted antiperiodic sector. A large-volume and aspect ratio extrapolation gives the symmetry-twisted thermodynamic Casimir difference $Δ_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}=0.327(2)$ directly, without lattice derivatives or bulk subtractions. This provides an independent twisted sector probe of tensions observed in periodic sector thermodynamic Casimir observables. More generally, the method gives direct but selective numerical access to CFT compactification data, including estimates of the effective thermal screening scale and the $\mathbb{Z}_{2}$-odd sector energy gap on $T^{2}$.

Velocity of a Quantum Particle in a Classically Forbidden Region

Summary. This theoretical work resolves a conflict regarding the measured speed of a quantum particle tunneling through a barrier, where experimental estimates disagreed with Bohmian predictions. The authors show that naive time estimates fail because they ignore entanglement between degrees of freedom. By correctly applying and conditioning the dwell-time formula, they demonstrate perfect agreement with Bohmian mechanics.

Why it may be interesting. It provides deep insight into the interpretation of quantum measurements in non-trivial regimes (like tunneling) and rigorously tests the equivalence between different formalisms (QM vs. Bohmian mechanics) under specific physical constraints.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper resolves discrepancies between particle speed estimates in classically forbidden regions (tunneling) derived from experiments/heuristics and predictions made by Bohmian mechanics.

Main result. A correct application of the dwell time formula, conditioned on penetration into the barrier, yields results that exactly agree with Bohmian mechanical predictions for the average velocity.

Method. The analysis compares three methods—tunneling time estimate, Büttiker dwell time formula, and Bohmian trajectory calculation—by analyzing time-dependent wave packets in 1D and 2D coupled waveguide models.

Model / system. The system is a non-relativistic quantum particle modeled by the Schrödinger equation in a potential landscape featuring a potential step realized via coupled waveguides (a double-well structure). The analysis covers both propagating and evanescent regimes.

Key observables. Particle populations ($|\Psi|^2$), Bohmian velocities, average speeds ($ar{v}_B$), and conditional dwell times ($ au_{pen}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Evanescent decay constants ($\delta_\kappa, ar{\kappa}$), energy splitting ($J_0$), spectral width ($\sigma_k$), and characteristic equilibration length scales ($X_{eq}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The initial discrepancy arose from assuming the tunneling time is set only by transverse coupling (ignoring longitudinal entanglement) or applying the standard Büttiker formula without conditioning on barrier penetration.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate potential landscapes, relative population dynamics in both oscillatory (propagative) and monotonic (evanescent) regimes, and numerical simulations comparing $|\Psi|^2$ density with Bohmian trajectories.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first identifying the experimental challenge, then analyzing the theoretical estimates (tunneling time/dwell time), demonstrating where these estimates fail due to entanglement effects in the evanescent regime, and finally resolving the conflict by applying a corrected, conditional dwell-time argument that matches Bohmian predictions.

Abstract

Recently, Sharoglazova et al. [Nature 643, 67 (2025)] proposed a procedure for determining the speed of a quantum particle in the classically forbidden region of a potential step, and implemented it in a beautiful experiment. The inferred speeds disagree significantly with the Bohmian velocities, which the authors presented as an experimental challenge to Bohmian mechanics. This is puzzling because the speeds are inferred from particle populations in coupled waveguides for which Bohmian mechanics and standard quantum mechanics make identical predictions. We resolve the puzzle by a detailed theoretical analysis of the experimental setup. We show that the speed inference rests on an assumption that fails in the relevant (evanescent) regime according to both Bohmian mechanics and standard quantum mechanics -- namely that the inter-waveguide tunneling time is set by the transverse coupling and is not affected by entanglement with the longitudinal degree of freedom. We also consider a second speed estimate suggested by Sharoglazova et al., which is based on the Büttiker dwell time formula for particles in the forbidden region. We show that the authors applied the formula incorrectly, and that a correct application yields exact agreement with the predictions of Bohmian mechanics. Our analysis includes explicit calculations of Bohmian trajectories, dwell times, and longitudinal speeds in the two-dimensional waveguide model of the experiment.

Summary. This work uses Quantum Monte Carlo simulations on a fractionalized model to study the transition between an antiferromagnetic insulator and a d-wave superconductor. The key finding is evidence for a second-order deconfined quantum phase transition (DQCP) at which both magnetic and superconducting orders vanish continuously. This provides theoretical support for understanding competing orders in strongly correlated electron systems.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter, the use of lattice gauge theory coupled to fermionic excitations and the explicit study of quantum critical points provide rich theoretical tools applicable to strongly interacting many-body systems relevant in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the quantum phase transition between an antiferromagnetic (Néel) insulator and a nodal d-wave superconductor in a lattice model.

Main result. Evidence is found for a second-order deconfined quantum phase transition (DQCP) where both Néel order and d-wave superconductivity orders vanish continuously.

Method. Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) simulations are employed on a fractionalized representation of the electron using spinon and chargon fields to avoid the sign problem.

Model / system. The system is modeled on a square lattice at half-filling, described by an SU(2) lattice gauge theory coupled to fermionic spinons and bosonic chargons. This setup mimics aspects of high-temperature superconductivity in cuprates.

Key observables. Electron spectral function $A(\mathbf{k}, \omega)$, dynamical spin structure factor $\chi_S(\mathbf{q}, \omega)$, and correlation ratios for dSC, AFM, and VBS orders.

Important parameters / regimes. The transition is tuned by parameters like $\kappa$ and $\kappa_ au$, which control the gauge field dynamics and coupling strengths.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is restricted to the half-filled, particle-hole symmetric case to ensure the QMC simulation remains sign-problem free.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams mapping out dSC, AFM, and deconfined regimes, alongside spectral functions showing Dirac dispersion in the superconducting phase that gaps into a gapped spectrum in the antiferromagnetic phase near the transition point.

Paper structure. The paper develops the fractionalized Hamiltonian using spinon/chargon fields, details the QMC simulation techniques (including gauge field updates), and then analyzes key observables like spectral functions and structure factors across different parameter regimes to pinpoint the DQCP.

Abstract

We present a quantum Monte Carlo study of the transition between the insulating Néel state and the nodal $d$-wave superconductor on the square lattice at half-filling. We access a regime of frustrated magnetic order without a sign problem using a parton representation of the electron in terms of fermionic spinons and bosonic chargons. Both partons move in a background $π$-flux (so the electron experiences no net flux) and are coupled to a quantum fluctuating SU(2) lattice gauge field. In contrast to earlier studies directly on the electronic degrees of freedom, we find evidence for a second-order deconfined quantum phase transition at which both the Néel and $d$-wave superconductivity orders vanish continuously. We compute correlators of the spinon-chargon composite with the same quantum numbers as the electron: we find a gapless Dirac dispersion inside the $d$-wave superconductor, turning into a gapped dispersion in the antiferromagnet.

Summary. This paper investigates qubit leakage as a major error source in transmons by quantifying 'leakage mobility.' Using advanced perturbation theory and non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, the authors derive conditions to suppress unwanted excitation hopping. They conclude by proposing and analyzing two passive hardware schemes that utilize this engineered mobility for effective leakage removal.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides detailed, quantitative theoretical tools for mitigating decoherence in superconducting qubits by engineering the system's Hamiltonian to actively suppress leakage pathways using tunable couplers.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Qubit leakage is a significant error source in quantum computation, necessitating methods to characterize its dynamics and implement passive removal units.

Main result. The authors propose two passive leakage removal units—one based on a tunable coupler/pumped transmon and another on junction readout—whose performance can be analyzed using leakage mobility concepts.

Method. The study employs perturbative methods, including Schrieffer-Wolff transformations and non-Hermitian Hamiltonians, to analyze the effective coupling strengths and decay rates in open quantum systems.

Model / system. The system modeled is superconducting transmons coupled via tunable couplers. The analysis extends beyond the computational subspace to include leakage excitations (e.g., $|2 angle$ states).

Key observables. Leakage hopping rates, population decay times ($T^*$), effective coupling strengths ($g_{ ext{eff}}$), and residual ZZ interactions ($\zeta$).

Important parameters / regimes. Frequency detunings ($\Delta$), anharmonicity differences ($\Delta U_{12}$), and coupler/qubit coupling strengths ($g_c, g_q$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the dispersive regime ($\lambda \ll 1$) and uses approximations like the Rotating-Wave Approximation (RWA) for passive dynamics.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate connectivity maps, detuning conditions required for interaction cancellation, time evolution of leakage populations in different removal schemes (Coupler20, Pumped21), and effective hopping strengths vs. detuning.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes leakage dynamics across increasing complexity: first characterizing general mobility; then analyzing two-transmon systems to suppress coherent exchange ($g_{ ext{eff}}=0$); subsequently extending to three-transmon chains to study higher-order tunneling; and finally proposing specific passive removal units.

Abstract

Qubit leakage is a noticeable source of errors for quantum computing. In quantum processors, leakage excitations traveling between qubits generate correlated errors and perturb gate implementations. Leakage mobility can also be utilized for creating dedicated leakage removal pathways and removal units. To quantitatively characterize leakage mobility and to guide better design of processor architectures, we study here leakage dynamics in transmons with tunable couplers through numerical and analytical methods. Even if the couplers are tuned to cancel the single-excitation exchange or the ZZ interaction, the leakage hopping rates still persists in the range of 0.8-10 MHz due to transmon nonlinearity. In typical operation regimes, however, transmon frequency detuning localizes leakage excitations. The next-nearest-neighbor transmons can be still be near-resonant opening leakage tunneling channels. To suppress longer-range hopping, we find that the frequency spread of the next-nearest-neighbor transmons needs to be in the range of 1-4 MHz. Utilizing leakage mobility, we propose two passive leakage removal units. One is based on a tunable coupler and a pumped transmon, and another on a junction readout scheme. Based on realistic experimental parameters, our results on selectively mobilizing or localizing leakage excitations are readily applicable in superconducting quantum devices.

Summary. This paper analyzes topological Anderson insulators under long-range correlated disorder, demonstrating that such correlations cause the system's properties to become non-self-averaging. The authors introduce the concept of a 'non-self-averaging topological Anderson insulator,' characterized by a finite relative variance of the Lyapunov exponent in the thermodynamic limit. This shows that for these systems, individual sample measurements are fundamentally different from ensemble averages.

Why it may be interesting. This work extends the understanding of topological phases into complex disordered regimes, providing a quantitative diagnostic ($R_\gamma$) for when simple averaging techniques fail, which is crucial for modeling real-world amorphous or glassy quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The research investigates topological Anderson insulators subjected to long-range correlated disorder, an area lacking tractable analytical methods compared to standard uncorrelated disorder models.

Main result. Long-range correlated disorder can induce non-self-averaging behavior in the topological states, meaning single sample properties deviate significantly from ensemble averages.

Method. The study uses numerical calculations involving ensemble averaging over multiple disorder realizations and analyzing the relative variance of the Lyapunov exponent to diagnose self-averaging breakdown.

Model / system. The primary model is a disordered Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain, where the disorder ($\epsilon_j$) is characterized by long-range correlations defined by a power-law spectral density $S(k) \sim 1/k^\alpha$.

Key observables. Topological Quantum Number ($Q$), Lyapunov Exponent ($\gamma$), and the Relative Variance of the Lyapunov Exponent ($R_\gamma$).

Important parameters / regimes. The correlation parameter $\alpha$ (where $\alpha=0$ is uncorrelated disorder) and the system size $N$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that non-self-averaging is confirmed by a finite relative variance ($R_\gamma$) in the thermodynamic limit, signaling a breakdown of the Central Limit Theorem.

Figures summary. Figures compare $\langle Q angle$, $\langle \gamma angle$, and $R_\gamma$ across different correlation parameters ($\alpha$), showing smooth crossovers for long-range disorder versus sharp transitions for uncorrelated disorder.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by establishing the limitations of current theory, introducing the model with long-range correlated disorder, demonstrating that this correlation leads to non-self-averaging behavior via finite $R_\gamma$, and concluding that ensemble averages are insufficient for characterizing the phase diagram.

Abstract

Current research on the disordered topological quantum phases primarily focuses on the uncorrelated and short-range correlated disorder regime. These topological Anderson insulators are typically self-averaging. However, topological quantum systems with long-range correlated disorder have received limited attention due to the absence of tractable analytical methods. In fact, the long-range correlated disorder introduces more complex effects on topological quantum states. Here, we demonstrate that the long-range correlated disorder could induce anomalously statistical feature where the topological Anderson states become non-self-averaging, and the phase diagram is strongly dependent on individual disorder configurations. We term this statistical phase the non-self-averaging topological Anderson insulator. The non-self-averaging property is identified by the non-vanishing finite values of the relative variance of the Lyapunov exponent in the thermodynamic limit, alongside the non-Gaussian distributions of the Lyapunov exponent. Consequently, the topological properties of a single disordered sample deviate from the ensemble average, causing a breakdown of the central limit theorem. The non-self-averaging topological Anderson insulator provides insights into the interplay between correlated disorder and topology.

Summary. The paper rigorously classifies three-qubit nonlocality paradoxes by developing advanced graph-theoretic and combinatorial techniques. It demonstrates that these paradoxical structures are far more abundant than previously believed, overturning restrictive assumptions about the necessary form of such paradoxes.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep structural insights into the limits of local realism in multipartite quantum systems, which is foundational knowledge for understanding genuine quantum advantage.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To completely classify all three-qubit nonlocality paradoxes established via a biconditional parity proof, extending beyond previously known examples like the GHZ paradox.

Main result. The authors provide a complete structural classification for these paradoxes, showing that the landscape of nonlocality is much richer than previously thought by identifying new exotic paradoxes.

Method. The work employs graph-theoretic formalisms (2-CNF formulae and implication graphs) combined with combinatorial techniques to map the set of specifications ($ ext{BPP}$) to a canonical data structure ($ ext{CanonicalTriples}$).

Model / system. The focus is on three-qubit quantum systems exhibiting 'nonlocality paradoxes,' which are logical obstructions to classical probabilistic models arising from restricted joint measurability contexts.

Key observables. Nonlocal fraction, the existence of a witness path in the implication graph $I_{\Psi}(z)$, and the structure of the associated biconditional parity proof.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of qubits (fixed at three), measurement settings $\{M\}$, and parameters defining the entangled state $| ext{B}(\lambda, \Phi) angle$.

Abstract

Quantum nonlocality paradoxes, such as that of GHZ, provide maximally sharp logical obstructions to classical probabilistic models of quantum correlations. They are key resources in a broad variety of information-theoretic tasks that exhibit unconditional quantum advantage. For example, in nonlocal games, which are communication tasks that serve as core technical tools in recent landmark results in quantum computational complexity theory. Their role in establishing quantum advantage motivated their study by Abramsky et al. who introduced an infinite family of three-qubit paradoxes exhibiting novel conditional structure. This was later extended by de Silva et al. into a full classification program. In this work, we completely classify all three-qubit nonlocality paradoxes established via a biconditional parity proof; this is a very large class of paradoxes that encompasses all earlier-known examples. We do this by introducing a suite of new structural and combinatorial techniques. We find that the landscape of nonlocality paradoxes is far richer than previously understood, violating regularity conditions underlying all prior constructions.

Abstract

Scalable fault-tolerant quantum computation requires quantum error-correcting codes that simultaneously support universal logical operations, suppress hardware-specific noise, and enable efficient handling of photon-loss errors. These features make the DRCC a promising bosonic code for hardware-efficient, bias-preserving, and erasure-resilient fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Abstract

The Obara-Saika (OS) method is one of the most widely used techniques in quantum chemistry for evaluating electron repulsion integrals (ERIs) via a set of recurrence relations that build higher angular momentum integrals from lower-order ones. The original derivation by Obara and Saika proceeded by directly relating integrals of differing angular momentum. In this work, we present a compact novel derivation of the OS vertical recurrence relation based solely on differential relations between Gaussian basis functions, expanding on a method suggested in earlier work. This approach has pedagogical value as a rigorous and self-contained derivation. Additionally, the resulting organization exposes independent primitive derivative quantities and may be useful for code generation and parallel implementations on modern GPU architectures.
