Laughlin quasihole geometry from a single snapshot ensemble

Authors: Kaushlendra Kumar
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22384v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work demonstrates that the complex quantum geometry of lattice Laughlin quasihole states can be fully determined from measuring the occupation probability distribution at just one reference point. By exploiting the known analytic structure of the state amplitudes, the authors reconstruct the quantum metric and Berry curvature, showing that single-shot measurements are surprisingly powerful tools for characterizing quantum geometry.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful, non-standard method to extract full geometric information (metric, curvature) from minimal quantum measurements, which is highly relevant for characterizing complex many-body ground states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is determining complex quantum geometric properties, such as the quantum metric and Berry curvature, using only a single probability distribution measurement (snapshot) from a lattice Laughlin quasihole state.

Main result. A single snapshot measurement is sufficient to reconstruct the complete complex Gram kernel, thereby fixing the quantum metric and Berry curvature of the state family.

Method. The reconstruction relies on a single-reference kernel identity, using the known analytic quasihole factor and the measured occupation probability $P_0(n)$ to determine the overlap kernel $K(w, v)$.

Model / system. The system is modeled by lattice Laughlin quasihole states, parameterized by the quasihole position $w$, whose amplitudes factorize based on the occupation configuration $n$.

Key observables. Quantum metric coefficient $G(w)$, Berry curvature $\Omega_{xy}$, Overlap Kernel $K(w, v)$, and the Connes distance $d_\Lambda$.

Important parameters / regimes. The reconstruction is limited by the Rényi-2 divergence $D_2(P_w\|P_0)$ and the local single-copy multiparameter information bound.

Assumptions / limitations. The reconstruction critically depends on the known analytic structure of the quasihole factor $f_n(w)$ and assumes the reference point $w_0$ is generic.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the reconstruction of the scalar metric coefficient $G(w)$ both analytically and numerically, alongside the singular values of the state matrix.

Paper structure. The paper develops a kernel identity from the single snapshot measurement, uses this identity to derive geometric quantities like the metric and distance, and finally analyzes the statistical limitations using divergence measures.

Abstract

Can a probability distribution measured in one basis determine complex quantum geometry? The answer is affirmative for a lattice Laughlin quasihole. The exact occupation law at one generic reference position, together with the known analytic quasihole factor, fixes the complete complex Gram kernel. A finite snapshot ensemble estimates this kernel without preparing another member of the family, and thereby determines finite-distance overlaps, Bargmann phases, the quantum metric, and the Berry curvature. The same analytic structure confines the family to an exact projective subspace whose dimension grows at most linearly with particle number. Exact enumeration of a Nielsen-Cirac-Sierra state demonstrates finite-shot reconstruction of both the metric and a geometric phase. Moreover, a Rényi-2 divergence sets the statistical range of the reconstruction while, at nondegenerate points, occupation readout also attains the local single-copy multiparameter information bound.

Measurement and reload costs in direct quantum simulation of nonlinear waves

Authors: Ziqing Guo, Viraj Dsouza, Alex Khan, Abhishek Chopra, Rut Lineswala, Ziwen Pan
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21647v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work analyzes the resource costs of simulating nonlinear waves like the NLSE using quantum processors via a hybrid measurement-update-reload scheme. The authors demonstrate that the constant need to measure and reload the field state causes the quantum computational cost to scale worse than classical methods. Consequently, they identify achieving a coherent, measurement-free nonlinear update as the critical goal for realizing quantum advantage in this domain.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous, resource-aware analysis of the limitations of current quantum simulation schemes for nonlinear dynamics, setting a clear, quantitative benchmark (measurement-free evolution) for future hardware development.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the high computational cost associated with simulating nonlinear wave equations on quantum processors due to the necessity of repeatedly measuring and reloading the field state.

Main result. The quantum cost per step, dominated by measurement and reload overhead, scales poorly with grid size, showing no end-to-end advantage over classical methods at fixed precision.

Method. A hybrid split-step solver is proposed, which alternates between quantum evolution (linear steps) and classical updates (nonlinear kicks), with the field state being measured and reloaded at every time step.

Model / system. The primary models are nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs), such as the Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation (NLSE) and the Burgers' equation. The quantum encoding maps an N-point field onto log_2(N) qubits.

Key observables. Overlap fidelity (F), phase-factor error, total step latency (T_step), and the ratio of quantum to classical work ($W_Q/W_{cl}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The resource budget is controlled by the timestep ($\Delta t$), polynomial degree ($d$), and shot count ($S$). The quantum cost scales as $O(N^2/\epsilon^2)$ due to readout requirements.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the measurement and reload costs dominate the overall computational work, and the PolyKick approximation is only accurate when the local nonlinear phase is small.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the measure-update-reload cycle, fidelity diagnostics showing error sources, and resource comparisons demonstrating the scaling mismatch between quantum and classical work.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the hybrid solver, develops a comprehensive cost model accounting for shots and gates, validates the kernels on hardware, compares the resource scaling against classical FFT-based methods, and concludes by identifying the measurement-free update as the necessary target for quantum advantage.

Abstract

Quantum processors encode an N-point field in log_2(N) qubits, which renders nonlinear wave equations an important application for quantum simulation. Nonlinear evolution, however, requires the field values themselves, and these are not directly accessible without quantum measurement. Existing algorithms circumvent this measurement through linear embeddings and state copies, thereby obscuring its cost within the truncation order, the auxiliary dimensions, and the state preparation. In order to expose this cost, a hybrid split-step solver is proposed in which the field is measured, updated classically, and reloaded at every step, with all shots and gates accounted for in a single cost-and-error model. Since the entire field is available at every step, a property unavailable to linear approximations in strongly nonlinear regimes, the design of the solver reduces to a budgeting problem over the timestep, the polynomial degree, and the shot count. The coherent kernels of the solver are validated on superconducting hardware. An identical structure and bottleneck govern the viscous Burgers' equation in one and two dimensions. Because every step reads the full field, the quantum cost per step, measured as circuit depth multiplied by measurement shots, exceeds the classical cost with increasing grid size. The framework consequently identifies a coherent, measurement-free nonlinear update as the quantitative target that any end-to-end advantage must meet.

Odd-frequency pair correlations in the trapped fermionic Tonks-Girardeau gas

Authors: M. Wong, K. Thompson, M. Governale, J. Brand, U. Zuelicke
Type: theory · Category: quantum gases · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21917v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5

Summary. This theoretical study examines the fermionic Tonks-Girardeau gas, an exactly solvable model for strongly interacting fermions. The authors demonstrate the presence of unconventional odd-frequency pairing, which is a boundary-localized, mesoscopic phenomenon. They achieve this by generalizing ODLRO theory to analyze the time-dependent two-body correlation matrix.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses advanced topics in strongly correlated quantum gases, specifically exploring unconventional pairing symmetries (odd-frequency) beyond standard BCS theory, which is highly relevant to modern AMO research.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the existence and nature of unconventional odd-frequency pair correlations within the fermionic Tonks-Girardeau (FTG) gas.

Main result. Odd-frequency pairing is predicted to exist in the trapped FTG gas, but it is a mesoscopic effect localized at the system's boundary and nonextensive thermodynamically.

Method. The analysis generalizes Yang's theory of Off-Diagonal Long-Range Order (ODLRO) by using the Time-Dependent Two-Body Correlation Matrix ($ ho_2$) and its time-ordered counterpart ($ ho_T^2$).

Model / system. The system is the fermionic Tonks-Girardeau (FTG) gas, an exactly solvable model known to exhibit conventional p-wave Cooper pairing, which is then analyzed under the influence of a trapping potential.

Key observables. The order parameter $\phi_T^0(x, y; t)$ and the macroscopic eigenvalue $n_0(t)$ derived from the T2bCM are key diagnostics for superfluidity and pairing.

Important parameters / regimes. Fixed particle number ($N$), finite time delay ($t$), and the trapping potential $V(x)$ (harmonic or square-well).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the Off-Diagonal Long-Range Approximation (ODLRO) and the use of the Time-Dependent Two-Body Correlation Matrix formalism.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the spatial dependence of wave functions and the time evolution of the macroscopic eigenvalue $n_0(t)$ for various particle numbers and potentials, alongside scaling plots for the odd-frequency pairing magnitude.

Paper structure. The work progresses by establishing the theoretical framework using the T2bCM, deriving the macroscopic eigenvalue $n_0(t)$ via ODLRO, and finally applying this formalism to calculate and analyze the signature of odd-frequency pairing in the trapped FTG gas.

Abstract

The fermionic Tonks-Girardeau gas is an exactly solvable model realizing a conventional macroscopic condensate of p-wave Cooper pairs. Here we demonstrate that, when confined by a trapping potential, it also hosts unconventional odd-frequency pairing, i.e., pair correlations with s-wave symmetry that are only present with a finite time delay t between fermions forming a pair. Quantitative results for fixed particle number are obtained using a generalization of Yang's theory of off-diagonal long-range order to define a t-dependent order parameter that is antisymmetric under combined t inversion and exchange of fermion-pair indices. Odd-frequency pairing in the fermionic Tonks-Girardeau gas is found to be a mesoscopic effect, localized at the system's boundary and therefore nonextensive thermodynamically. Our results show that this elusive hidden order is accessible in systems having fixed particle number, suggesting new avenues towards its experimental realization and further detailed study.

Towards a Digital Twin for the Ground to QEYSSat Quantum Link

Summary. This paper presents a novel Monte Carlo simulator designed as a digital twin for the QEYSSat satellite quantum link. It accurately models all physical impairments—from source jitter and atmospheric turbulence to detector afterpulsing—in a probabilistic framework. This tool allows researchers to predict the achievable secure key rate, providing a crucial validation baseline for future space-based quantum communication missions.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it applies advanced simulation techniques to model open quantum systems (photon propagation) in a complex, noisy environment (space channel), directly impacting the feasibility and security analysis of quantum communication.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Simulating quantum key distribution (QKD) over space links requires high-fidelity models because simple analytical approximations compromise the provable security of the key.

Main result. The authors introduce a comprehensive Monte Carlo simulator that serves as a digital twin for the QEYSSat mission, capable of predicting secure key lengths under realistic physical conditions.

Method. A novel Monte Carlo simulation framework is developed to probabilistically model every physical interaction a photon undergoes from emission through transmission and detection.

Model / system. The system is the Quantum Encryption and Science Satellite (QEYSSat) quantum link. The simulator models the entire chain: Source (using Poisson statistics), Channel (accounting for atmospheric effects, loss, and turbulence), and Detector (incorporating memory effects like afterpulsing).

Key observables. Secure key length, Quantum Bit Error Rate (QBER), and detailed photon count data (timetags).

Important parameters / regimes. Link loss (potentially $> 35 ext{ dB}$), detector deadtime, afterpulsing probability, and atmospheric parameters (e.g., from MODTRAN).

Assumptions / limitations. The simulation simplifies state tracking by using a 'state palette' instead of tracking full density matrices, and it assumes the primary loss mechanism is channel attenuation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the full software stack (digital twin), the simulation chain (Source $ ightarrow$ Channel $ ightarrow$ Detector $ ightarrow$ TimeTags), and the main iterative process of the simulator.

Paper structure. The paper details the necessity of high-fidelity simulation, introduces the QMCS methodology covering source, channel, and detector modules, demonstrates its application to QEYSSat links (including Eve's attack), and discusses its utility as a predictive digital twin for mission validation.

Abstract

Simulations of physical systems require high-fidelity models to accurately represent reality. Simple models may be analytically tractable, but may not be sufficiently representative of reality for the given application. The cost of this simplicity is accuracy, or in the case of quantum key distribution, provable security. Sources of this accuracy gap include the difficulty of modelling physical effects which do not lend themselves well to analytical descriptions, such as afterpulsing. Here, we introduce a novel Monte Carlo based photon emission, transmission, and detection simulator, designed in the context of the Quantum Encryption and Science Satellite (QEYSSat) mission. Within this simulator, every major physical effect a photon may experience during an experiment, from emission to detection, can be accounted for in a probabilistic manner. This methodology allows for the inclusion of experimental parameters which are relevant for a satellite mission, and their impacts on secure key lengths. This simulator serves as a comprehensive baseline to predict and validate experimental data for the upcoming QEYSSat mission.

Belief Propagation-based Disentanglers for Tensor Network State Preparation

Authors: Tomasz Szołdra, Peter Schmelcher
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21902v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a Belief Propagation-based Disentangler (BPD) to synthesize quantum circuits for preparing complex Tensor Network states. By iteratively minimizing local edge entropy using nearest-neighbor gates, the method efficiently prepares states like TFIM ground states and Gaussian wavefunctions. This work significantly advances the transfer of classical tensor network physics onto quantum hardware.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a scalable, hardware-agnostic protocol to map classically tractable quantum states (like those from BP) onto quantum circuits, which is crucial for near-term quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing a quantum circuit synthesis method to prepare complex Tensor Network (TN) states for quantum simulation.

Main result. The Belief Propagation-based Disentangler (BPD) successfully prepares target states like Gaussian distributions and TFIM ground states on large lattices with high fidelity using only a few layers of local gates.

Method. The method reduces state preparation to a sequence of classical, local variational optimizations by applying nearest-neighbor 'disentangler' gates that minimize edge entropy, avoiding barren plateaus.

Model / system. The method targets quantum many-body states represented by Tensor Networks (TN), specifically those tractable via Belief Propagation (BP) approximations on graphs, including loopy lattices.

Key observables. Edge 2-Rényi Entropy, Fidelity ($F$), $\langle Z angle$, $\langle X angle$, and $\langle ZZ angle$ for the TFIM.

Important parameters / regimes. Bond dimension ($\chi$), number of disentangling layers ($L$), and the graph structure (e.g., heavy-hex lattice).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on the BP approximation for loopy graphs and assumes the optimization landscape is free of barren plateaus.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the BPD process, showing entropy reduction across layers for both Gaussian and TFIM targets, and comparing resulting observables against established benchmarks like DMRG.

Paper structure. The paper details the BPD algorithm, its application to preparing Gaussian states and TFIM ground states, and analyzes the convergence and stability of the process by monitoring entropy and observables across increasing bond dimensions.

Abstract

We develop a quantum circuit synthesis method for preparing a class of tensor network states. The scheme applies to states tractable with belief propagation (BP), a tensor network gauging scheme which recently allowed for classical simulations at large scales. The problem is reduced to independent, strictly local, classical variational optimizations: each nearest-neighbor two-qubit "disentangler" gate minimizes the entropy defined on an edge. Disentanglers drive the state to a product state and their Hermitian conjugate prepares the target. Each disentangling layer has depth at most $z+1$ (with $z$ the maximal number of nearest neighbors per site), the optimization has no barren plateaus, and the bond dimension stays bounded. As a demonstration, with only $3$-$5$ disentangling layers we prepare a $102$-qubit tree tensor network encoding a $17$-dimensional normal distribution and the transverse-field Ising model ground states on a $64$- to $127$-qubit heavy-hex lattice with fidelities of order $0.9-0.999$. The method opens new possibilities for quantum applications by transferring classical tensor network states onto hardware.

Entanglement governs early-time growth of randomness in projected ensembles

Authors: Thuwaragesh Jayachandran, Wai-Keong Mok
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22158v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · scars & prethermalization 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes how randomness builds up in quantum systems when a subsystem is measured, a process known as projected ensemble dynamics. It proves that the initial rate of this randomness growth is dictated solely by the initial bipartite entanglement between the measured and unmeasured parts of the system. This establishes entanglement as the primary mechanism controlling the onset of local quantum scrambling.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, quantitative link between entanglement measures (bipartite entanglement) and the initial rate of local information scrambling, which is fundamental to understanding thermalization dynamics in many-body quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the mechanism governing the initial growth of randomness in projected quantum ensembles, contrasting this initial dynamics with the later emergence of universal pure-state statistics (deep thermalization).

Main result. The initial growth of local randomness in projected ensembles is governed entirely by the bipartite entanglement between the unmeasured subsystem and its complement, setting the initial timescale independently of the measurement basis.

Method. The analysis quantifies randomness using 'frame potentials' ($F^{(k)}(t)$) and employs time-series expansions (Taylor expansion) to analyze the short-time dynamics of projected states.

Model / system. The study considers a bipartite quantum system AB, initially in a product state, where subsystem B undergoes measurement, projecting the state onto an ensemble on subsystem A. The dynamics are governed by a general Hamiltonian $H$.

Key observables. Frame potentials ($F^{(k)}(t)$), subsystem purity ($F^{(1)}(t)$), and the entanglement timescale ($ ext{T}_{ ext{ent}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The order of the frame potential ($k$), time ($t$), and the entanglement timescale ($ ext{T}_{ ext{ent}}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The initial bath state must have full support in the measurement basis for the leading-order results to hold; the analysis focuses strictly on the early-time regime.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate early-time approximations of $F^{(k)}(t)$ for specific models (like the 1D mixed-field Ising model) and compare these results to theoretical predictions derived from spectral form factors.

Paper structure. The paper develops the formalism for frame potentials, derives the early-time expansion for $F^{(k)}(t)$ based on the initial product state, and extends the analysis to unitarily invariant Hamiltonian ensembles, establishing a connection to spectral form factors.

Abstract

Deep thermalization concerns the emergence of universal pure-state statistics in projected ensembles at late times, yet the mechanism governing the initial growth of randomness remains unclear. Here, we study the short-time dynamics of projected ensembles generated from initially unentangled states, quantifying their randomness using frame potentials. For arbitrary Hamiltonians, provided the initial bath state has full support in the measurement basis, we show that the frame potentials to cubic order in time are determined entirely by the subsystem purity, and hence by the bipartite entanglement generated between the unmeasured subsystem and its complement. The entanglement timescale therefore sets the initial timescale for the growth of local randomness, independently of the bath measurement basis. For unitarily invariant Hamiltonian ensembles, we further relate the projected-ensemble frame potential at order $k$ to the $4k$-point spectral form factor, or equivalently to the $2k$th frame potential of the global unitary dynamics, establishing a direct connection between local and global randomness. Our results identify entanglement as the mechanism governing the onset of randomness in projected ensembles and clarify how local randomness emerges from global quantum dynamics.

Estimating the Fisher information from ARPES in general two-band models

Summary. This paper presents a novel theoretical and experimental scheme to measure the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) in two-band solid state systems using Angle-Resolved Photoemission Spectroscopy (ARPES). By relating the QFI to the Classical Fisher Information derived from Pauli measurements, the authors provide a pathway to probe fundamental quantum properties, such as the quantum metric or strain response, using tunable light polarization and incidence angles.

Why it may be interesting. This work bridges fundamental quantum information theory (QFI) with cutting-edge experimental condensed matter spectroscopy (ARPES). The ability to map a theoretical quantum metric onto a measurable optical observable is highly relevant for quantum sensing applications in solid-state physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of estimating the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) for a quantum state in a general two-band solid state system using Angle-Resolved Photoemission Spectroscopy (ARPES). It seeks to establish a measurable link between the theoretical QFI and experimentally accessible quantities.

Main result. The authors propose a detailed experimental scheme that relates the QFI to the Classical Fisher Information (CFI) derived from Pauli measurements, which can be measured by tuning ARPES incidence angles and light polarization.

Method. The methodology involves deriving the CFI from the measured photoemission intensity, which is proportional to a Pauli measurement observable. This requires advanced reconstruction techniques using multiple polarization measurements (e.g., EDAC code) to determine the necessary polarization vectors.

Model / system. The study focuses on general two-band solid state systems, exemplified by 2D materials like graphene and hexagonal boron phosphide (hBP). The physics is described using a Bloch Hamiltonian and analyzed via ARPES measurements of the photoemission intensity.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), Classical Fisher Information (CFI), Quantum Metric, ARPES intensity $I(E, \mathbf{k}_f, \omega)$, and the dichroism coefficient (CDAC).

Important parameters / regimes. System parameters ($\lambda$) such as strain, incidence angles ($ heta_{ ext{inc}}, \phi_{ ext{inc}}$), and light polarization ($\alpha, \delta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes rapid photoionization and approximates the final state as a scattered wave. Furthermore, the optimal measurement basis requires careful selection of polarization vectors to satisfy specific symmetry conditions.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the conceptual flow from Hamiltonian to QFI to CFI to ARPES measurement. Later figures show the numerically calculated optimal polarization angles required to measure the QFI response to strain or other parameters for graphene and hBP.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical link between QFI and measurable CFI via Pauli measurements. It then details the experimental implementation using ARPES, involving the reconstruction of orbital matrix elements from multiple polarization measurements. Finally, it applies this framework to calculate the QFI response to external perturbations like strain for graphene and hBP.

Abstract

The Quantum Fisher information is frequently studied in the fields of quantum sensing and quantum metrology, as it specifies the sensitivity of a sensor through the Crámer-Rao bound, and in condensed matter physics where it relates to the quantum metric. Measuring the quantum Fisher information is therefore of significant current interest. In this work, we propose a scheme for measuring the quantum Fisher information with respect to any parameter in a general two-band solid state system using angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy with carefully tuned incidence angles and light polarization. We investigate conditions under which this can be related back to information about the ground state of the material itself changing with system parameters.

Quantum-Geometric Length Scale for Long-distance Squeezing in Bosonic Bogoliubov Systems

Authors: Sonu Verma, Sangmo Cheon, Myung-Joong Hwang, Moon Jip Park
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23380v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. The authors demonstrate that engineered flat bands in bosonic lattices enable long-range quantum correlations and squeezing, bypassing limitations imposed by dispersive wave propagation. They introduce an intrinsic quantum-geometric length scale ($\xi_S$), determined by a complex-momentum singularity, which governs the decay of these non-classical resources. This provides a powerful theoretical tool for controlling quantum resources in condensed matter systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a deep theoretical link between the spectral geometry (flat bands) of interacting bosonic systems and the spatial extent of non-classical quantum resources like squeezing, which is highly relevant for quantum simulation and quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses how to achieve and quantify long-distance, phase-sensitive quantum correlations and sub-vacuum squeezing in bosonic lattices without relying on standard dispersive propagation mechanisms.

Main result. Parametric pairing can create an exactly flat Bogoliubov band, defining an intrinsic quantum-geometric length scale ($\xi_S$) that controls the spatial range of squeezing and anomalous correlations.

Method. The analysis involves constructing canonical Bogoliubov modes from compact generators and quantifying squeezing using the squeezing-sector symplectic quantum metric, linking the scale to a complex-momentum singularity.

Model / system. The system is a one-dimensional bosonic lattice modeled using Bogoliubov theory, specifically utilizing parametric pairing ($\Delta(k)$) to engineer an exactly flat band ($\omega_f$) within the spectrum.

Key observables. Anomalous correlation $F(R)$, sub-vacuum squeezing depth $D_{ ext{out}}(R)$, and the intrinsic quantum-geometric length scale $\xi_S$.

Important parameters / regimes. The flat band frequency ($\omega_f$), the complex-momentum singularity location ($z_S$), and the weak-damping limit.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of an exactly flat band and, in some analyses, assumes the weak-linewidth and narrow-filter limit for pole structure analysis.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the lattice setup showing the flat band, the mechanism involving compact generators, and the relationship between the correlation decay length and the momentum-space width defined by $\xi_S$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the physical model (flat band via pairing) to deriving the mathematical structure (canonical modes, symplectic metric) and finally quantifying the resulting physical resource (squeezing range and length scale $\xi_S$) through various diagnostic tools.

Abstract

Multimode squeezing is a key resource for continuous-variable quantum technologies, but its spatial range in bosonic lattices is usually tied to dispersive propagation. Here we show that parametric pairing can create an exactly flat Bogoliubov band spanned by compact Bogoliubov generators, while producing phase-sensitive anomalous correlations and sub-vacuum collective-mode squeezing that extend far beyond their finite support. Each compact generator mixes annihilation and creation operators, thereby encoding the Bogoliubov squeezing structure, and neighboring translated generators can have nonzero commutator overlap. Enforcing canonical bosonic commutation relations therefore requires spatially extended linear combinations of these translated compact generators, which define the canonical Bogoliubov modes. The squeezing transformation of these modes varies with momentum and is quantified by the squeezing-sector symplectic quantum metric. A complex-momentum singularity of the analytically continued canonical Bogoliubov modes sets both the anomalous-correlation decay length and the momentum-space width of this metric, defining an intrinsic quantum-geometric length scale. This singularity can be tuned continuously while preserving exact flatness, thereby controlling the spatial range of correlations and squeezing. Away from exact flatness, long-distance correlations persist through multiple decay channels, while modes localized by defects and dimerized boundaries provide complementary probes of the underlying quantum geometry. In the weak-damping limit, the same quantum-geometric length scale can be extracted from frequency-filtered two-port correlations. Our results establish the quantum geometry of canonical Bogoliubov modes as a mechanism for spatially extended quantum resources arising from compact Bogoliubov generators.

Theory of the Spinon-Mediated Witness Spin Glass in Herbertsmithite

Authors: Mitikorn Wood-Thanan, Felix Flicker
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21514v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical work models the spin glass behavior in Herbertsmithite, where magnetic impurities interact via spinons from the underlying Quantum Spin Liquid (QSL). By comparing predictions from $\text{Z}_2$ and $\text{U}(1)$ QSL models against multiple experimental measurements, the authors propose a definitive neutron scattering test to distinguish between the two candidate QSL ground states.

Why it may be interesting. This work bridges the gap between abstract QSL theory and concrete experimental signatures in frustrated magnets, providing a clear, testable prediction (neutron scattering difference) that guides future experimental efforts.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To provide a full theoretical account of the spin glass formation among magnetic impurities (witnesses) in Herbertsmithite, mediated by the Quantum Spin Liquid (QSL) spinons.

Main result. The model successfully captures multiple experimental signatures, and crucially, predicts a qualitative difference in neutron scattering intensities between the $\text{Z}_2$ and $\text{U}(1)$ QSL candidates, offering a definitive test.

Method. The authors developed a theoretical model by deriving an effective witness-witness interaction ($J_{ij}$) from the QSL spinon susceptibility, followed by large-scale classical Monte Carlo simulations.

Model / system. The system is Herbertsmithite, a prototypical Quantum Spin Liquid (QSL) candidate on a kagome lattice. The model treats magnetic impurities ('witnesses') whose interactions are mediated by the underlying QSL spinons, considering both $\text{Z}_2$ and $\text{U}(1)$ QSL ground states.

Key observables. Spin glass formation temperature ($T^*$), magnetic noise ($S(f, T)$), DC magnetic susceptibility ($\chi(T)$), and static neutron scattering structure factor $S(\mathbf{q})$.

Important parameters / regimes. Witness-kagome spin coupling ($\gamma$), temperature ($T$), and witness concentration ($x$).

Assumptions / limitations. Key approximations include treating the witness spins as classical Ising spins and using mean-field theory for the QSL description, neglecting back-action of witnesses on the kagome spins.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the model setup, showing $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ witnesses randomly occupying $\text{Zn}$ sites, and the theoretical predictions for various experimental observables like susceptibility and neutron scattering structure factor.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical problem (spin glass in Herbertsmithite), derives the effective Hamiltonian via QSL susceptibility, applies the model to fit multiple experimental data points, and finally uses the differences between $\text{Z}_2$ and $\text{U}(1)$ predictions to propose a definitive experimental test.

Abstract

Herbertsmithite is a prototypical candidate quantum spin liquid (QSL), believed to feature a long-range entangled ground state and deconfined fractionalised spinon excitations. Confirmation of these properties has been hindered by the presence of magnetic impurities (spin-1/2 Cu2+ spins substituted onto non-magnetic Zn2+ sites). Recently these impurities were reconceptualised as 'witnesses' of the QSL, inheriting long-range interactions and entanglement mediated by the QSL spinons, leading to spin glass formation amongst witnesses below 260 mK. Here we present a full theoretical account of this idea. Despite having only one free parameter (the witness-kagome spin coupling), our model captures the full range of experimental data, including: the formation of a spin glass amongst witnesses; the frequency and temperature dependence of the magnetic noise; a sharp peak in the DC magnetic susceptibility as a function of temperature; and the static neutron scattering structure factor at 2 K. Both candidate QSLs (Z2 and U(1)) give similar agreement with all data; however, our model predicts a qualitative difference between the Z2 and U(1) neutron scattering intensities below 260 mK, providing a long-sought definitive test to distinguish the two cases. Abstract

Thermodynamic bounds on the spectrum of a Markov cycle depend on the cycle affinity and maximum escape rate, but they ignore how the local transition rates are distributed around the cycle. In this work, we show that a single additional cycle-wide kinetic quantity, the geometric means of forward and backward rates, yields a strictly stronger bound for every nonuniform cycle. By comparing each winding sector of the eigenmode with a product-matched uniform cycle, we bound oscillation frequencies, tighten the admissible spectral region, and show that only uniform cycles can saturate the Uhl-Seifert boundary. Our results show that retaining a coarse-grained kinetic scale can sharpen thermodynamic spectral bounds without requiring knowledge of the full generator. Our method also provides a complex-analytic framework which unifies generator-specific kinetic information with established affinity-winding bounds.

Abstract

Equilibrium free-energy differences can be measured from nonequilibrium work fluctuations using fluctuation theorems, such as the Jarzynski equality and the Crooks fluctuation theorem. However, these approaches become statistically inefficient in high-dissipation regimes because their convergence requires trajectories with negative entropy production, events that occur exponentially rarely. Here, we demonstrate that repeated measurements of trajectory fluctuations are sufficient to determine entropy production without relying on such rare events, or to determine a lower bound on it when only partial measurements are available. This information then yields exact estimates of free-energy differences, or rigorous bounds. We validate this approach, named EquiNET, with numerical simulations, including one of biomolecular folding and unfolding, showing that it recovers accurate free-energy estimates even in regimes where conventional approaches fail.
