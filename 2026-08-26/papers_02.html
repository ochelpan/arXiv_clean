Breakdown of Aharonov-Bohm cage in Rydberg synthetic lattices: the roles of inhomogeneity and long-range exchange

Authors: Li Pan, Xinlu Chen, Hong Zhang, Jian-Song Pan
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21768v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: 🔥 Rydberg arrays 4/5 · 🔥 analog quantum simulation 4/5 · interference shaping light 1/5

Summary. This paper analyzes the breakdown of the Aharonov-Bohm cage in Rydberg synthetic lattices by studying two-particle dynamics. Using the Evolution-Path Symmetry framework, the authors show that spatial inhomogeneity in nearest-neighbor exchange interactions is the critical ingredient required to break the cage. This mechanism links microscopic path interference directly to the emergence of delocalized, dispersive eigenstates, enabling transport.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a deep, analytical understanding of how specific interaction symmetries (or lack thereof, due to inhomogeneity) dictate the transition between localized and delocalized quantum transport in engineered lattice systems, highly relevant to Rydberg physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the microscopic mechanism by which interaction-induced exchange interactions cause the breakdown of the Aharonov-Bohm (AB) cage in quantum systems.

Main result. Inhomogeneous nearest-neighbor exchange interactions are necessary and sufficient to break the AB cage by disrupting path interference, leading to delocalized transport. Long-range interactions alone are insufficient to cause this breakdown.

Method. The analysis employs the Evolution-Path Symmetry (EPS) framework, analyzing two-particle dynamics through path interference in Fock space and comparing projections onto localized versus dispersive eigenstates.

Model / system. The system is modeled using Rydberg synthetic lattices, which realize flat-band physics and exhibit AB cage confinement. The dynamics are governed by a two-particle Hamiltonian incorporating nearest-neighbor and long-range exchange interactions.

Key observables. Population dynamics ($P_6$ vs. interaction strength $V$), spatial distribution of particles, and the coherence of the path interference factor ($\Xi(n)$).

Important parameters / regimes. The relative strengths and spatial variation (inhomogeneity) of nearest-neighbor and long-range exchange coupling constants.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis focuses on the two-particle sector, and the initial state is assumed to possess exchange symmetry.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the time evolution of particle population showing regimes of cage preservation, delocalized transport, and freeze-out, alongside spatial distributions confirming confinement or delocalization.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the physical system and Hamiltonian to applying the EPS framework. It then systematically compares the effects of different interaction components (uniform NN, inhomogeneous NN, LR only, combined) on the path interference, linking this microscopic path behavior to macroscopic spectral reorganization and transport.

Abstract

While the interaction-induced breakdown of Aharonov-Bohm (AB) cage is typically attributed to uniform bound-pair transport, systems with inhomogeneous exchange interactions realized with Rydberg synthetic lattices exhibit more complex dynamics. Employing the evolution-path symmetry (EPS) framework developed recently, we analyze the two-particle dynamics via path interference in Fock space. We find that a homogeneous nearest-neighbor exchange interaction cannot break the AB cage, regardless of whether the long-range exchange interaction is present or not. In contrast, we demonstrate that inhomogeneous nearest-neighbor exchange interaction breaks the destructive-interference EPS, and lifts the degeneracy of many-body compact localized states, thereby generating non-local dispersive eigenstates. Consequently, the initial state gains a non-zero overlap with these dispersive states, enabling delocalized transport. Furthermore, while long-range exchange interaction alone preserves the AB cage, its coupling with nearest-neighbor inhomogeneous exchange interaction opens non-canceling pathways that alter the diffusion profile. Our work connects microscopic path interference with macroscopic spectral reorganization, offering an analytical understanding of the mechanism underlying exchange-interaction-induced transport in Rydberg synthetic lattices.

Main problem. The study investigates the stability and dynamics of persistent supercurrents in spinor atomic Bose-Einstein condensates confined to a ring structure with multiple Josephson junctions.

Main result. The presence of a second atomic species allows for unprecedented control over supercurrents, enabling mechanisms like phase-slip engineering and current-inversion switching.

Abstract

The investigation of superflow in multi-junction Josephson circuits is currently a leading frontier of physics research, probing the fundamental manifestations of macroscopic phase coherence and enabling applications to quantum simulation, metrology and computing. In this work, we extensively investigate the stability and dynamics of persistent currents of a spinor atomic Bose-Einstein condensate on a ring with multiple Josephson junctions. Specifically, we examine the effects of positive and negative interspecies interactions, co- and counter-rotation, population and Manakov asymmetry, as well as Rabi coupling on the persistent currents carried by the two components. Our analysis reveals that the presence of a second species offers multiple mechanisms for unprecedented manipulation of supercurrents on the ring, including phase-slip engineering, stability control, current-inversion switching and supercurrent pumping. Our study provides a roadmap for the engineering of persistent currents in binary ring condensates in necklace potentials, with significant implications for atomtronics, matter-wave interferometry and sensing using atomic Bose gases.

Summary. This paper reports on a novel sympathetic cooling technique applied to a long, dual-isotope ion chain. The technique effectively suppresses motional heating across all relevant vibrational modes during quantum operations. This achievement is a critical step toward building fault-tolerant, scalable quantum processors using trapped ions.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial experimental demonstration of noise mitigation (motional heating suppression) in a scalable, multi-qubit platform, directly addressing a major bottleneck for trapped-ion quantum computing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge addressed is suppressing motional heating in long ion chains to enable deep quantum computations without causing qubit decoherence or degrading connectivity.

Main result. The authors successfully demonstrated steady-state mid-circuit sympathetic cooling of all relevant motional modes in a 23-ion chain, while preserving qubit coherence throughout the cooling sequence.

Method. The cooling is achieved sympathetically using a dual-isotope system, performed without direct qubit operations, and is tested over a realistic 56-ms quantum circuit duration.

Model / system. The system is a 23-ion chain composed of two different Yb isotopes (${}^{171} ext{Yb}^+$ qubits and ${}^{172} ext{Yb}^+$ coolant ions) trapped in a room-temperature linear RF ion trap.

Key observables. Mean phonon occupations of axial and radial modes, qubit coherence, and the decay rate of Rabi oscillations.

Important parameters / regimes. The experiment operates over a 56-ms circuit time with a 25% gate duty factor, and specific secular frequencies are mentioned.

Assumptions / limitations. The cooling scheme assumes the ability to perform initial state preparation via Doppler and Raman sideband cooling, and the analysis relies on fitting Rabi oscillations to model heating rates.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental setup (side, front, and top views of the 23-ion chain) and show diagnostics like population transfer probability vs. time and mean occupation number vs. idle time.

Paper structure. The paper details the experimental setup, characterizes the initial heating rates for both axial and radial modes, implements the sympathetic cooling protocol, and evaluates the preservation of qubit coherence and gate fidelity.

Abstract

Owing to their high controllability and connectivity, one-dimensional ion chains are attractive building blocks for near-term quantum computers. However, long ion chains are susceptible to motional heating caused by fluctuating external electric fields. To enable deep computations, this source of noise must be suppressed without inducing qubit decoherence or degrading qubit connectivity. We demonstrate steady-state mid-circuit sympathetic cooling of all computationally-relevant motional modes of a 23-ion chain consisting of \qubit qubit and \coolant coolant ions without using any qubit operations. In a room-temperature system, our cooling scheme preserves the mean phonon occupations of the long-wavelength axial and radial modes near their values following state preparation, while keeping the modes used to implement entangling gates near their ground states throughout a 56-ms circuit. Crucially, we show that our cooling sequence preserves qubit coherence, and evaluate its impact on single- and two-qubit gate operations. Additionally, we demonstrate a parallel qubit reset protocol leveraging the shared radial mode coupling between species. Our sympathetic cooling scheme represents a critical step toward reducing gate errors in individually-addressed long ion chains and establishes a versatile platform for simulating open quantum systems.

Summary. This theoretical study models an atomic chain subjected to a weak transverse electric field, analyzing how this field modifies the electronic band structure using Bloch theory. By combining the intrinsic lattice potential with a catenary-shaped field potential, the problem reduces to solving a Mathieu equation. The results show that the field induces measurable changes in the band gaps, providing insight into field-induced spectral reorganization in low-dimensional solids.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, solvable model demonstrating how external fields can tune the band structure of low-dimensional periodic systems, which is relevant for understanding field effects in quantum materials and optical lattices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how a weak, static electric field applied transversely to an effectively one-dimensional periodic atomic chain reorganizes its electronic Bloch spectrum.

Main result. The transverse field modifies the Bloch spectrum by introducing field-dependent band energies and corresponding Bragg gaps, which is governed by a Mathieu equation.

Method. The analysis uses Bloch reduction in reciprocal space, treating the total potential as a sum of an intrinsic lattice potential and a field-induced catenary potential.

Model / system. The system is an infinite 1D atomic chain with a periodic potential ($V_{ ext{latt}}$) subjected to a weak transverse field, modeled by a catenary potential ($V_{ ext{cat}}$). The resulting Hamiltonian is analyzed via Bloch theory.

Key observables. Field-dependent band energies and the hierarchy of Bragg gaps.

Important parameters / regimes. The field strength ($E_\perp$), which dictates the magnitude of the catenary potential ($V_c$), and the lattice periodicity ($G$).

Assumptions / limitations. The field is weak, allowing for approximations, and the transverse displacement is assumed negligible, projecting the response onto the chain axis.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the effective Hamiltonian by combining the intrinsic lattice potential (yielding a $|r-s|^{-1}$ hierarchy) and the catenary contribution (yielding a $|r-s|^{-2}$ correction). It then solves the resulting secular equation, leading to a Mathieu equation, to determine the field-dependent band structure.

Abstract

We consider an effectively one-dimensional periodic atomic chain subjected to a weak static electric field transverse to the chain axis. The intrinsic lattice is represented in reciprocal space by a harmonically weighted periodic pseudopotential, while the transverse field produces a weak elastic and polarizable response whose minimum-energy distribution is approximated by a periodically repeated catenary profile. Bloch reduction places both contributions in a common reciprocal-space Hamiltonian. The intrinsic lattice produces a \ (|r-s|^ {-1} ) hierarchy, whereas the catenary contribution is a symmetric Toeplitz correction with coefficients proportional to \((1+π^2(r-2) ^ {-1} \) and therefore asymptotically to \ (|r-s|^ {-2} ). The complete nonzero reciprocal weight of the catenary contribution is absolutely convergent and sums to \ (|V_ {\rm c} |/e ). At the unit-cell level, the lowest intrinsic harmonic together with the catenary term gives a Mathieu equation containing both cosine and hyperbolic-cosine modulations. A first-Brillouin-zone reduction then yields the field-dependent band energies and the corresponding hierarchy of Bragg gaps. The model isolates a simple mechanism by which a transverse electric field can reorganize the Bloch spectrum of a low-dimensional periodic system. Keywords: Bloch theory; transverse electric field; catenary potential; Mathieu equation; Brillouin zone; band structure.

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes how different measurement readouts (Arthurs-Kelly vs. homodyne) affect Gaussian filtering in a continuous-variable cluster wire. It shows that finite AK measurements implement a conditional filter whose output properties depend on the input state, distinguishing it from simple additive noise channels. This provides a rigorous tool for understanding measurement-level control in quantum information processing.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed, quantitative understanding of how measurement choices (readout patterns) fundamentally alter the quantum information processing capabilities of continuous-variable quantum systems, which is crucial for quantum networking and measurement-based quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper characterizes the Gaussian filtering implemented by a continuous-variable cluster wire when using Arthurs-Kelly (AK) readout, contrasting it with standard homodyne readout.

Main result. Finite AK readout generates a pattern-resolved, conditional Gaussian filter whose gain and residual covariance depend on the input state, unlike the input-independent additive channel from homodyne measurements.

Method. The analysis uses the covariance-matrix formalism and the Schur complement to derive the conditional output covariance for various readout patterns (HH, AH, HA, AA).

Model / system. The system is a three-node line graph representing a continuous-variable cluster wire, analyzed using Gaussian state descriptions and controlled-phase network calculations.

Key observables. Output-to-input symplectic-area ratio ($\eta_R$) and the gain determinant ($ au_R$).

Important parameters / regimes. Readout patterns (HH, AH, HA, AA); finite readout variances ($ u_q, u_p$); input covariance entries ($Q, P, C$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes calibrated, uncorrelated AK readout and focuses on the reduced finite-nullifier description for finitely squeezed wires.

Figures summary. Figure 2 shows $\eta_R$ for the four patterns over the physical input-covariance region, while Figure 3 shows the gain determinant $ au_R$ for the three finite-AK patterns, demonstrating area contraction.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mathematical framework for the readout patterns, derives the conditional Gaussian filtering rules via the Schur complement, and then compares the resulting filtering properties ($\eta_R$ and $ au_R$) of the finite-AK patterns against the homodyne reference.

Abstract

Measurement readout programs the Gaussian operation implemented by a continuous-variable cluster wire. For the standard three-node wire, momentum homodyne readout gives an additive parity channel with finite-squeezing noise. We classify the pattern-resolved input--output covariance transformations generated by calibrated uncorrelated Arthurs--Kelly readout on one or both consumed nodes. Any finite Arthurs--Kelly position record produces a conditional Gaussian filter whose gain and residual covariance depend on the input covariance entries, rather than an input-independent additive Gaussian channel. Writing $\mathrm{A}$ for Arthurs--Kelly readout and $\mathrm{H}$ for homodyne readout, the readout pattern selects the filtered sector, with $\mathrm{AH}$ selecting position, $\mathrm{HA}$ selecting momentum, and $\mathrm{AA}$ retaining both. The hierarchy $0 < τ_{\mathrm{AA}} < \min \left\{ τ_{\mathrm{AH}}, τ_{\mathrm{HA}} \right\} < 1 = τ_{\mathrm{HH}}$ shows that finite Arthurs--Kelly position records contract the gain-transferred covariance area. Our results identify calibrated uncorrelated Arthurs--Kelly readout as a measurement-level method for covariance-sensitive Gaussian filtering inside a fixed cluster graph.

Quantum observables as Fréchet sensitivity kernels

Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.23191v1

Summary. This paper proposes a variational-adjoint interpretation of quantum mechanics where the interaction between forward and adjoint wavefunctions defines a general 'Fréchet sensitivity kernel.' It demonstrates that the standard Born probability rule emerges as a specific, positive-definite instance within this broader class of sensitivity measures. This offers a mathematically rigorous framework connecting quantum observables to sensitivity analysis, with implications for quantum control.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a novel mathematical lens—sensitivity analysis—to re-examine the measurement problem in QM, potentially unifying different interpretations under a single mathematical structure.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the conceptual meaning of quantum mechanics by providing a variational-adjoint interpretation to reconcile the deterministic evolution of the wavefunction with the probabilistic nature of measurement outcomes.

Main result. It shows that the familiar Born probability density is a special, positive-definite case of a general 'Fréchet interaction density' derived from the interaction between forward and adjoint wavefunctions.

Method. The core method is the variational-adjoint formulation applied to the Schrödinger equation, utilizing concepts from functional analysis and sensitivity analysis.

Model / system. The system is general quantum mechanics, governed by the Schrödinger equation. The mathematical framework involves defining an adjoint wavefunction ($\psi^\dagger$) associated with a chosen observable or measurement functional.

Key observables. Fréchet interaction density, Born probability density, momentum, energy, and spin sensitivity kernels.

Important parameters / regimes. The Hamiltonian ($H$), time ($t$), and the choice of observable defining the adjoint wavefunction.

Assumptions / limitations. The interpretation assumes the validity of a variational structure that allows the definition of an adjoint field ($\psi^\dagger$) to quantify measurement sensitivity.

Figures summary. No specific figures were described, but the notes mention a comparison table (Table 1) summarizing major interpretations of QM.

Paper structure. The paper develops the variational-adjoint formalism, derives the general Fréchet sensitivity kernels, demonstrates that the Born rule is a special case, and discusses connections to time-symmetric QM and quantum control.

Abstract

We present a variational--adjoint interpretation of quantum mechanics in which the interaction between forward and adjoint wavefunctions defines a general sensitivity kernel of the system. This Fréchet interaction density quantifies how perturbations in the wavefunction (or system parameters) influence a chosen observable. The familiar Born probability density appears as a special case when the adjoint wavefunction is chosen as the complex conjugate of the forward wavefunction, for which the interaction density becomes real and non-negative. Within this framework, probability is a particular positive-definite form of sensitivity described by the Fréchet interaction density. In general, the variational--adjoint interpretation also produces Fréchet sensitivity kernels associated with other quantum observables, including momentum, energy, and spin. This suggests that the Born probability density belongs to a broader class of Fréchet sensitivity kernels associated with quantum observables. The proposed interpretation also provides a connection with time-symmetric interpretations of quantum mechanics and possible future applications in quantum control and quantum metrology.

Summary. This work uses the deceptively simple 'do-nothing' state-transfer task to rigorously probe the physical connectivity and noise profile of quantum computers. By measuring both uniform reliability and best-case path lengths, the authors distinguish between how robust a processor is everywhere versus how far it can achieve a perfect path. This provides critical, hardware-specific metrics for assessing quantum hardware quality.

Why it may be interesting. It provides an empirical, hardware-specific diagnostic tool to quantify the spatial noise landscape of quantum processors, which is crucial for designing fault-tolerant quantum circuits that require long-range state transfer.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper assesses the spatial limitations of quantum processors by determining how far a quantum state can be reliably transferred across physical qubits using a minimal 'do-nothing' protocol.

Main result. The study reveals a sharp distinction between uniform spatial reliability (isotropic radius) and best-case performance (longest successful route), with different quantum processors exhibiting vastly different noise profiles.

Method. A route-resolved comparative assessment is performed using the 'do-nothing' state-transfer protocol, which involves routing a prepared quantum state across physical qubits via SWAP gates and measuring final state fidelity.

Model / system. The experiments are conducted on commercial Quantum Processing Units (QPUs), specifically Rigetti’s Cepheus-1-108Q and IBM Heron-r2 processors, treating the physical qubit connectivity as the system under test.

Key observables. State fidelity, isotropic radius (uniform reliability), and maximum successful swap distance (best-route performance).

Important parameters / regimes. The success criterion is defined by maintaining a fidelity above the classical limit (e.g., F > 2/3).

Assumptions / limitations. The reported quantities are empirical and conditional on the specific route families, finite-shot decision rules, and execution time, not universal constants.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the do-nothing protocol implementation and map the connectivity and successful routing areas on the QPU architectures.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the 'do-nothing' protocol as a spatial probe, details the methodology for calculating isotropic radius and maximal path length, and presents comparative results for different QPU hardware platforms.

Abstract

We present a route-resolved comparative assessment of Rigetti's Cepheus-1-108Q and IBM Heron-r2 processors using the established 'do-nothing' state-transfer protocol. Rather than proposing a new protocol, we use this deterministic, low-complexity task as a high-resolution spatial probe. For each evaluated initial qubit, we report two complementary quantities: the largest tested radius within which every evaluated shortest route satisfies the operational success rule, and the longest successful route identified within the evaluated route family. To achieve this, we address a deceptively simple yet foundational question: ``How far can you do-nothing on a quantum computer?'' Operationally, this do-nothing protocol serves as a fundamental state transfer protocol: we prepare an initial quantum state, route it across the physical qubits using SWAP gates, and measure the final state fidelity against the well-established classical fidelity limit for single-qubit state transfer. While this trivial state-transfer protocol serves as the most intuitive baseline, actively preserving a quantum state across a physical lattice proves to be a non-trivial task that exposes the information to cumulative relaxation, dephasing, and environmental cross-talk. In the highlighted IBM QPU case, we identify an isotropic radius of 10 and a successful path of swap distance 27, whereas the highlighted Rigetti Cepheus case exhibits an isotropic radius of 1 but selected above-threshold routes reaching swap distance 8. These results reveal a sharp distinction between uniform spatial reliability and best-route performance. The presented quantities are empirical and conditional on the evaluated route families, finite-shot decision rule, calibration state, and execution time; they are not architecture-wide constants.

Summary. This theoretical study analyzes how energy flows anomalously in spin-Peierls chains by coupling lattice vibrations (phonons) to magnetic excitations. By comparing continuous versus pulsed laser driving, the authors show that the energy transfer mechanism is dictated by dynamic impedance matching, not just the material's inherent energy spectrum. This establishes crucial limits for controlling magnetic states using light fields without destroying the underlying quantum coherence.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a detailed, non-equilibrium quantum description of energy coupling in magnetic insulators, which is highly relevant for understanding quantum control in strongly correlated materials using light fields.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates anomalous energy flows and energy cascades in spin-Peierls chains, specifically focusing on how nonlinear phononics mediate energy transfer between the lattice and magnetic sectors.

Main result. Energy transfer is governed by a dynamical impedance-matching condition rather than the unperturbed density of states. Furthermore, pulsed driving preserves quantum interference features, while continuous driving leads to back-action and feature smearing.

Method. The analysis employs the bond-operator formalism combined with a second-order cumulant expansion of the Lindblad master equation to model non-equilibrium dynamics.

Model / system. The system is a strongly dimerized spin-Peierls chain, modeled with coupled phonon modes (IR and Raman) and a spin Hamiltonian described by bond operators. The total dynamics are governed by a complex Hamiltonian incorporating nonlinear phononic coupling.

Key observables. Energy transfer rates ($P_{l o IR}$, $P_{IR o S}$), phonon damping/power broadening, and sharp Fano-like quantum interference features.

Important parameters / regimes. The comparison between Continuous-Wave (CW) vs. pulsed THz driving regimes, and the condition for impedance matching.

Assumptions / limitations. Key approximations include the strong-dimerization limit ($J \gg J'$) and treating optical phonons as dispersionless Einstein modes.

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the multi-stage energy transfer process: THz laser $ o$ IR phonon $ o$ Raman phonon $ o$ Magnetic modulation $ o$ Triplet generation.

Paper structure. The paper builds the model by defining the coupled Hamiltonian, deriving the equations of motion via the Lindblad master equation, calculating sequential energy flow rates using commutators, and finally contrasting the dynamics under CW versus pulsed driving.

Abstract

We investigate the nonequilibrium spin-phonon dynamics and energy cascades in a strongly dimerized spin-Peierls chain using a multi-tiered nonlinear phononics architecture. To bypass linear selection rules prohibiting the direct excitation of the Raman-active dimerization mode, a terahertz laser drives an infrared mode that nonlinearly couples to the Raman lattice displacement, subsequently modulating the magnetic exchange. Employing a bond-operator formalism with a second-order cumulant expansion of the Lindblad master equation, we show that maximum energy transfer into the magnetic sector is governed by a dynamical impedance-matching condition rather than the unperturbed triplon density of states. We find that the sustained energy input of continuous-wave driving builds high excitation densities that severely back-act on the lattice, overdamping the primary phonon and smearing magnetic features via power broadening. Conversely, the small time-integrated energy of a pulse keeps the response perturbative, terminating before back-action accumulates and preserving sharp Fano-like quantum interferences. These insights establish limits for controlling dynamic magnetic states without quenching the driving lattice modes.

We also characterize the finite-driving corrections, together with the discontinuous $π$ jump that the Uhlmann phase undergoes on the equatorial cycle as the temperature is varied. Finally, we examine an isolated transversal gap closing, where this zero-temperature correspondence can break down: the pure-state path becomes open, its closure is ambiguous, and gap-opening regularizations close it along a geodesic set by the direction of the bias field, splitting the Berry phase into a one-parameter family of values. The Gibbs path instead closes smoothly through the maximally mixed state, so the Uhlmann holonomy requires no regularization and remains unique and continuous at every finite temperature. In the low-temperature limit the Uhlmann phase selects a single member of the Berry family---the geodesic closure in the osculating plane, fixed by the velocity and acceleration of the driving field. The Uhlmann--Berry correspondence of the gapped regime thus survives the gap closing as a geodesic selection rule.
