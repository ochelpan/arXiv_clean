A Physical Response-and-Memory Model for Muon Optimization

Authors: Yinze Hu, Hongjun Xiang, Xingao Gong, Hongyu Yu
Type: theory · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22994v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Frenkel-Kontorova 1/5 · correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5 · non-equilibrium universality 1/5

Summary. This paper develops a physical model treating the neural network weight matrix as a memory-laden, responsive medium. By interpreting optimization momentum as accumulated internal stress, the authors propose the Bi-Maxwell optimizer, which uses a two-timescale memory kernel. This approach successfully models the underlying physics of learning dynamics and achieves state-of-the-art performance on LLM training benchmarks.

Why it may be interesting. The framework draws deep analogies between the physics of dissipative media (stress, relaxation, dissipation) and the abstract mathematics of deep learning optimization, providing a physical grounding for empirical ML heuristics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental problem of determining the optimal update rule (optimizer) for training large language models, recognizing that this choice dictates the final achievable loss for a given computational budget.

Main result. The authors propose the Bi-Maxwell optimizer, which incorporates a two-timescale stress memory, leading to significantly faster convergence on benchmark LLM training tasks.

Method. They build a physical model treating the weight matrix as a responsive medium with memory, deriving the optimal update direction from principles of maximal dissipation under an output-side safety budget.

Model / system. The system is the weight matrix ($W$) of a neural network, modeled as a responsive medium whose dynamics are governed by a mismatch potential ($\Phi$) that dictates restoring forces and structural rearrangement rates.

Key observables. Momentum (interpreted as internal stress), the optimal memory length, and the structural rearrangement rate ($V$).

Important parameters / regimes. The relaxation timescales of the stress memory (fast and slow), and the output-side safety budget ($\epsilon$).

Assumptions / limitations. The structural rearrangement rate is assumed to be governed by a viscous analogy related to the mismatch potential, and the model predicts that the optimal memory length must grow with the training stage.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the responsive medium construction and mismatch potential; Figure 8 shows the local kernel-age proxy ($n_\star$) confirming that the optimal memory length increases across training stages.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical analogy by defining the weight matrix as a responsive medium, deriving the governing dynamics from a mismatch potential. It then proposes the Bi-Maxwell optimizer by extending the memory kernel to two timescales, and finally validates this model by testing the predicted growth of optimal memory length against empirical benchmarks.

Abstract

Training large language models is costly. How low a loss the same compute can ultimately reach depends on how each step's gradient is converted into a weight update; the rule that performs this conversion is the optimizer. From SGD and AdamW to the recent Muon, effective update rules have mostly been shaped by engineering intuition and then selected on benchmarks. Muon semi-orthogonalizes the momentum matrix before applying the update and has kept breaking records on public training benchmarks; yet why the semi-orthogonalized direction works, and over how long a history the momentum should average, are two questions at present answered mainly by experience. Here we treat the weight matrix during training as a responsive medium with memory and build a physical model for it, in which both questions find answers: the semi-orthogonalized direction is the maximally dissipative response under an output-side safety budget, which explains why it works; momentum is the internal stress accumulated by the medium; how long it should average is set by the relaxation of this stress, and a real medium relaxes on more than one timescale, the simplest form being one fast and one slow. On this basis we propose the Bi-Maxwell optimizer. The framework further yields a testable consequence: gradient directions change fast early in training and more slowly later, so the optimal memory length should grow with training stage; step-by-step measurements of a proxy for it by a read-only probe across 8 independent training trajectories are consistent with this consequence. Replacing the memory kernel alone, from a single timescale to two, brings training to the target loss in noticeably fewer steps on a public large-language-model optimizer benchmark.

Summary. This paper numerically investigates Boltzmann's H-theorem by simulating how an ensemble of states approaches maximum entropy under detailed balance dynamics. It compares different update rules, finding that the rate of probability equalization is highly dependent on the connectivity of the state space. The work highlights the mathematical mechanisms underlying the arrow of time in statistical mechanics.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a detailed numerical study of how fundamental concepts like entropy increase and equilibrium are realized in abstract dynamical systems, touching upon the deep connection between microscopic dynamics and macroscopic irreversibility.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates Boltzmann's H-theorem, which posits that the entropy of an ensemble increases until all states become equally probable, exploring the dynamics of this approach to equilibrium.

Main result. The rate of probability equalization depends heavily on the update dynamics; random pairing leads to rapid convergence, while restricted dynamics (like on a circle) are significantly slower.

Method. The study employs numerical Monte Carlo simulations to track the evolution of the ensemble's entropy and the equalization of state probabilities over time.

Model / system. The system is an abstract ensemble of thermodynamic states with probabilities $\{p_i\}$, whose evolution is governed by dynamics satisfying detailed balance conditions.

Key observables. Entropy change ($\Delta S$), fraction of states acquiring equal probability, and the distribution of relaxation times.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of states ($N$), the temperature ($T$), and the specific update rule (e.g., random pairing vs. nearest neighbor on a circle).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the framework of detailed balance dynamics and compares results from specific discrete update rules, noting the conflict between the theorem's irreversibility and time-reversal symmetry.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the rapid equalization of probabilities under random pairing versus slow equalization on a circle, show entropy fluctuations around equilibrium, and map the spectrum of relaxation times.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the H-theorem and the relevant dynamics, presents numerical simulations comparing different update rules, analyzes the convergence rates and entropy fluctuations, and discusses the theoretical implications regarding time's arrow.

Abstract

The Boltzmann H-theorem states that entropy of an ensemble of thermodynamic states increases until all states become equally probable. The evolution randomly picks one of the states in the ensemble and brings every other state to have the same probability as the selected state. Different realizations of evolution yield a distribution of selected states. We study this distribution numerically and discuss its relevance for equilibrium fluctuations in thermal as well as non thermal systems.

A minimal mechanism to generate long timescales without fine tuning

Summary. This work proposes a minimal modification to a linear recurrent neural network by adding a dynamical feedback gain. This gain self-regulates the network's dynamics, ensuring that the system naturally operates at a critical point, thereby generating long-range, scale-free temporal correlations without requiring precise parameter tuning.

Why it may be interesting. The mechanism of self-organization to maintain a critical state is a general principle applicable to many complex systems, including those studied in AMO physics where parameter tuning is often a major hurdle.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of generating long timescales and scale-free correlations in neural dynamics without relying on fine-tuning of synaptic coupling strengths.

Main result. Introducing a dynamical recurrent feedback gain self-organizes such that the top eigenvalue of the effective recurrent matrix always lands at the stability edge, leading to scale-free correlations generically.

Method. The analysis involves linearizing the coupled dynamics (neuron activity and gain) around an active fixed point and analyzing the resulting relaxation rates and correlation functions.

Model / system. The system is a minimal linear stochastic recurrent neural network model incorporating a dynamical gain term that depends on the total network activity. The dynamics are governed by coupled differential equations for neuron states and the gain parameter.

Key observables. Long-time autocorrelation function $C(t)$, which exhibits algebraic decay ($t^{-\alpha}$) when the spectrum is gapless, and the self-organized gain $a^*$.

Important parameters / regimes. The coupling strength $c$ (interaction strength), the noise variance $\sigma^2$, and the characteristic timescales $ au_x, au_a$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the recurrent matrix $M$ is symmetric (e.g., GOE) and that the gain unit only senses the total network activity ($\|x\|^2$).

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the emergence of an active fixed point at a critical coupling strength $c_{ ext{crit}}$. Figure 2 shows the deterministic flow spiraling into the active fixed point in the $(y_N, a)$ plane. Figure 3 confirms the $\propto t^{-1/2}$ power law decay of the normalized correlation function for various coupling strengths.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the problem of fine-tuning in standard models. It then introduces the dynamical gain mechanism, deriving the coupled equations. The core analysis shows that the gain self-organizes to enforce criticality, leading to a stable critical phase characterized by power-law decay, independent of the initial coupling strength.

Abstract

Long timescales in brain dynamics give rise to power law correlations measured in experiments. A simple linear recurrent neural network model can reproduce this power law behavior, but the recurrent interaction strength needs to be fine tuned in order to sit at the edge of stability. We show that by adding a dynamical recurrent feedback gain to the simple linear model removes the need for fine tuning, because the gain self-organizes so that the spectrum of the effective recurrent matrix is always gapless with the top eigenvalue landing on the stability edge for any recurrent strength. As a consequence, long timescales and scale free correlations arise generically, as we demonstrate both analytically and numerically.

Summary. This work investigates DyPtBi, an antiferromagnetic topological insulator candidate, to show how ferroquadrupolar order couples strongly to its electronic structure. By measuring resistivity and elastic constants under varying stress, the authors demonstrate that the material's topological properties can be actively controlled by manipulating its magnetic domain state. This highlights a promising route for engineering topological materials using strain engineering.

Why it may be interesting. The strong coupling between lattice degrees of freedom (quadrupolar order) and topological electronic states provides a platform for understanding emergent quantum phenomena controllable by strain, which is relevant to designing novel quantum materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Investigating anomalous metallic states at magnetic interfaces in an antiferromagnetic topological insulator candidate, focusing on the interplay between magnetic order, topological states, and ferroquadrupolar order.

Main result. The material exhibits topological electronic states entangled with multipolar degrees of freedom, allowing for in situ control of topological properties via external stress.

Method. The study combines transport measurements (resistivity), ultrasonic measurements (elastic constants), and neutron diffraction experiments under uniaxial stress.

Model / system. The physical system is DyPtBi, an antiferromagnetic topological insulator candidate. The analysis involves modeling the coupling between the $4f$ electron spins (governed by Crystal Electric Field theory) and lattice distortions (ferroquadrupolar order).

Key observables. Resistivity jumps, softening of the transverse ultrasonic mode ($6\%$), and a strong resistivity enhancement ($14\%$) under compressive uniaxial stress.

Important parameters / regimes. The magnetic transition temperature ($T_N = 3.2 ext{ K}$), the applied uniaxial stress ($\sigma//[111]$), and the coupling constants governing quadrupolar interactions.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the low frequency limit for CEF analysis and models the total free energy including contributions from $4f$-electrons and phonons.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the temperature dependence of resistivity, magnetization, and elastic constants, showing anomalies near the magnetic transition, and schematics detail the electronic states at domain walls.

Paper structure. The paper systematically presents evidence for the interplay between magnetic and structural transitions, using transport and elastic measurements to confirm the role of ferroquadrupolar order, culminating in stress experiments that control the domain wall conductivity.

Abstract

Antiferromagnetic topological insulators provide a fertile platform where symmetry-breaking magnetic order is intertwined with topological electronic states. In particular, magnetic domain walls have attracted much attention, as they can be easily controlled by external fields as in general magnets, and moreover, host nontrivial electronic states distinct from those in bulks and sample surfaces. Here, we report a new antiferromagnetic topological insulator candidate DyPtBi, which hosts conductive magnetic domain walls controllable by uniaxial stress and magnetic field. We find that the resistivity exhibits abrupt increase upon the magnetic and structural phase transition. Concomitantly, the transverse ultrasonic mode shows remarkable softening of 6 %, indicating that the Dy 4f ferroquadrupolar order plays a vital role in the phase transition. Furthermore, we reveal by neutron experiments that applying compressive uniaxial stress aligns the magnetic domain state, leading to the strong resistivity enhancement of 14 % while eliminating the conductive magnetic domain walls. These findings demonstrate that DyPtBi exhibits topological electronic states entangled with multipolar degrees of freedom, providing a promising route for in situ control of topological properties.

Summary. This theoretical work develops general correlation inequalities for quantum spin glass models under a transversal field. By applying these inequalities, the authors characterize the phase diagram, predicting a transition from a low-field replica-symmetry-breaking regime to a high-field regime where replica order is absent. This provides deep insights into the quantum nature of glassy dynamics.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous mathematical tools developed for bounding overlaps and proving concentration are highly relevant for understanding quantum phase transitions in complex, disordered quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to derive general correlation inequalities for transversal field models and apply these tools to analyze the behavior of quantum spin glasses.

Main result. The authors establish distinct low- and high-field overlap phases for quantum spin glasses, showing that replica order vanishes in the high-field limit.

Method. The core methodology involves deriving a general correlation inequality using Gaussian convolution estimates, which extends the Ding-Song-Sun inequality and employs Brascamp-Lieb convexity techniques.

Model / system. The study focuses on transversal field models with pair interactions, encompassing the quantum Curie-Weiss, transversal field Ising model, and quantum spin glasses based on the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) and Edwards-Anderson models.

Key observables. Self-overlap ($S_N$), replica overlap ($R^{(1,2)}_N$), and the normalized quenched pressure ($p_N$).

Important parameters / regimes. Transversal field strength ($b$), interaction strength ($\|J\|$), and inverse temperature ($eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of sufficiently strong transversal fields and relies on functional integral representations and concentration inequalities.

Paper structure. The paper builds from establishing general correlation inequalities (Theorem 1.1) to proving concentration bounds for self- and replica-overlaps (Theorems 1.3 and 1.4), culminating in the determination of high-field asymptotics for the pressure in the quantum SK model.

Abstract

We study the class of transversal field models with pair interactions, including the quantum Curie-Weiss and the transversal field Ising model, as well as quantum spin glasses based on the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) and Edwards-Anderson models. Our main result is a general correlation inequality based on a Gaussian convolution estimate, which is derived from an extension of the Ding-Song-Sun inequality and an application of Brascamp-Lieb convexity techniques. The results apply for sufficiently strong transversal fields and, for quantum spin glasses, yield a high-field regime without replica order. Combined with the previously established low-field replica-symmetry-breaking regime, this gives distinct low- and high-field overlap phases. As a corollary of the logarithmic Sobolev inequalities for the underlying functional integrals derived here, we show the concentration of the self- and replica-overlap, and determine the high-field asymptotics of the pressure in the quantum SK model.

Numerical Evaluation of ZX Calculus Optimization for Solovay Kitaev Quantum Circuit Synthesis

Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22810v1

Summary. This work quantifies the structural redundancy present in quantum circuits generated by the Solovay-Kitaev algorithm. By employing diagrammatic post-processing via ZX-calculus, the authors demonstrate that significant portions of the gate count and T-count can be removed without affecting the implemented quantum operation. This suggests a powerful, systematic optimization pathway for compiling complex quantum circuits.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on quantum computation, the rigorous analysis of structural redundancy and the development of efficient compilation techniques using diagrammatic methods (ZX-calculus) provides valuable theoretical tools applicable to optimizing quantum simulations in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the structural redundancy inherent in quantum circuits synthesized using the Solovay-Kitaev algorithm for approximating arbitrary single-qubit unitaries.

Main result. Diagrammatic post-processing using ZX-calculus rewriting successfully recovers a significant fraction of this redundancy, removing 26.6-30.1% of the total gate count and 18.5-22.2% of the T-count.

Method. The authors synthesize target unitaries using Solovay-Kitaev, map the resulting gate sequences to ZX-diagrams, and then apply automated graph-theoretic rewriting rules to simplify the structure.

Model / system. The model is fault-tolerant quantum computation, specifically synthesizing single-qubit unitaries ($U \in SU(2)$) using the Clifford+T gate set.

Key observables. Total gate count, T-count, and the percentage reduction achieved after post-processing.

Important parameters / regimes. Recursion depth (controls approximation level $\epsilon_k$), target unitary family ($R_x, R_y, R_z, U( heta, \phi, \lambda)$), and the required precision $\epsilon$.

Assumptions / limitations. The comparison is against unoptimized Solovay-Kitaev output, and the compile-time cost of the rewriting layer can become dominant at deep recursion levels.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the fundamental rewrite rules of the ZX-calculus and show the evolution of gate/T-count reduction versus recursion depth.

Paper structure. The paper details the synthesis process, the mapping to ZX-diagrams, the application of automated rewriting rules, and quantitative measurements comparing the initial circuit size to the optimized size across various depths and targets.

Abstract

Fault-tolerant architectures implement non-Clifford T gates through magic-state distillation, so the T-count of a synthesized circuit dominates its physical cost. The Solovay-Kitaev algorithm approximates any single-qubit unitary from a finite gate set with a sequence length that grows only polylogarithmically in the inverse target error, but it optimizes for numerical convergence rather than circuit economy, and its output carries structural redundancy that a gate-level compiler cannot see. We report a measurement of what diagrammatic post-processing recovers from that redundancy. Twelve hundred random single-qubit targets, spanning the three Pauli rotation families and the general gate U(theta, phi, lambda), are synthesized over Clifford+T at three recursion depths, translated into graph-like ZX-diagrams, simplified by automated rewriting, and extracted back to circuits. Post-processing removes 26.6-30.1% of the total gate count and 18.5-22.2% of the T-count. The absolute saving grows with recursion depth, from about 60 to about 1600 gates, while the fractional saving does not: it rises slightly from the shallowest setting and is then flat across a twenty-five-fold change in circuit length, and by the deepest setting the four target families are no longer distinguishable from one another. Because the rewrite rules preserve the implemented linear map, the approximation error is unchanged. The compile-time cost of the rewriting layer, by contrast, grows sharply with depth and comes to dominate the synthesis itself.

Anomalous Local Heat Capacity and Bipartite Entanglement

Authors: Jake Xuereb, A. de Oliveira Junior
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.21508v1

Summary. This work establishes a quantitative link between quantum entanglement and the local heat capacity of interacting quantum systems. By deriving a separability bound based on energy fluctuations, the authors show that anomalous local heat capacities can serve as a thermodynamic witness for quantum correlations. This offers a powerful, general tool for characterizing quantum states using measurable thermal properties.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a novel, model-independent thermodynamic diagnostic tool—the local heat capacity bound—to detect quantum entanglement, bridging concepts from quantum information theory and classical thermodynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how quantum entanglement at equilibrium influences the local heat capacity of interacting quantum systems, particularly focusing on the occurrence of anomalous (negative) heat capacities.

Main result. A model-independent separability bound is derived connecting local heat capacity fluctuations to entanglement, showing that violations of this bound detect quantum correlations.

Method. The authors use thermodynamic identities relating heat capacity to covariance and variance, deriving separability bounds based on local uncertainty relations for the system's energy operators.

Model / system. The study analyzes bipartite interacting quantum systems, exemplified by nearest neighbour spin-1/2 chains, coupled quantum harmonic oscillators (QHOs), and general Hamiltonians $H = H_A + H_B + H_{ ext{int}}$.

Key observables. Local Heat Capacity ($C_X(T)$), Global Heat Capacity ($C(T)$), Mutual Information, and the separability bound derived from energy fluctuations.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$) or inverse temperature ($eta$), interaction strength ($J, \chi$), and the nature of the coupling ($H_{ ext{int}}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is primarily restricted to equilibrium states ($ ho_eta$) and bipartite systems, although the derived bounds are claimed to be model-independent.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate that local heat capacities can be anomalous in interacting systems, and they compare entanglement detection using the derived heat capacity bound against other criteria like the PPT boundary.

Paper structure. The paper develops the general framework by defining local heat capacity and deriving its decomposition. It then establishes the separability bound using fluctuation relations, applying this bound to specific models like spin chains and QHOs to demonstrate the connection between entanglement and thermodynamic anomalies.

Abstract

The heat capacity of a system quantifies how it energetically responds to changes in temperature at equilibrium. Whilst this quantity is positive and even additive for non-interacting systems, self-gravitating systems such as stars or subsystems of strongly interacting quantum systems are known to have negative or anomalous specific heat capacities. In this work, we investigate how the presence of entanglement at equilibrium can influence how an interacting system responds energetically to changes in temperature. We examine the local heat capacity of interacting quantum systems providing an analytical understanding for when anomalies occur. Most interestingly, we find a connection between local heat capacity anomalies and entanglement by deriving a separability bound based on the fluctuations of local and interaction energies. We illustrate our results with two examples (i) a nearest neighbour spin-1/2 chain and (ii) two coupled quantum harmonic oscillators. Lastly, we provide an information-theoretic formula connecting mutual information and athermality to the non-additivity of the heat capacity. Our results provide model-independent thermodynamic entanglement detection bounds and insight into the relationship between quantum correlations and the heat capacity of quantum systems.

Using semidefinite programming and a robust self-test framework, the authors provide theoretical and numerical evidence for practical key generation rates. This advances the field by quantifying the tolerance for imperfections in real-world quantum devices.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides theoretical guarantees on the practical feasibility of QKD, showing that small experimental deviations from ideal quantum correlations do not necessarily lead to a complete loss of cryptographic security.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To demonstrate that the security of device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD) remains robust and guarantees a constant key rate even when the observed Bell violation is only slightly less than the theoretical maximum (Tsirelson bound).

Main result. The authors prove that a constant, non-zero secret key rate can be guaranteed if the observed Bell violation is sufficiently close to the respective Tsirelson bound for the considered Bell inequalities.

Method. The work employs a 'robust self-test' approach, utilizing semidefinite relaxations of the minimum min-entropy consistent with a given Bell violation to derive lower bounds on the key rate.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on DIQKD protocols involving two parties (Alice and Bob) using uncharacterized devices, whose security is tested by measuring violations of specific Bell inequalities.

Key observables. Bell Inequality Violation ($\eta$), Key Rate ($R$), Tsirelson Bound ($ au$), Classical Bound ($eta$).

Important parameters / regimes. The deviation parameter ($\epsilon$) quantifying how close the observed violation is to the Tsirelson bound.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is based on specific families of Bell inequalities and assumes the asymptotic (i.i.d.) limit for the key rate calculation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the key rate as a function of the Bell score, showing the rate remains non-zero even as the violation approaches the classical boundary.

Paper structure. The paper builds upon previous work by extending the key rate guarantee from requiring the absolute maximum Bell violation to requiring only a sufficiently close approximation to that maximum.

Abstract

Recently, Wooltorton et al. [Phys. Rev. Lett. 132, 210802 (2024)] and Farkas [Phys. Rev. Lett. 132, 210803 (2024)] have exhibited the mismatch between Bell inequality violations and their cryptographic application in device-independent quantum key distribution, by showing that arbitrarily close to the set of local behaviours there exist quantum correlations guaranteeing a constant rate of secret key. While these results require correlations attaining the maximum quantum value of a suitable Bell observable (aka the Tsirelson bound) and rely on a kind of ideal self-testing of a maximally entangled state and associated Bell measurement, here we show that the effect is robust: for every one of the Bell inequalities considered by Wooltorton \emph{et al.}, a constant rate of secret key ensues if the observed Bell violation is sufficiently close to the respective Tsirelson bound. For these and also the Bell inequalities of Farkas, we furthermore present numerical results based on semidefinite relaxations of the minimum min-entropy consistent with a certain Bell violation, which demonstrate that small but nonzero key rates can be guaranteed (in principle) by practical and efficient means.
