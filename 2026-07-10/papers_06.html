Summary. This paper investigates the security limits of CV-QKD when using discrete modulation formats shaped probabilistically. It compares various distribution matching algorithms, finding that Constant Composition Distribution Matching (CCDM) provides the most accurate approximation to optimal distributions. This confirms that careful implementation of PCS is crucial for maximizing key rates and maintaining noise tolerance in practical quantum communication links.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous theoretical bounds on achievable secure key rates when practical digital signal processing techniques (like discrete constellation shaping via DM) are used in quantum communication systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes how using Distribution Matching (DM) algorithms for Probabilistic Constellation Shaping (PCS) affects the Secret Key Rate (SKR) in Continuous Variable Quantum Key Distribution (CV-QKD) protocols with discrete modulation.

Main result. Constant Composition Distribution Matching (CCDM) performs significantly better than symbol-by-symbol Huffman matching, achieving SKRs that closely match the theoretical optimum and maintaining high excess noise tolerance.

Method. The analysis involves calculating the SKR using established CV-QKD security formulas, comparing results from different DM techniques (HDM vs. CCDM), and quantifying statistical dependence on finite block lengths.

Model / system. The system is a CV-QKD protocol transmitting light field quadratures through a quantum channel characterized by transmittance ($ au$) and excess noise ($\xi_c$). The modulation uses discrete constellations shaped probabilistically.

Key observables. Secret Key Rate (SKR), maximum tolerable excess noise ($\xi^*_c$), and the performance degradation relative to ideal Gaussian modulation.

Important parameters / regimes. Constellation size (e.g., 64-QAM, 256-QAM), channel parameters ($ au$, $\xi_c$), and block lengths ($10^3$ or $10^5$ symbols).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis compares the performance of finite-length approximations (HDM/CCDM) against the ideal, continuous Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution.

Figures summary. Figures compare SKR vs. distance for various shaping methods and show $\xi^*_c$ values, demonstrating CCDM's superior noise tolerance compared to other matching techniques.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by establishing the need for PCS in CV-QKD, detailing the mathematical framework for SKR calculation, comparing multiple DM algorithms (HDM, GHDM, CCDM) against the optimal MB benchmark, and concluding with performance metrics like noise tolerance.

Abstract

Continuous variable quantum key distribution protocols (CV-QKD) with discrete modulation have been intensively investigated to bridge the gap between ideal Gaussian modulation and modern coherent optical communication systems. To mitigate the penalty of discrete modulation, probabilistic constellation shaping (PCS) is applied to the modulation format and is typically performed by distribution matching (DM) algorithms. In this paper, we address the application of DM algorithms to perform PCS in CV-QKD protocols. We investigate the impact of approximating optimized Maxwell-Boltzman distributions with DM algorithms based on Huffman (HDM) and constant composition (CCDM) codes on the protocol's secret key rate (SKR) and tolerance to excess noise. Our results show that specifically symbol-by-symbol HDM degrades the SKR by at least 30\%, whereas CCDM matches the optimal SKR with code length of $10^3$ or more symbols. Furthermore, we also provide a statistical analysis of symbol dependence for both approaches, showing that CCDM must operate with blocks of at least $10^5$ symbols for the correlations become negligible. Finally, we propose an algorithm to generate independent symbols following near-optimal distributions.

A quantum model for synchronizing finite state transition systems

Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.06953v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

Summary. This paper develops a quantum model to solve the synchronization problem for Finite State Transition Systems. By encoding the system dynamics into unitary operators and employing Grover search on superpositions of input sequences, it finds a Resetting Input Sequence (RS). The approach offers a significant quadratic speedup over known classical brute-force methods.

Why it may be interesting. This work demonstrates applying advanced quantum search techniques (Grover's algorithm) to solve a classically hard problem in automata theory, providing a clear computational complexity advantage relevant to quantum computation theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is finding a Resetting Input Sequence (RS) for a Finite State Transition System (FA) that forces the system into a specific state regardless of its initial condition.

Main result. The authors propose a quantum algorithm achieving a quadratic speedup over classical brute-force methods for solving this computationally hard synchronization problem.

Method. They encode the FA dynamics into unitary operators and utilize superposition, followed by Amplitude Amplification and Grover search to efficiently locate the required synchronizing sequence.

Model / system. The system models an FA using quantum registers for states ($|S angle$) and inputs ($|X angle$), embedding the transition function into global unitary matrices ($\Delta$).

Key observables. The primary observable is finding a collection of reached states that are all identical, corresponding to the target synchronizing state.

Important parameters / regimes. Complexity scales are compared: classical brute-force complexity vs. quantum search complexity (e.g., $\sqrt{M}$ steps).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes the ability to embed non-permutative transition matrices into unitary permutative matrices using ancilla qubits.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate circuits for applying single inputs, sequences of length $l$, and the overall evolution on a Quantum Total State (QTS).

Paper structure. The paper first defines the NP-Hard/PSPACE-Complete problem, then constructs the quantum model by encoding transitions into unitaries ($\Delta$), simulates state evolution via superposition, and finally applies Grover search to find the RS.

Abstract

We propose a quantum model for finding a resetting input sequence (RS) which can take a finite state transition system (FA), to particular state independent of its current state. The complexity of finding such sequences for various types of FA can be NP-Hard or even PSPACE-Complete. To this end, we represent the FA states, inputs, and transition function in quantum space. Accordingly, we propose a model to represent the execution of an input sequence of a particular length $l$ starting form an initial FA state. The model is extended considering the application in superposition of all input sequences of length $l$ to an initial state of the FA. The model is further extended considering the application of all input sequences to all initial states of the FA capturing for every input sequence the collection (ordered list) of states reached by applying the sequence to all states of the FA. The amplitude amplification algorithm is then used as it combines similar collections of reached states while preserving all input sequences that reach these collections. A Grover search for a reached collection where its elements correspond to the same FA state provides a RS for the FA. Our approach offers a quadratic gain over the exponential complexity of traditional brute-force method, which is the only method that can be applied to a general FA class. As a proof of concept we provide results of several simulated FAs on a quantum simulator.

Error bounds for the truncated Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff and Zassenhaus formulas in unitary problems

Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07692v1

Summary. This paper develops a rigorous mathematical framework to calculate precise error bounds when approximating complex unitary time evolution operators using truncated versions of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff and Zassenhaus formulas. By bounding the error in terms of nested commutators, it provides crucial theoretical guarantees for numerical quantum simulation techniques.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides essential mathematical tools for quantum simulation algorithms (like Trotterization or product formulas) by quantifying exactly how much error is introduced when truncating the necessary exponential series expansions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is deriving rigorous, general error bounds for approximating the logarithm of operator products (BCH) or the exponential of sums (Zassenhaus) when these infinite series must be truncated for practical computation.

Main result. The authors provide a general strategy and explicit formulas showing that the truncation error can be bounded by linear combinations of nested commutators, which exhibit a predictable scaling property.

Method. The analysis relies on comparing two representations of the evolution operator (e.g., $e^A e^B$ vs. $e^{\Phi(A, B)}$) and using differential equations satisfied by time-dependent unitary operators to bound the difference.

Model / system. The context is general unitary problems in quantum physics where the evolution operator is expressed as a product or sum of exponentials of non-commuting, skew-adjoint operators (e.g., $e^{tA}e^{tB}$).

Key observables. The error norm, $\| ext{Exact Evolution} - ext{Truncated Approximation} \|$, which is shown to be related to the $(m+1)$-th order term in the expansion.

Important parameters / regimes. The truncation order ($m$ or $q$) and the norms of the involved operators ($\|A\|, \|B\|$).

Assumptions / limitations. The operators $A$ and $B$ are assumed to be skew-adjoint, which is typical for quantum evolution problems.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate that the theoretically derived error bounds closely match the actual computed errors for small truncation orders ($m=2, 3$), confirming the accuracy of the established bounds.

Paper structure. The paper systematically develops the general theory by first establishing the framework using differential equations, then deriving specific, increasingly complex error bounds for both the BCH and Zassenhaus formulas in terms of nested commutators.

Abstract

The Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff (BCH) formula plays a critical role in many branches of mathematics and physics. It expresses the logarithm of the product of exponentials of non-commuting operators as an infinite series of nested commutators of the operators involved. The Zassenhaus formula is the dual of the BCH formula: the exponential of a sum of operators is written as an infinite product of exponentials involving the operators and their commutators. In practical computations, however, one typically has to truncate the expansions, and so understanding the error committed by the resulting approximations and eventually providing suitable bounds for this error is of paramount interest. In this work we present a general strategy to derive rigorous error bounds and explicit error constants for the BCH and Zassenhaus formulas when the operators involved are skew-adjoint, as is the case for quantum evolution problems.

Summary. This paper introduces Hybrid Quantum Neighborhood Selection (HQNS) to tackle large combinatorial optimization problems formulated as dense QUBOs. By decomposing the problem into small, manageable quantum subproblems via frontier selection, HQNS significantly reduces computational resource demands compared to classical methods. This demonstrates a practical, resource-aware pathway for utilizing near-term quantum hardware in complex scientific simulations.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, resource-efficient architectural blueprint for integrating quantum processors into complex optimization pipelines, which is highly relevant for developing algorithms applicable to molecular simulation or material design using near-term hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Solving large-scale combinatorial optimization problems, specifically those formulated as dense QUBO instances like Maximum Diversity Subset Selection.

Main result. The proposed HQNS framework achieves near-baseline solution quality while drastically reducing computational resource consumption (time, CPU, memory) compared to strong classical baselines on large problem sizes ($N=1000$).

Method. HQNS decomposes the large QUBO instance into smaller, bounded-width quantum subproblems by selecting a stochastic 'frontier' of active variables for local optimization on the QPU.

Model / system. The problem is modeled using QUBO formulations derived from pairwise similarity matrices. The context is Near-Term Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) processors, which struggle with monolithic encoding of large interactions.

Key observables. Diversity score retention (99.9908% vs baseline), wall-clock time reduction (94.91%), peak CPU utilization reduction (64.68%), and QPU execution time stability ($\sim 6-7$ seconds).

Important parameters / regimes. Problem size $N$ up to 1000; Frontier size $F \ll N$; QUBO formulation complexity.

Assumptions / limitations. The quantum component's workload is bounded by the fixed frontier size ($F$), decoupling it from the global dimension ($N$) for practical NISQ deployment.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the computational bottleneck of large QUBO problems, introduces HQNS as a resource-aware hybrid solution, evaluates its performance against state-of-the-art classical solvers (SA), and concludes by demonstrating its practical utility for NISQ hardware.

Abstract

Large-scale combinatorial optimization remains demanding for classical heuristics, particularly when dense Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) formulations induce large memory footprints, high CPU utilization, and long execution times. While near-term quantum processors cannot yet deliver unconditional quantum advantage, hybrid architectures can provide practical value by reducing the resource burden. This paper presents a resource-efficiency study of Hybrid Quantum Neighborhood Selection (HQNS), a framework that decomposes large dense QUBO instances into bounded-width quantum subproblems via stochastic frontier selection. We evaluate HQNS on the Maximum Diversity Subset Selection Problem (MDSSP), focusing on the trade-off between solution quality retention and resource consumption. Benchmarks up to N=1000 candidates show that HQNS preserves 99.9908% of the mean diversity score of an 11-restart parallel Simulated Annealing baseline, while reducing wall-clock time by 94.91%, peak CPU utilization by 64.68%, and peak memory usage by 88.61%. The QPU execution time remains bounded within a 6-7 second envelope across scales, indicating that the quantum component is decoupled from the global QUBO dimension when the frontier size is fixed. These results suggest that HQNS provides a resource-aware pathway for deploying hybrid quantum optimization in practical large-scale settings, serving as an efficient architecture for incorporating near-term quantum processors into classical optimization pipelines.

Summary. This paper introduces spectral Born machines, a class of quantum generative models designed specifically for learning discrete, integer-structured data. By leveraging the Quantum Fourier Transform over $\mathbb{Z}_n^d$, these models gain an inductive bias that is both powerful and classically trainable via MMD loss minimization. The work demonstrates scalability by successfully training large models on complex biological sequences like rRNA.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on using group theory (Fourier analysis) to impose structure on quantum models is highly relevant for building physically motivated and efficiently trainable quantum circuits beyond simple binary encoding schemes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing quantum generative models capable of learning complex, discrete data structures (like integers) while remaining classically trainable for practical implementation.

Main result. The spectral Born machine provides a general framework that uses group Fourier analysis to create an inductive bias suitable for integer-structured data, enabling scalable training on classical hardware.

Method. Training is achieved using the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) loss derived from graph spectral analysis, which allows efficient estimation of quantum expectation values via classical sampling techniques.

Model / system. The model generalizes IQP Born machines by employing the Quantum Fourier Transform over $\mathbb{Z}_n^d$. It utilizes parameterized unitary gates constructed from generalized Pauli/Heisenberg-Weyl observables acting on qudits.

Key observables. Expectation values of Heisenberg-Weyl observables, $\langle D(k, m) \rangle_{\theta}$, and the Mean Maximum Discrepancy (MMD) loss function used for training.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension $d$ and length $n$ of the discrete group $\mathbb{Z}_d^n$; model size up to 190 qubits with over 1 million parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. Classical trainability relies on the expectation values of specific observables being estimable via classical algorithms, and efficient computation of the phase function $\Phi_{\theta}(z)$ is assumed.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of a spectral Born machine, showing its construction using diagonal parameterized unitaries sandwiched between QFT layers for both classical training visualization and quantum deployment.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the model by generalizing IQP Born machines via group Fourier analysis. It then details the mathematical framework, including generalized observables (HW) and the explicit formulas for calculating expectation values classically. Finally, it focuses on developing efficient, batched estimators for these expectations and the MMD loss.

Abstract

We present \emph{spectral Born machines}, a class of quantum generative models that results from viewing and generalizing the class of IQP Born machines through the lens of group Fourier analysis. These quantum models exploit the quantum Fourier transform to create an inductive bias that make them naturally suited to learning integer-structured data, while remaining classically hard to sample from in general. Similar to IQP Born machines, spectral Born machines can be trained efficiently at scale on classical hardware via a maximum mean discrepancy loss based on graph spectral analysis, which we make available in a new \emph{tcdq} module of the PennyLane software platform. In numerical experiments, we show how the spectral bias of the model leads to significantly reduced parameter counts compared to unstructured approaches, and demonstrate the scalability of the software by training a 190-qubit model with over 1 million parameters to successfully learn a distribution of 93 nucleotide-long ribosomal RNA. Our results suggest that highly over-parameterized spectral Born machines may be immune to overfitting, even in strongly data-scarce regimes.

Summary. This paper analyzes the difficulty of approximating the quantum value for a specific class of nonlocal games called tilted XOR games. By reducing known hard computational problems like E3-LIN and the halting problem to these games, the authors prove that approximation is RE-complete. This result establishes a deep link between quantum information theory and high levels of computational complexity.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects fundamental concepts in computational complexity theory (RE-completeness) directly to the physical limitations of quantum correlations, providing theoretical boundaries on what can be efficiently verified or simulated using quantum resources.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining the computational complexity of approximating the quantum value for a generalized class of nonlocal games called 'tilted XOR games.'

Main result. The authors prove that approximating the quantum value of tilted XOR games to constant precision is RE-complete, implying that general binary nonlocal games are hard to approximate.

Method. The hardness result is established via a polynomial-time reduction from E3-LIN games (linear constraint systems) and later extended using reductions involving the halting problem for tracial strategies.

Model / system. The study focuses on quantum nonlocal games, specifically 'tilted XOR games,' which are binary games whose winning condition is a slight variation of simple XOR constraints. The analysis extends to quantum tracial strategies ($\omega^*_{tr}(G)$).

Key observables. The primary observable is the quantum value (or approximation thereof) of the game, $\omega^*(G)$ or $\omega^*_{tr}(G)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The complexity depends on parameters like completeness probability ($c$) and soundness probability ($s$), which define the decision problem thresholds.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on reductions from known hard problems (E3-LIN, halting problem) and utilizes characterizations of optimal strategies for XOR games.

Figures summary. Figure 1 summarizes complexity regions for classical and quantum values ($ ext{TXOR}{c,s}$ vs $ ext{TXOR}^*$), highlighting a red triangular region labeled RE indicating the hardness boundary.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general RE-completeness result for tilted XOR games. It then refines this analysis using specific parameters ($c, s$) and extends the hardness proof to quantum tracial strategies ($\omega^*_{tr}(G)$) via a reduction from the halting problem.

Abstract

It is well known that the quantum value of an XOR nonlocal game, where the winning condition depends only on the XOR of the two players' output bits, may be approximated in polynomial time. We study a variant of the XOR game model, which we call tilted XOR games, where the winning condition can additionally depend on only one of the output bits. We show that this dramatically increases the expressive power: the computational complexity of the problem of approximating the quantum value of tilted XOR games to constant precision is RE-complete. Also, our result extends to succinct versions of tilted XOR games, where the questions can be polynomial-length binary strings, generated by a polynomial-time verifier. For classical strategies, the distinction between XOR games and tilted XOR games is inconsequential. Håstad (J. ACM, 2001) shows that they are both NP-complete to approximate, by using a reduction from linear systems to XOR games. Our approach is to show that this is also quantum-sound, but as a reduction from linear system games to tilted XOR games. Since titled XOR games are a special case of binary games (where each party outputs a single bit), our result implies that binary games are RE-hard to approximate.
