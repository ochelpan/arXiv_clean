Summary. The paper presents a method to drastically reduce the computational resources needed to solve linear differential equations using a one-ancilla quantum computer. By integrating classical extrapolation techniques with the quantum solver, the required circuit depth scales logarithmically with the inverse of the target accuracy. This breakthrough makes simulating complex physical dynamics, especially non-unitary ones, much more feasible for near-term quantum hardware.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles resource optimization for quantum simulation algorithms, which is critical for realizing fault-tolerant quantum computation by reducing required circuit depth for solving fundamental physical equations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to reduce the circuit depth required for a one-ancilla quantum solver of linear differential equations.

Main result. By combining the quantum solver with classical step-size postprocessing techniques like extrapolation, the circuit depth scales down from $\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)$ to $\mathcal{O}( ext{polylog}(1/\epsilon))$.

Method. The approach uses Richardson or Chebyshev extrapolation on estimates derived from running the quantum solver at a logarithmic number of finite time steps. This requires advanced mathematical tools like holomorphic adjoint lifts for non-unitary dynamics.

Model / system. The work addresses solving linear differential equations, including dissipative and non-unitary systems modeled by operators like $A = -iH - \sum D_j$. Examples include the Hatano-Nelson model (ODE) and convection-diffusion PDEs.

Key observables. The observable estimated is an unnormalized expectation value $\langle\psi(T)| O|\psi(T) angle$, specifically measuring low-Fourier-shell content for PDE examples.

Important parameters / regimes. Target accuracy ($\epsilon$) and the required circuit depth (the resource being minimized).

Assumptions / limitations. The reduction relies on obtaining necessary regularity estimates through a holomorphic adjoint lift, which is crucial for non-unitary evolution maps. The one-step block underlying the algorithm is assumed to be first-order accurate in step size.

Figures summary. Figures compare resource scaling: direct simulation requires depth $\sim 1/\epsilon$, while extrapolation methods achieve much smaller depths ($ ext{polylog}(1/\epsilon)$), demonstrating superior convergence rates for accuracy improvement.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, details the one-ancilla solver structure, develops the theoretical framework using classical postprocessing (extrapolation) to mitigate discretization errors, and validates the improved scaling with numerical examples on ODEs and PDEs.

Abstract

Solving linear differential equations is a fundamental task in scientific computing and an important primitive for quantum computing. A recent one-ancilla quantum differential equation solver provides a hardware-friendly and locality-preserving approach with provable performance guarantees, making it highly suitable for the early fault-tolerant and near-term regimes. Its simple circuit structure comes with a natural trade-off: the maximum single-run circuit depth scales as $O (1/ε)$ in the target accuracy $ε$. In this work, we reduce this depth by combining the solver with classical step-size postprocessing. By running the one-ancilla solver at a logarithmic number of finite time step sizes and using classical post-processing to cancel leading discretization errors, we reduce the maximum single-run circuit depth to $O(\mathrm{polylog}(1/ε))$ without adding quantum ancillae or sacrificing locality. Technically, extending extrapolation ideas beyond Hamiltonian and Lindbladian dynamics requires regularity estimates for observable maps under nonunitary evolution, which we obtain through a holomorphic extension of the adjoint evolution. Numerical experiments on the Hatano-Nelson model (ODE) and the convection-diffusion equation (PDE) demonstrate the effectiveness of the approach.

Summary. This paper develops an operator-based formulation of quantum geometry designed specifically for non-compact bosonic systems undergoing vacuum instability. Model / system. The work applies to non-compact bosonic quantum systems, initially focusing on a single mode but extending to coupled systems using Feshbach projection techniques. The geometry is analyzed in terms of operator commutation relations rather than state expectation values.

Abstract

We formulate the geometric structure of non-compact bosonic quantum systems in regimes where vacuum instability renders the relevant quantum states non-normalizable, causing conventional state-space quantum geometry -- described by the Berry connection, curvature, and quantum metric -- to become ill-defined. To overcome this breakdown, we develop a formulation of quantum geometry at the level of canonical operator frames, allowing for complexified Bogoliubov-Valatin transformations that lift the requirement that creation operators be Hermitian conjugates of annihilation operators. Canonical operator frames are defined as choices of bosonic creation and annihilation operators realizing the canonical commutation relations. A natural equivalence relation among such frames generalizes the phase ambiguity of quantum states and determines a parameter space that analytically extends the stable-regime parameter space. The space of canonical operator frames forms a principal bundle over parameter space -- the operator-frame bundle -- equipped with a natural Ehresmann connection that defines parallel transport while preserving the canonical commutation relations. In the stable regime, this construction reduces to the Berry connection, while more generally it yields a well-defined operator-space quantum geometric tensor (QGT) that remains valid across vacuum instabilities. Using the framework of rigged Hilbert spaces, we define a notion of quantum frame vacuum and obtain a consistent state-space QGT. Focusing on a single bosonic mode, we demonstrate analyticity of the QGT across the quantum frame-vacuum phase transition and present the corresponding phase diagram on the complexified squeezing-parameter plane. We further introduce a realistic physical setting that allows continuous paths connecting stable and unstable regimes, along which the QGT evolves smoothly across Stokes lines.

Summary. This paper develops the Bayes-optimal RMSD (BRMSD) metric to overcome the limitations of standard structural comparison metrics for biomolecules. By optimizing per-atom weights using a Bayesian framework, BRMSD effectively down-weights highly flexible regions and concentrates analysis power on the stable, rigid functional core of proteins. This provides robust tools for alignment, clustering, and domain identification in molecular dynamics simulations.

Why it may be interesting. While primarily applied to biophysics, the underlying mathematical machinery—optimizing weights based on local fluctuation statistics via free energy minimization and entropy regularization—shares conceptual links with statistical mechanics approaches used in analyzing complex many-body systems or disordered materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Standard RMSD fails for biomolecules because it weights all atoms equally, allowing flexible regions to dominate the metric while ignoring rigid functional cores.

Main result. The proposed Bayes-optimal RMSD (BRMSD) significantly reduces structural deviation metrics compared to standard RMSD on benchmark systems like SND3. It successfully identifies and focuses analysis on the stable, rigid core of proteins.

Method. The authors introduce a Bayesian framework that optimizes per-atom weights by maximizing a posterior derived from minimizing an effective free energy functional combining Mean-Square Deviation (MSD) and relative entropy regularization.

Model / system. The methods are applied to biomolecules studied via Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations, specifically benchmarking on the SND3 translocon protein and adenylate kinase. The framework is designed for structural alignment, clustering, and domain identification within these systems.

Key observables. BRMSD value, per-atom weights ($w_\alpha$), cluster responsibilities ($q(a|i)$), and the Calinski–Harabasz (CH) index for clustering quality.

Important parameters / regimes. The position-fluctuation parameter $\sigma$ controls the weight suppression threshold; parameters like $\mu/ heta$ control domain focusing strength, and window size $W_{op}$ is used for trajectory smoothing.

Assumptions / limitations. Minimizing the free energy functional $G$ may only yield a local minimum, necessitating multiple random restarts. The method assumes that weight optimization can effectively separate rigid core contributions from flexible noise.

Figures summary. Figures show BRMSD significantly lower than standard RMSD over time; they also illustrate the anticorrelation between high-RMSF residues and low optimized weights, confirming domain identification via weight concentration.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the BRMSD framework by defining the free energy functional $G$. It then details specialized modules for focused alignment (adding a concentration penalty), trajectory smoothing (using windowed sums), soft K-means clustering (introducing responsibilities), and rigid domain identification (via sequential peeling).

Abstract

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) is the standard metric of structural comparison in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. In its conventional form, RMSD assigns equal weight to all atoms regardless of mobility. Hence, flexible loops and disordered regions can dominate a global RMSD, while the rigid functional core contributes negligibly to the overall metric. To address this issue, we introduce the Bayes-optimal RMSD (BRMSD), which optimizes per-atom weights jointly with structural averages by maximizing a Bayesian posterior. In a trade-off between low RMSD and weight uniformity, a position-fluctuation parameter $σ$ controls the transition from classical RMSD ($σ\to \infty$) to a progressive focus on a rigid core ($σ\to 0$). The BRMSD framework supports analysis modules for structural alignment, focused alignment onto a user-specified domain, trajectory smoothing, soft $K$-means conformational clustering, and rigid-domain identification. These modules are implemented in the open-source Python package BRMSD (https://github.com/bio-phys/BRMSD) and benchmarked on two MD systems, the endoplasmic reticulum translocon-associated protein SND3 and the phosphotransferase adenylate kinase.

Abstract

The spectral condition number is a widely adopted measure of worst-case cost for quantum linear system solvers. Yet it can significantly overestimate the actual runtime for a typical problem instance. We present two quantum algorithms that produce the normalized solution $|x\rangle$ of linear system $Ax=| b \rangle$ to accuracy $ε$ with complexity independent of the condition number $κ=\lVert A^{-1}\rVert$. We focus on the standard input model where $A$ is accessed through a block encoding and $| b \rangle$ is prepared by a unitary. But we also introduce an affine dilation model that encodes $A$ and $| b \rangle$ jointly, allowing further refinements of the query complexity. Our truncation-based solver makes an optimal number of queries to $| b \rangle$ and $\operatorname{\mathbf{O}}\left(κ_{\mathrm{eff}}\operatorname{polylog}\left(\frac{κ_{\mathrm{eff}}}ε\right)\right)$ queries to $A$. We prove a family of upper bounds on the effective condition number, including $κ_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq\frac{\lVert(A^\dagger A)^{-t/2}|x\rangle\rVert^{1/t}}{ε^{1/t}}$ for positive even integer $t$ and $κ_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq\frac{\lVert A^{-1\dagger}(A^\dagger A)^{-(t-1)/2}|x\rangle\rVert^{1/t}}{ε^{1/t}}$ for positive odd $t$, overcoming the $κ$-barrier. Our filtering-based solver is extremely simple with a favorable runtime prefactor. In particular, the solver has query complexity $6\frac{\lVert A^{-1\dagger}|x\rangle\rVert}ε\ln\left(\frac{1}ε\right)$ to leading order when the solution norm is known. We then present a similarly simple solution norm estimator with the same asymptotic cost up to logarithmic factors. Our quantum linear system solvers thus substantially improve a recent algorithm of Li, enabling faster quantum linear system solving beyond the condition number.

Abstract

Abstract

A major difficulty arises from the time reparameterization invariance of time series data, which complicates the extraction of meaningful temporal features.
