Summary. This paper provides a deep algebraic characterization of Tree Tensor Network (TTN) varieties, proving they are defined by minors of matrix flattenings and are equivalent to general Markov models. Furthermore, it develops a combinatorial method to compute the degree of these varieties, which is a crucial invariant for understanding the complexity of quantum states represented by tensor networks.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between tensor network representations (used extensively in simulating strongly correlated electron models) and algebraic geometry provides powerful, rigorous tools for analyzing the complexity and structure of quantum states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to characterize the algebraic structure, specifically the prime ideal, of tree tensor network varieties ($ ext{TTN}_{T,V,r}$). It also focuses on computing the degree of these varieties for specific cases like order 3 tensor trains.

Main result. The prime ideal of the TTN variety is generated by the minors of matrix flattenings, and the variety is shown to be equivalent to a general Markov model on the associated spaced tree. A combinatorial method is provided to compute the degree for order 3 tensor trains.

Method. The authors employ algebraic geometry, viewing the variety as a projective algebraic variety defined by rank constraints on matrix flattenings. They use techniques involving initial ideals and non-intersecting path counting to determine the degree.

Model / system. The core model is the Tree Tensor Network (TTN), which is a mathematical tool used to represent high-order tensors. These networks are relevant in quantum many-body physics, particularly for describing 2D quantum systems, and are related to tensor train and subspace decompositions.

Key observables. The prime ideal generators (minors of matrix flattenings) and the degree of the variety are key algebraic observables.

Important parameters / regimes. The structure of the rooted tree $T$, the attached vector spaces $V$, and the admissible rank sequence $r = (r_v)$ are the critical parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on relating the TTN variety to general Markov models and utilizing results from the theory of determinantal and initial ideals for degree computation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the structure of TTNs (e.g., binary TTN of order 4) and show how the TTN structure realizes both TT and Subspace decompositions for order 3.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the algebraic characterization of the TTN variety using minors (Theorem 1.1). It then proves the equivalence between TTN, TT, and Subspace varieties (Proposition 2.5). Finally, it focuses on computing the degree of the TT variety using combinatorial methods based on initial ideals (Proposition 6.7).

Abstract

We show that tree tensor network varieties, including tensor train varieties, are general Markov models associated to spaced trees. This allows us to prove that the prime ideals of these varieties are generated by minors of matrix flattenings. In the case of tensor train varieties, we discuss whether these minors form a Gröbner basis and provide a combinatorial method to compute the degree for order $3$ tensor trains.

Summary. This work characterizes the electronic and excitonic properties of AgCrP2S6, a magnetic semiconductor. By combining structural measurements with advanced many-body calculations, the authors determine that the low-energy optical excitation is a localized Frenkel exciton. This highlights the delicate balance between covalent bonding and magnetic interactions in determining optical response in correlated materials.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed analysis of exciton localization and the explicit calculation of exciton-phonon coupling channels provide a rich model for understanding how lattice vibrations modulate electronic excitations in strongly correlated magnetic insulators.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the competition between covalency, magnetic localization, and structural symmetry in determining the nature of the lowest optical exciton in the antiferromagnetic semiconductor AgCrP2S6.

Main result. The lowest exciton is found to be predominantly a localized Frenkel exciton, despite increased covalency, due to the combined effects of antiferromagnetism and Ag-site disorder.

Method. The research combines single-crystal X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, lattice-dynamical calculations, and advanced many-body theory (vertex corrected Feynman diagrammatic approaches).

Model / system. The system is the layered thiophosphate semiconductor AgCrP2S6, which features an antiferromagnetic Cr sublattice and nonmagnetic Ag sites, allowing for the study of competing electronic interactions.

Key observables. Lowest exciton energy (~1.4 eV), exciton character (Frenkel vs. Wannier-Mott), exciton-phonon coupling channels, and quasiparticle gap.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature range (100-300 K), exciton binding energy (~770 meV), and the relative strength of p-d hybridization versus intersite coherence.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the material remains dynamically stable in its monoclinic phase and that the low-energy optical response is dominated by $q=0$ excitations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the crystal structure, band structure along high-symmetry paths, and detailed analysis of the exciton character, real-space hole density, and phonon dispersion/Raman spectra.

Paper structure. The paper follows a structure moving from structural characterization (XRD, lattice dynamics) to electronic structure (quasiparticle gap) and finally to the detailed excitonic and exciton-phonon coupling analysis using advanced many-body techniques.

Abstract

AgCrP2S6 combines a low-symmetry thiophosphate framework with an antiferromagnetic Cr sublattice and nonmagnetic Ag sites, providing a setting in which covalency and magnetic localization compete in the low-energy optical response. We combine single-crystal x-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, lattice-dynamical calculations, and self-consistent vertex corrected Feynman diagrammatic many-body approaches to determine the structural, electronic, and excitonic properties of bulk AgCrP2S6. The material remains monoclinic between 100 and 300 K, with additional Ag-site disorder resolved at low temperature, and the optimized structure is dynamically stable. The many-body calculations yield a reduced quasiparticle gap relative to Cr trihalides, but the lowest excitons remain predominantly local: the calculated low-energy (~1.4 eV) excitation cluster is best described as a weakly bright, strongly anisotropic Frenkel exciton with dominant onsite d-d character and substantial ligand-assisted d-p admixture. Symmetry analysis in the C2h setting identifies this state as predominantly Bu and constrains its leading exciton-phonon coupling channels to Ag diagonal renormalization and Bg-mediated bright-dark mixing. These results place AgCrP2S6 in a localized excitonic regime in which the stronger p-d hybridization, relative to the more ionic Cr trihalides, narrows the quasiparticle gap, while the antiferromagnetic exchange and Ag-site dilution disfavor the intersite coherence associated with strongly delocalized low-energy excitons.

It overcomes the rigidity of existing methods by enabling dynamic reconfiguration of the quantum lattice in real-time. This results in significantly improved resource efficiency, allowing complex quantum algorithms to run on much smaller and faster physical quantum chips.

Method. The authors propose a hardware-compiler co-design called RushHour, which formalizes dynamic lattice management via a new ISA and a Lattice Management Unit (LMU).

Model / system. The system models fault-tolerant quantum computation using the surface code, where logical operations are performed via dynamic lattice surgery (LS) on patches of physical qubits.

Key observables. Space-time trade-off, benchmark success rate on small chips, execution time (speedup factor), and resource footprint.

Important parameters / regimes. Chip size constraints, logical error exposure, and the efficiency of dynamic reconfiguration (achieved in a single round).

Abstract

Practical fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) requires efficient lattice surgery (LS), so that large algorithms fit on resource-constrained quantum chips. Existing approaches, however, are rigid: qubits, routing space, and resource states are allocated ahead of execution, which prevents running on small chips, leaves statically scheduled executions with large time overheads, and fixes each design at a single area of the space-time trade-off. We present dynamic LS, which enables efficient reconfiguration of the ancilla space, just-in-time allocation of resource states, and dynamic rotations of logical qubits, thereby spanning the entire space-time trade-off with a single, unified approach. We realize dynamic LS with RushHour through a hardware-compiler co-design: the RushHour ISA formalizes and programs our dynamic lattice model, the Lattice Management Unit abstracts dynamic lattice management and performs efficient lattice reconfiguration, and the RushHour Compiler compiles logical circuits for physical chips into optimized ISA programs while pipelining instructions. We evaluate RushHour against six state-of-the-art compilers and two resource models. On the smallest chips, 86% of benchmarks run only with RushHour, while existing approaches require 1.2-3.5$\times$ larger chips. On space-constrained early-FTQC chips, RushHour runs a median 2.0-7.2$\times$ faster than the best feasible alternative, while achieving results comparable to the state of the art on very large chips. RushHour's constructive results run 4.8$\times$ from an idealized-machine resource limit.

Summary. This work demonstrates that for ML models predicting material properties, the inclusion of parity labels in the model's features is the single design element that dictates whether the model respects exact physical symmetries. Models lacking this label frequently predict physically impossible, non-zero values for properties that must vanish due to crystal symmetry.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter ML, the rigorous mathematical framework for enforcing exact physical laws (like zero tensors) through structural design choices is highly relevant to building reliable quantum simulation models.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses whether machine learning models designed for materials can predict physically impossible properties due to insufficient incorporation of exact symmetry constraints.

Main result. The ability to enforce exact symmetry constraints (like zero for centrosymmetric materials) is determined solely by a single design choice: whether the model's features are explicitly labeled with parity information.

Method. The authors derive the 'parity gap' from group theory to quantify the difference between approximate (rotational) and exact symmetry constraints, testing this using ML models trained on materials data.

Model / system. The system is crystalline materials, and the models are machine-learned surrogates for first-principles calculations, specifically tested on properties like the piezoelectric tensor.

Key observables. The predicted magnitude of the piezoelectric tensor, and the 'false-flag fraction' (percentage of predictions exceeding a physical threshold when the true value is zero).

Important parameters / regimes. The parity label (a binary design choice), the symmetry group ($ ext{E}(3)$ vs $ ext{SO}(3)$), and the physical threshold ($ au = 0.01 ext{ C m}^{-2}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the validity of permutation and translation invariance, and crucially, that the parity label is an 'upstream' design choice.

Figures summary. Figures compare the predictions of parity-labelled ($ ext{O}(3)$) vs. parity-blind ($ ext{SO}(3)$) models for the piezoelectric tensor, showing a separation of six orders of magnitude, with the $ ext{SO}(3)$ model frequently predicting forbidden, non-zero responses.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical distinction between approximate and exact symmetry constraints, derives the parity gap criterion, and then empirically demonstrates this gap by comparing the performance of ML architectures that either incorporate or ignore parity labels when predicting physical tensors.

Abstract

Machine-learned models are replacing first-principles calculations across materials discovery, and physical symmetry is the central guarantee built into them. The debate over how much symmetry to hard-wire rather than learn has run on rotations, where a symmetry error is an approximation error. Some constraints are exact: symmetry forces certain property tensors to exactly zero, so a nonzero prediction is physically impossible rather than inaccurate. Here we show that whether a model can make such predictions is decided before training by one rarely reported design bit, whether its features carry parity labels, and derive a criterion, the parity gap, that computes from group theory alone which properties and crystals are exposed. Across matched architecture pairs differing only in that bit, evaluated on two thousand centrosymmetric crystals whose piezoelectric tensor must vanish, parity-labelled arms sit at the floating-point floor while rotation-only arms predict forbidden responses on 90-96% of crystals, six orders of magnitude apart, at no accuracy cost. Training on explicit zeros does not recover exactness, and a head on a frozen universal potential inherits its backbone's symmetry group. One reflection at random initialization verifies the label in seconds.

Summary. This paper establishes a fundamental separation between entanglement and 'magic' by quantifying how much entanglement remains visible when measurements are restricted to stabilizer operations. Through explicit constructions, the authors show that entanglement can be asymptotically hidden, meaning the distillable entanglement can be large while the stabilizer-visible entanglement vanishes. This reveals intrinsic limitations on entanglement extraction using only stabilizer operations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, quantitative measure of how much quantum correlation is 'hidden' from computation protocols relying only on stabilizer measurements, which is crucial for understanding the limits of fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the fundamental operational difference between entanglement visible under general measurements (LOCC) versus entanglement visible only through stabilizer measurements.

Main result. They demonstrate 'magic-free asymptotic entanglement hiding,' showing that highly entangled states can possess arbitrarily large LOCC-distillable entanglement while having vanishing entanglement visible under stabilizer restrictions.

Method. The analysis involves constructing explicit convex mixtures of stabilizer states and applying bounds derived from the relative entropy and Wigner function analysis to quantify the gap between different entanglement measures.

Model / system. The study uses bipartite quantum states, specifically focusing on $N$ qutrits per party, Haar-random pure states, and Werner states, all analyzed within the framework of stabilizer operations.

Key observables. Stabilizer-visible entanglement ($ ext{ESTAB}$), Unrestricted visible entanglement ($ ext{EALL}$), and LOCC-distillable entanglement ($ED_{LOCC}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The number of qutrits per party ($N$), the local dimension ($p$, an odd prime), and the state parameter ($t$ for Werner states).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is the restriction of measurements to the set of stabilizer operations, which defines the 'magic' resource theory.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the entanglement hiding mechanism by comparing the unrestricted visible entanglement (solid curve) to the stabilizer-restricted bound (dashed curve) for an interpolated state.

Paper structure. The paper builds by first defining the measures, then proving the gap for specific states (Werner states, mixed stabilizer states), and finally establishing the asymptotic scaling laws for general mixed stabilizer states.

Abstract

Entanglement is central to quantum information processing, while stabilizer operations underpin fault-tolerant quantum computation. We ask how much entanglement remains visible or distillable under stabilizer restrictions. We quantify stabilizer-visible entanglement by restricting the measured relative entropy of entanglement to stabilizer measurements, thereby obtaining converse bounds on entanglement distillation under stabilizer operations. We demonstrate magic-free asymptotic entanglement hiding: we construct explicit convex mixtures of pure stabilizer states on $N$ qutrits per party whose unrestricted visible entanglement and LOCC-distillable entanglement both grow as $Ω(N/\log N)$, while their stabilizer-visible and stabilizer-distillable entanglement vanish as $N\to\infty$. Thus, an unbounded amount of LOCC-distillable entanglement carried by stabilizer states can become asymptotically invisible and undistillable under stabilizer restrictions. We further prove that stabilizer-visible entanglement is $O(1)$ with high probability for Haar-random pure states despite extensive unrestricted visibility, and vanishes uniformly over entangled Werner states as the local dimension grows through odd primes. These results reveal a fundamental separation between entanglement and magic as resources, exposing intrinsic limits on entanglement extraction using stabilizer operations.

Summary. This paper extends Pauli Check Sandwiching (PCS) from qubits to qudits of arbitrary dimension $d$ for quantum error detection and mitigation. It develops generalized checks using the Heisenberg-Weyl set, proving that these checks can deterministically identify arbitrary qudit errors. The results validate the technique numerically, showing high fidelity preservation even under realistic error rates.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly advances the theoretical toolkit for fault-tolerant quantum computation by extending established error correction paradigms to higher-dimensional quantum systems (qudits), which are relevant for many physical implementations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper generalizes the Pauli Check Sandwiching (PCS) technique, traditionally used for qubit error detection and mitigation, to arbitrary qudit dimensions.

Main result. The generalized PCS protocol successfully detects arbitrary Heisenberg-Weyl errors in qudit systems, achieving unit fidelity in the noiseless check limit.

Method. It introduces generalized left and right Pauli checks using the Heisenberg-Weyl operator set, relying on commutation relations and Fourier orthogonality for error syndrome extraction.

Model / system. The model involves qudit systems of arbitrary dimension $d$, where error detection is performed by sandwiching the computation between controlled Pauli checks.

Key observables. Error-mitigated fidelity (validated above 97.5% for $d=2$ to $d=9$), and the deterministic mapping of an error to a unique ancilla readout.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension $d$ of the qudit space, and the depolarizing error rate $p$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the error channel can be decomposed into the Pauli (or Heisenberg-Weyl) basis, and that the checks themselves are noiseless.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase kickback in qubits and the general PCS circuit layout for both qubit and qudit systems, showing the structure of the error checks.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the generalization of PCS to qudits using the Heisenberg-Weyl set, mathematically proves the detection capability via commutation relations, and concludes with numerical validation on specific circuits (Mod-2 and GHZ-2) across various dimensions.

Abstract

Pauli Check Sandwiching (PCS) is a quantum error detection (QED) technique that protects a quantum circuit by utilizing a pair of controlled Pauli operators, or checks, and detecting errors that anti-commute with the checks. Further, PCS can be used for quantum error mitigation (QEM) via post-selection based on the Pauli check syndrome values. Currently, PCS is leveraged in the qubit space. In this paper, we introduce a generalized approach for applying PCS-based QED and QEM to quantum information of arbitrary dimension in the Hilbert space. Each pair of these extended checks consists of a sequence of gates in the Heisenberg-Weyl operator set that extend Pauli operators into the qudit space. These qudit checks use at least one ancilla qudit to detect qudit errors that do not commute with the unitary selected for the check. We show that our proposed methods for qudit QED can detect errors of arbitrary dimensions. More specifically, we prove that an arbitrary Heisenberg-Weyl error maps deterministically to a unique ancilla readout, and further, post-selecting on the $|0 \rangle$ readout guarantees unit fidelity in the noiseless check limit. We validate these findings numerically across dimensions $d=2$ through $d=9$, achieving error-mitigated fidelities above $97.5\% $ under realistic depolarizing error rates.

Abstract

Many rigorous results in the modern era of statistical mechanics have been obtained through geometrical representations. A much studied tool in this setting is the random cluster representation of lattice spin models. Another area of statistical mechanics that is of great interest but lacking rigorous results is the theory of the renormalization group. This paper investigates the idea to find a common ground between these two concepts in order to obtain rigorous results on the renormalization group flow. Explicit results will be established on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ followed by a discussion of the available generalizations to higher dimensions.

Abstract

For certain Abelian TOs, decoding can be performed by independently matching particle-antiparticle pairs of each species. Abstract

Hard combinatorial optimization problems, many of which are NP-hard, present fundamental algorithmic challenges. Average-case analysis on random instances has emerged as a powerful framework for understanding typical algorithmic performance beyond worst-case guarantees.
