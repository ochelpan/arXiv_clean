Electromagnetic and Acoustic Fano Interference in Surface Acoustic Wave Resonators

Authors: P. K. Rath, J. M. Kitzman, M. Mesbah, C. Undershute, J. Pollanen

Summary. This paper investigates enhancing the sensitivity of SAW resonators by engineering their spectral response using Fano interference. The authors demonstrate the simultaneous control of both electromagnetic and acoustic Fano interference by modifying the resonator's acoustic confinement. This technique proves that spectral asymmetry can be tuned, offering a promising route for advanced classical and quantum sensing applications.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to engineer spectral features via controlled interference mechanisms (Fano resonance) provides a powerful, non-traditional method for enhancing sensor sensitivity, which is relevant for developing high-precision quantum transducers or detectors.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to enhance the sensitivity of Surface Acoustic Wave (SAW) resonators for sensing applications by engineering their spectral response.

Main result. The work successfully demonstrates the simultaneous introduction of both electromagnetic and acoustic Fano interference to shape the spectral response, confirming that Bragg mirror design acts as a tunable knob for spectral symmetry.

Method. The researchers measured the transmission spectrum ($S_{21}$) of SAW resonators across varying temperatures and systematically modified the acoustic confinement using different Bragg mirror configurations.

Model / system. The physical platform is a SAW resonator fabricated on Y-cut LiNbO3, operating at GHz frequencies. The analysis models the spectral response using the Fano lineshape function, which accounts for interference between resonant and continuum scattering pathways.

Key observables. Spectral line shape asymmetry, quantified by the Fano factor ($q$), resonance frequency ($f_0$), and the temperature dependence of these parameters.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature (ambient to $\sim 1 ext{ K}$), acoustic reflectivity (controlled by Bragg mirror pairs), and the Fano parameter $q$.

Assumptions / limitations. The interference effects can be separated by systematically modifying the acoustic confinement, allowing independent analysis of electromagnetic and acoustic contributions.

Figures summary. Figures show schematics of the interference setup, measured transmission spectra ($S_{21}$) exhibiting asymmetric dips, and plots detailing the temperature dependence of the Fano factor $|q(T)|$ and the resonance frequency.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the concept of using Fano interference to enhance SAW sensor sensitivity. It details the experimental setup using multiple devices with varying acoustic confinement. The core of the work involves fitting the measured spectral data to the Fano lineshape function to quantify the interplay between acoustic and electromagnetic interference, concluding with temperature-dependent analysis.

Abstract

Surface acoustic wave-based resonators are sensitive probes of condensed matter systems, as well as surface-selective sensors for chemistry and biology. Surface acoustic wave devices have also been integrated into hybrid quantum systems with qubit platforms for applications in quantum information processing and sensing. The sensitivity of piezoelectric surface wave-based resonators to investigate these various systems can be enhanced by optimizing the device architecture and electrical measurement techniques. Alternatively, tailoring the spectral symmetry, arising from interference effects, offers a promising route to further improve sensitivity. In this work, we demonstrate the simultaneous introduction of both electromagnetic and acoustic Fano interference to shape the spectral response of GHz-frequency surface acoustic wave resonators. By systematically modifying the acoustic reflectivity of the resonators, we are able to isolate and analyze each interference mechanism independently. The broad range of temperature operation, from ambient to cryogenic temperatures highlights the potential for both classical and quantum sensing applications.

Summary. The authors propose using patterned light fields as a tunable alternative to physical strain or twisting to create superlattices in bilayer graphene. By controlling the electromagnetic drive, they can independently modulate the electronic couplings, leading to the emergence of flat bands and measurable topological invariants like the Chern number. This establishes a highly flexible platform for realizing and studying moiré-like quantum phases.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the engineering of topological matter using external, tunable fields, which is highly relevant to open quantum systems and quantum optics approaches to condensed matter physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To find a flexible and tunable method to engineer flat bands and topological phases in bilayer graphene, bypassing the limitations of physically fixed strain or twisting.

Main result. Patterned electromagnetic fields allow for the independent and tunable control of intralayer and interlayer couplings, inducing isolated, nearly flat bands and rich topological structures characterized by calculable Chern numbers.

Method. The study employs Floquet engineering by driving the system with patterned light fields, analyzing the resulting quasienergy band structure and topological invariants.

Model / system. The system is untwisted bilayer graphene (AA- and AB-stacked) subjected to time-periodic driving from patterned in-plane and out-of-plane electromagnetic fields.

Key observables. Electronic band structure (minibands), quasienergy band structures, and valley Chern numbers.

Important parameters / regimes. Driving frequency ($\Omega$), superlattice period ($L$), and the relative strengths of the patterned fields ($a_0$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis uses the Floquet-Bloch formalism and assumes the driving frequency is high enough to prevent overlap of Floquet replicas.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the band structures (quasienergy plots) under various driving conditions (circularly polarized, longitudinal, combined) as a function of driving frequency and superlattice period, alongside phase diagrams showing Chern numbers.

Paper structure. The paper systematically introduces the problem, details the optical coupling mechanisms (intralayer vs. interlayer), and then presents results comparing the topological outcomes (Chern numbers) and band flattening achieved using different combinations of patterned light drives.

Abstract

Two-dimensional superlattices in van der Waals materials host flat bands and nontrivial topology, most famously at the so-called magic angles of twisted bilayer graphene, where flat bands give rise to correlated and topological phases. Yet these superlattices are usually created by twisting the layers or applying strain, and once a sample is fabricated, their period is fixed and extremely difficult to tune. Here we propose an alternative route: imprint the superlattice optically, using patterned electromagnetic fields rather than a physical twist or strain. We show that patterned in-plane cir- cularly polarized light and a combined drive consisting of a patterned out-of-plane longitudinal field and a uniform circularly polarized field produce isolated bands in both AA- and AB-stacked bilayer graphene. In AB stacking, the central bands additionally become nearly flat, capturing key features of a driven moir/'e superlattice. In this approach, the superlattice period is set by the illumination and is straightforward to tune, and circularly polarized light breaks time-reversal symmetry. Computing the valley Chern numbers of the central bands, we find a rich topological structure with several phase transitions in both stackings. Our results establish light-induced superlattices as a flexible and tunable platform for engineering flat bands and topological phases in bilayer graphene.

Main problem. The paper investigates how periodic driving using light can induce topological phase transitions and engineer topological insulating bands in semiconductor van der Waals heterobilayers.

Main result. The system exhibits non-trivial Floquet topological insulating phases, achieving Chern numbers up to $\pm 2$ and demonstrating chiral edge states indicative of robust topological protection.

Model / system. The physical system is based on commensurate type-II TMD heterobilayers (e.g., $ ext{WSe}_2/ ext{MoSe}_2$). The model uses an effective Hamiltonian derived from the lowest-order $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}$ theory, incorporating the interaction with an incident electromagnetic wave.

Important parameters / regimes. Light frequency ($\Omega$), electric-field amplitude ($E_0$), and polarization (LPL, CPL, RCPL). The analysis focuses on the near-resonant regime ($\hbar\Omega \gtrsim \Delta_0$).

Paper structure. The paper systematically develops the effective Hamiltonian using the Floquet formalism, calculates the Chern number as a function of driving parameters, and then analyzes the resulting topological phase diagrams and edge state properties for different stacking and polarization configurations.

Abstract

We show that periodic driving with near-infrared to visible light drives topological phase transitions in the photon-dressed band structure of transition-metal dichalcogenide heterobilayers. We apply the Floquet formalism to a light-coupled lowest-order $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}$ Hamiltonian, and obtain an effective four-band model that correctly captures the essential photon-dressed bands of type-II TMD heterobilayers in the vicinity of the first photon resonance. Crossings between the zero- and one-photon sectors effectively invert the bands, yielding topological phases with Chern numbers up to $\pm 2$ and gaps of order 10 meV. Our results establish TMDs as prime candidates for engineering bands with higher Chern numbers, and exploring driven topological states in solid state media.

Efficient calculation of real-space lattice propagators in the presence of a Fermi sea

Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18466v1

Summary. This paper presents an efficient, dimension-agnostic method to calculate real-space lattice Green's functions for doped tight-binding models. By leveraging Kramers-Kronig relations and FFTs, the authors bypass the computational bottleneck of direct Brillouin-zone integration. The results demonstrate that the spatial decay and structure of the propagator directly encode the geometry of the Fermi surface.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, dimension-independent computational tool for studying electronic structure in doped solids, which is highly relevant for understanding transport and correlation effects in materials science.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops an efficient theoretical method to calculate real-space lattice propagators (Green's functions) for a tight-binding model when the system is doped, i.e., in the presence of a Fermi sea.

Main result. The method achieves a computational cost independent of the lattice dimensionality by utilizing Kramers-Kronig relations evaluated via FFTs, allowing the mapping of Fermi surface geometry onto the spatial structure of the propagators.

Method. The core technique involves relating the finite-$x$ propagators to the $x=0$ case using known identities and then employing Kramers-Kronig relations, which are efficiently computed for all energies simultaneously using Fast Fourier Transforms.

Model / system. The model is a non-interacting, tight-binding Hamiltonian defined on an arbitrary lattice, characterized by a Fermi sea determined by the carrier concentration $x$. The calculation focuses on the particle-addition part of the lattice Green's function.

Key observables. Real-space lattice propagators ($G_n(z; x)$), the real and imaginary parts of the propagator, and the resulting diffraction patterns.

Important parameters / regimes. Carrier concentration ($x$), Fermi energy ($E_F$), lattice distance ($n$), and energy ($\omega$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method is valid for any lattice and dispersion, provided the $x=0$ propagators are known, and it is derived in the limit of zero broadening ($\eta o 0$).

Figures summary. Figures compare the proposed method against direct integration across 1D, 2D, and 3D lattices. Maps illustrate how the Fermi surface geometry (e.g., circular vs. square) imprints itself on the spatial structure of the propagator magnitude and sign.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, derives the necessary identities relating finite-$x$ propagators to $x=0$ counterparts, details the use of Kramers-Kronig relations via FFTs, and validates the method by comparing results across various dimensions and doping levels.

Abstract

We present an efficient method for computing the real-space propagators (lattice Green's functions) of any tight-binding Hamiltonian in the presence of a Fermi sea with carrier concentration $x$. The method is valid for any lattice, dispersion, and dimension, provided the corresponding $x=0$ propagators are known. We show that suitable combinations of the finite-$x$ particle-addition propagators have a real or imaginary part trivially related to their $x=0$ counterpart, while the remaining part follows from a Kramers-Kronig relation that can be evaluated for all energies at once using the fast Fourier transforms. The computational cost is therefore independent of the dimensionality, unlike that of direct Brillouin-zone integration. The particle-removal propagators follow from general identities. We validate the method against direct integration for hypercubic lattices in one, two, and three dimensions, and use the 2D square lattice to illustrate how the shape of the Fermi surface is imprinted on the spatial structure of the propagators. In particular, at energies far outside the band, the propagator maps converge to the Fraunhofer diffraction pattern whose aperture is the unoccupied part of the Brillouin zone.

Optical poling of a quantum ferroelectric metal across the order-disorder phase transition

Summary. This work investigates the ferroelectric transition in quantum ferroelectric metals using advanced optical techniques. By employing RA-SHG and pump-probe measurements, the authors confirm the order-disorder nature of the transition. Crucially, they demonstrate that femtosecond optical pulses can deterministically align the disordered polar nanoregions, opening an all-optical pathway for controlling these quantum materials.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to use light to deterministically write and read out polarization textures in a quantum material provides a novel, non-electrical control mechanism, which is highly relevant for understanding and manipulating quantum states in condensed matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the unsettled microscopic origin of the ferroelectric transition in quantum ferroelectric metals and seeks to develop methods to probe and control the associated polar nanoregions.

Main result. The study unambiguously demonstrates the order-disorder nature of the ferroelectric transition and establishes an all-optical route to deterministically control the orientation of these polar nanoregions.

Method. The research combines symmetry-resolved optical measurements (Rotational Anisotropy Second Harmonic Generation, RA-SHG) with ultrafast pump-probe spectroscopy to track polarization dynamics.

Model / system. The physical system is electron-doped strontium titanate, a prototypical quantum ferroelectric metal. The analysis models the local dipoles as tunneling between states, and the SHG response is analyzed based on fluctuations in local polarization ($\langle P^2 angle$-type).

Key observables. RA-SHG intensity patterns, which reveal orientational statistics; the divergence of $\Delta I_{ ext{SHG}}$ near the transition temperature ($T_{ ext{FE}}$); and the degree of alignment achieved via optical poling.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$), carrier concentration, and the polarization angle ($\delta$) of the incident optical pump field.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the SHG signal is dominated by local dipole fluctuations and that the optical poling process can be modeled by fitting the pump-probe signal to a specific functional form.

Figures summary. Figures show temperature-dependent phase diagrams, RA-SHG patterns confirming 2-fold symmetry, and pump-probe measurements demonstrating that the optical field aligns over 80% of the polar nanoregions above the transition temperature.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical system and the debate between ferroelectric mechanisms. It then uses RA-SHG to characterize the transition as order-disorder. Finally, it employs pump-probe spectroscopy to demonstrate deterministic, ultrafast optical control (poling) of the polar nanoregions.

Abstract

Electron-doped strontium titanate has emerged as a prototypical quantum ferroelectric metal. It provides a fertile ground to explore how ferroelectric instability intertwined with itinerant electrons creates quantum phenomena, including unconventional superconductivity. Despite extensive studies, the microscopic origin of the ferroelectric transition remains unsettled, with distinct interpretations based on displacive mechanism driven by soft mode and order-disorder alignment of local dipoles. In particular, the local dipoles form nanoscale, spatially heterogeneous clusters, termed polar nanoregions, posing a significant challenge for probing or manipulating them. Here, using rotational anisotropy second harmonic generation, a symmetry-resolved probe, we quantify the orientational statistics of polar nanoregions in dilute electron-doped Sr$_{0.95}$Ba$_{0.05}$Ti$_{1-x}$Nb$_x$O$_3$. By tracking the alignment and meltdown of polar nanoregions in thermal cycles, we unambiguously demonstrate the order-disorder nature of the ferroelectric transition. We further show that, above transition temperature, femtosecond optical fields enable deterministic control of otherwise disordered polar nanoregions, realizing reversible write and readout of polar textures on ultrafast timescales. Our findings provide new insight into ferroelectric instability and establish an all-optical route of controlling polar metal systems where conventional electrical approaches are not feasible.

Summary. This theoretical work establishes the phase-coherence length ($\lambda_{\phi}$) as the key metric for assessing dimensionality in nanoscale semiconductors. By analyzing electron-phonon coupling in Si nanosheets, the authors provide quantitative estimates for critical thicknesses, arguing that confinement is determined by how far an excitation can maintain phase coherence. This has significant implications for designing next-generation quantum devices.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous focus on the phase-coherence length ($\lambda_{\phi}$) as the primary diagnostic tool provides a quantitative, advanced perspective on confinement effects, which is crucial for understanding open quantum systems in solid-state devices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of quantitatively determining the electronic and vibrational dimensionality of semiconductor nanostructures, such as silicon nanosheets.

Main result. The critical length scale for confinement is argued to be the electron and phonon phase-coherence length ($\lambda_{\phi}$), which dictates whether the system behaves as a 2DEG or approaches bulk 3D behavior.

Method. The analysis employs theoretical tools, including perturbation theory to calculate the electron self-energy ($\Sigma_0$) due to electron-phonon interactions, and deriving 2D phonon density of states.

Model / system. The primary system studied is silicon nanosheets, modeled as quantum wells or thin films, with the physics governed by electron-phonon coupling and phonon scattering processes.

Key observables. Electron spectral density $A(\mathbf{K}, \omega)$, electron self-energy $\Sigma_0(\mathbf{K}, \omega)$, and the effective phase-coherence length ($\lambda_{\phi}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Critical confinement lengths are estimated to be around 8 nm for electrons at room temperature, while phonon confinement depends heavily on wavelength (from 10 nm to $1 \mu$m).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on perturbation theory for calculating scattering rates and assumes that the coherence length is the definitive diagnostic parameter for dimensionality.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes reference figures illustrating the comparison of coherence lengths to physical dimensions and the spectral broadening due to electron-phonon coupling.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first defining the problem of dimensionality assessment, then developing a theoretical framework using self-energy calculations for electrons, and finally extending this analysis to phonon confinement effects.

Abstract

We discuss the problem of assessing the electronic and vibrational dimensionality of a semiconductor nanostructure: How thin and/or wide must a nanostructure be in order to induce electron and phonon confinement? Clarifying the physical justification for common criteria found in the literature, we view the electron coherence length (defined as the electron and phonon inelastic mean free path) as their field of view' and argue (or, better yet,speculate') that this sets the important length scale. Considering the example of Si nanosheets at room temperature, and drawing from results found in the literature, we estimate that the critical length below which electrons are subject to quantum confinement is of the order of (or smaller than) 8 nm, when their coherence length is determined by energy losses to phonons and remote phonons in gated structures. On the contrary, no single length-scale can be given for phonons: Taking their coherence length as determined by scattering with electrons and anharmonic three-phonon processes, short wavelength acoustic and optical phonons may be confined only by structures as small as 10 nm. Long-wavelength acoustic phonons, instead, may exhibit a coherence length of the order of 1 micrometer, so that they may be confined over much larger distances.

Abstract

We introduce a graphite-gated architecture for bilayer graphene devices in which the active device is completely isolated from the natural graphene edges. Using a single patterned graphite-gate layer, we realize a fully electrostatically defined Hall-bar. Raman spectroscopy and Kelvin probe force microscopy suggest that this boundary is disordered due to the used fabrication methods. Comparably, the quantum mobility in a fieldeffect transistor fabricated with the same architecture is not limited by boundary scattering and the visibility of quantum oscillations down to 4 mT suggests a record value of 2.5 x 10^6 cm2/Vs.

Abstract

Nanometer-thick fluid films play a critical role in confined multiphase processes, yet the thermodynamics and stability of free gas nanofilms remain poorly understood compared with their liquid counterparts. Here, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are employed to systematically investigate gas nanofilms confined between bulk liquid phases using Lennard-Jones argon as a model system. The results show that the surface tension decreases exponentially with decreasing film thickness, accompanied by an increasing magnitude of the negative disjoining pressure. Upon thinning, the planar gas film undergoes a distinct morphological transition from a stable planar state to a transient or persistent spherical bubble through the formation and growth of a liquid bridge. The film surface area strongly affects its thermodynamic properties, with larger areas producing stronger thickness dependence and larger deviations from classical density functional theory (cDFT) predictions. The closer agreement between MD and cDFT at smaller surface areas suggests that the discrepancy primarily arises from thermal capillary-wave fluctuations, which are included in MD but omitted in mean-field cDFT. Moreover, at small film thicknesses, the magnitude of the disjoining pressure increases with decreasing temperature, consistent with the enhanced sensitivity of the confined gas phase to thickness variations and contrasting with the trend generally reported for liquid nanofilms. These findings provide molecular insights into the thermodynamics and stability of gas nanofilms, with implications for confined multiphase transport and droplet coalescence.
