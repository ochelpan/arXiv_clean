Summary. The paper investigates the optimality of the free time evolution for a quantum wave function when its initial and final probability densities are fixed. By formulating this as an optimal transport problem, it shows that while the evolution is globally optimal without nodes, the presence of nodes renders the minimum only local, suggesting inherent instability in such configurations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a deep variational characterization of free quantum evolution by linking it to optimal transport theory. The distinction between global minima (no nodes) and local minima (nodes present) offers novel insights into the stability of quantum states in open or constrained systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is determining whether the free evolution of a quantum wave function, constrained by fixed initial and final probability densities, corresponds to an optimal path in a variational sense.

Main result. The free evolution minimizes a generalized action functional (related to quadratic optimal transport cost) when no nodes are present; however, the presence of nodes suggests intrinsic instability for this minimum.

Method. The analysis uses techniques from Optimal Transport Theory and Stochastic Mechanics, formulating the problem as minimizing an action functional $AQ( ho, v)$ derived from necessary conditions for extremality (Hamilton-Jacobi equations).

Model / system. The system is governed by the free Schrödinger equation in finite dimensions. The mathematical framework relates the quantum evolution to a diffusion process whose density $ ho$ and current $v$ are analyzed.

Key observables. Probability density $ ho(x, t) = |\psi(x, t)|^2$, associated velocity field $v(x, t)$, and the action functional $AQ( ho, v)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The time interval $[0, 1]$ and the boundary conditions defined by fixed initial ($ ho_0$) and final ($ ho_1$) moduli.

Assumptions / limitations. Assumes $\psi$ is unique up to a constant phase; handling nodes requires restricting analysis to a subset $\Xi$ where strong solutions of the associated SDE exist.

Figures summary. Not specified by notes, but the structure suggests proofs involving functional minimization and convergence arguments using sequences of approximations.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by defining the relevant state space ($\Xi$), deriving necessary conditions for extremality via calculus of variations (leading to HJM equations), establishing the connection to stochastic processes, and finally proving the optimality theorem through convex functional analysis and limiting procedures.

Abstract

Extending and refining a preceding work, the free evolution of a quantum wave function, with fixed initial and final modulus, is considered in the general case where nodes are allowed. The method is based on considering a suitable simple functional related to the quadratic optimal transport cost in discrete time. Under reasonable assumptions it is shown that, if nodes are present, the free evolution of the wave function with fixed initial and final modulus is a sort of local minimum. If no nodes are present the minimum is global, suggesting intrinsic instability of the nodes in a free quantum evolution.

Summary. This theoretical paper explores quantum advantages in Bayesian games by comparing equilibria derived from classical versus separable quantum correlations. It proves that these local quantum states can achieve better outcomes than any classically correlated strategy, even when the game involves conflicting interests. This advances the understanding of non-classical resources beyond simple entanglement.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly about AMO systems, the rigorous mathematical tools developed for quantifying non-local correlations in game theory are highly relevant to understanding resource theories in open quantum systems or multipartite entanglement verification.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The research investigates how different types of correlations (classical vs. quantum) affect the equilibria achieved by independent agents in Bayesian games of incomplete information.

Main result. The paper demonstrates that even separable quantum states can generate new, genuinely quantum equilibria in competitive Bayesian games that yield higher social welfare than any equilibrium achievable using only classical correlations.

Method. The analysis uses game theory frameworks (Nash, Correlated Equilibria) combined with quantum information tools to compare the set of possible joint probability distributions derived from local vs. quantum measurements.

Model / system. The model is a general Bayesian game involving multiple players and incomplete information about their types. The correlation structure is modeled using quantum states (separable vs. entangled) acting on the players' shared Hilbert space.

Key observables. Social Welfare (SW), expected utility for individual players, and the comparison of equilibrium sets ($ ext{Corr}(G)$ vs. $ ext{QuSEP}(G)$).

Important parameters / regimes. The structure of the game itself, including payoff functions and prior distributions $p(t)$. Specific examples use parameters related to CHSH inequalities.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that separable quantum correlations are sufficient to demonstrate a quantum advantage in competitive settings, moving beyond previous reliance on full entanglement.

Figures summary. Figures compare various equilibrium scores (e.g., CHSH scores) against classical limits ($eta(G)$), showing separations between correlated and Nash equilibria when quantum strategies are employed.

Paper structure. The paper builds by first establishing the general framework of Bayesian games, then comparing correlation types (classical vs. quantum), and finally applying these concepts to specific examples like modified CHSH games to quantify the advantage.

Abstract

Bayesian games, also known as games of incomplete information, are a fruitful arena for exploring the impact of correlations on a set of independent agents (players) via the game equilibria to which they give rise. It was realised some time ago that quantum states shared between the players can lead to new and beneficial equilibria, compared to classical correlation. While until now examples of this effect required an entangled state, here we show that even separable states can create new, genuinely quantum equilibria in games, that are advantageous with respect to all classically correlated equilibria. This shows that non-classical correlations beyond entanglement are indeed a resource, even in otherwise entirely classical situations. Our result brings quantum advantage in games significantly closer to possible realisation.

The Apparatus Strikes Back: Momentum Conservation and the Cost of Spatial Superpositions

Authors: Lucas C. Céleri, Diogo O. Soares-Pinto, Daniel A. Turolla Vanzella
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08819v1

Summary. The authors derive a universal constraint showing that preparing large spatial superpositions of massive particles inevitably entangles the particle with the momentum recoil of the apparatus used for splitting. This entanglement causes decoherence in the measured particle state, setting a fundamental lower bound on experimental feasibility based only on conservation laws.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a fundamental, model-independent constraint on quantum metrology experiments involving massive objects, linking macroscopic mechanics (momentum conservation) directly to microscopic quantum coherence limits. It is crucial reading for anyone designing next-generation matter-wave interferometers or gravity tests.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper identifies a universal constraint on preparing massive particles in coherent spatial superpositions arising purely from momentum conservation when an apparatus is used for splitting.

Main result. Momentum conservation necessitates that the particle becomes entangled with the apparatus due to recoil, leading to a quantitative lower bound on achievable coherence even for macroscopic setups.

Method. The analysis uses quantum state evolution operators and calculates the reduced density matrix of the particle by tracing out the apparatus degrees of freedom, quantifying coherence via relative entropy.

Model / system. The system involves a massive particle ($m$) placed in superposition ($\pm d/2$) using an auxiliary apparatus of mass $M$. The analysis focuses on the entanglement generated between the particle and the apparatus's Center-of-Mass (CM) motion, modeled as harmonic oscillators.

Key observables. Coherence factor ($\chi_c$), relative entropy of coherence ($C_{rel}$), and the resulting bound relating $m$, $d$, $M$, and $T$.

Important parameters / regimes. Particle mass ($m$), separation distance ($d$), apparatus mass ($M$), temperature ($T$), and characteristic frequencies ($\omega$). The low-temperature limit yields a temperature-independent constraint.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis primarily assumes that only the CM degree of freedom of the apparatus participates in state preparation, ignoring internal vibrational modes (which would tighten the bound).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how coherence decreases with increasing particle mass and show contour plots mapping the momentum-conservation constraint on achievable superposition separation ($d/\lambda_C$) based on apparatus parameters.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the fundamental conservation law, derives the entanglement consequence for the reduced state, quantifies the resulting decoherence bound using CM motion, and finally discusses practical implications, including internal mode excitations and conditions for 'false' coherence loss.

Abstract

Preparing massive particles in coherent spatial superpositions is a central objective of modern quantum science, motivated by applications ranging from fundamental tests of quantum mechanics and gravity to quantum-enhanced sensing. The experimental difficulty of realizing such superpositions is usually attributed to environmental decoherence mechanisms whose impact depends on the details of the experimental implementation. Here we identify a universal constraint arising from momentum conservation and the quantum nature of the preparation apparatus. Any protocol that places a particle of mass $m$ in a spatial superposition with separation $d$ necessarily entangles the particle with the center-of-mass degree of freedom of the apparatus responsible for the splitting. The resulting recoil displacement, fixed by conservation of the mass dipole moment, reduces the coherence of the particle when the apparatus is not included as part of the quantum system. For an apparatus of mass $M$, preserving coherence requires the recoil displacement to remain smaller than the coherence length of the apparatus center-of-mass state, leading to a quantitative bound expressible as a constraint on its temperature and on how rigidly the apparatus is anchored to the laboratory frame. We analyze the implications of this bound for current matter-wave interferometry experiments, proposals for gravitationally mediated entanglement, and tests of quantum mechanics near the Planck scale. Our main result, which may seem counterintuitive, is that the recoil of even heavy macroscopic apparatuses can pose a surprisingly strong constraint on the coherence of spatial superpositions of particles with masses well below the Planck mass. Finally, we discuss why the resulting limitation should be regarded as a conservative estimate and under which conditions it can be interpreted as an instance of false decoherence.

Abstract

Accurate noise classification is essential for operating near-term quantum processors, yet existing approaches, such as quantum process tomography, scale exponentially with system size, limiting their practicality for routine calibration. We propose a scalable noise fingerprinting pipeline that combines structured classical shadow tomography with physics-informed feature engineering to identify noise channels from a fixed set of 3-qubit probe circuits. Each sample is represented by a 279-dimensional feature vector constructed from randomized Pauli measurements and derived observables, designed to resolve physically similar noise channels that produce overlapping signatures under generic measurement sets. We evaluate three classifiers, i.e., random forest, extra trees, and a multilayer perceptron, on a dataset of 14,000 labeled samples spanning 10 noise types. The random forest classifier achieves the highest test accuracy of 0.8426 with a macro F1 score of 0.8437, outperforming both baselines. Confusion analysis reveals that many noise types are classified with high reliability, with the remaining confusions occurring between channels sharing similar physical decay mechanisms, motivating future work on richer probe states and noise parameter estimation.

Abstract

We investigate the evolution of spin correlations and the nature of the multi-stage magnetic transition in the quasi-two-dimensional easy-plane van der Waals magnet $\rm CrCl_3$. By combining broadband ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) spectroscopy and DC SQUID magnetometry on mechanically exfoliated micro-flakes with non-local dynamical mean-field theory (DFT+DMFT) calculations, we analyze both long- and short range magnetic order in CrCl$_3$. Experimentally, SQUID and FMR measurements confirm the presence of the crossover to a spin polarized phase with the subsequent transition into an antiferromagnetic ground state upon cooling, but show robust short-range correlations at temperatures far above the magnetic ordering temperatures. Theoretically, we show the existence of highly stable local magnetic moments at room temperature, with a giant room temperature lifetime $τ$ of 130--300 ps due to a wide Mott bandgap. Below room temperature, a rapid growth of the in-plane correlation length $ξ$ signals the formation of strong short range magnetic order consistent with the experimental observations. We also obtain a temperature-driven crossover of the interlayer exchange interaction, which changes from positive (ferromagnetic) at high temperatures to negative (antiferromagnetic) in the low-temperature ordered phase.

Abstract

High Performance Computing (HPC) centers are expanding to encompass resources that extend beyond traditional computing. By extending resources to quantum computing, hybrid quantum-classical workflows tackle complex optimization problems that have never before been possible. However, integrating quantum processing units (QPUs) into cloud-native and scientific workload managers presents a unique orchestration challenge: remote quantum devices introduce a second, external queue -- a two-queue problem -- alongside the queue owned by the traditional scheduler. In this work we present Fluence, a Kubernetes scheduler plugin backed by the Fluxion graph-based scheduler, that enables informed, gang-scheduled placement for quantum-classical workloads and custom resources. We evaluate Fluence across three scenarios using AWS Braket simulators and real QPUs. First, under node contention, Fluence's atomic gang placement all but eliminates the wasted node-time that a default scheduler accrues by partially placing gangs. Second, we introduce a synchronization primitive for the two-queue problem in which a single producer submits a shared quantum task while consumers remain scheduling-gated, reducing worker idle time by roughly 5x under short device queues and by orders of magnitude when a real device queue stretched to hours. Third, cost- and queue-aware backend selection pins the cheapest or shortest-queue device satisfying a workload, cutting mean per-run cost by roughly 70x and time-to-result from hours to under a minute. Together, these results show that quantum-awareness can be added to a cloud-native scheduler without modifying user containers.

Abstract

We investigate the formation of loop-current ordered phases in half-filled SU($N$) two-leg fermionic ladders. Using a low-energy approach, we uncover the existence of non-perturbative duality symmetries relating four competing orders. Two of these orders correspond to loop-current ordered phases that spontaneously break the time-reversal symmetry and describe charge currents circulating in a staggered pattern either around the plaquettes or along the diagonals of the ladder. These unconventional phases are shown to be dual to conventional (charge and bond) density-wave phases through an exact density-current duality symmetry existing on the lattice. From a perturbative renormalization group approach, we find that these phases for $N>2$ are stabilized in a half-filled SU($N$) two-leg Hubbard ladder with an additional SU($N$) Hund's interaction. The effect of a small doping on these phases is also discussed.

Abstract

Imaginary-time evolution (ITE) is a powerful method for ground-state preparation of a given Hamiltonian. The normalized ITE can be viewed as a gradient flow of the energy expectation value with respect to the Fubini--Study metric. In this work, we propose a generalized nonlinear imaginary-time evolution (NITE) for more general quantum state-preparation tasks. We further present a hardware-efficient variational implementation of NITE and reveal its connection to quantum natural gradient descent. NITE is applied to several subroutine tasks, including variance minimization in variational eigensolvers, probe-state preparation in variational quantum sensing, and excited-state preparation using penalty terms. We prove that NITE achieves a local exponential convergence rate under reasonable assumptions. Our results show that NITE outperforms standard gradient descent and can serve as an efficient optimization method for variational tasks beyond ground-state preparation.

Authors: Alan Rodrigo Mendoza Sosa, Atahualpa S. Kraemer, Michael Schmiedeberg, Erdal C. Oğuz
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.09274v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Frenkel-Kontorova 1/5

Summary. This work analyzes quasicrystals to show that increasing rotational symmetry ($N$) drives the system toward apparent randomness. The authors identify two key length scales—a crossover scale ($\kappa_N$) and a statistical unit cell size ($\lambda_N$)—which quantify how deterministic order is progressively concealed by disorder-like fluctuations.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of an emergent 'statistical unit cell' provides a powerful mathematical tool to quantify how deterministic structure persists even when conventional periodicity is absent, which has implications for understanding glassy or disordered quantum phases.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how deterministic order, characteristic of quasicrystals, can become statistically indistinguishable from true randomness as the rotational symmetry ($N$) increases.

Main result. Increasing $N$ causes a crossover length ($\kappa_N$) below which local statistical signatures are suppressed, leading to a geometric critical point where order and randomness merge over any finite observation window.

Method. The authors analyze density fluctuations ($\sigma^2(R)/R$) and pair correlation functions ($g(R)$) using generalized dual methods applied to $N$-fold quasicrystals in 1D and 2D.

Model / system. The system under study is perfect quasicrystals, which possess long-range order but lack translational periodicity. The analysis compares these structures against conventional crystals and amorphous matter by varying the rotational symmetry parameter $N$.

Key observables. Number variance ($\sigma^2(R)$), pair correlation function ($g(R)$), crossover length ($\kappa_N$), and statistical unit cell size ($\lambda_N$).

Important parameters / regimes. Rotational symmetry $N$ (or $\epsilon = 1/N$), which controls the degree of quasiperiodicity; dimensionality (1D vs. 2D).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the statistical properties can be characterized by emergent length scales ($\kappa_N, \lambda_N$) derived from fluctuation measurements.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate density fluctuations showing crossovers near $\kappa_N$ and oscillations at $\lambda_N$. Comparisons are made with random local phasonic flips, and scaling behavior of these characteristic lengths with $N$ is quantified.

Paper structure. The paper establishes a quantitative framework by analyzing how increasing rotational symmetry ($N$) suppresses quasiperiodic signatures. It identifies two key scales ($\kappa_N$ and $\lambda_N$), demonstrating that the system approaches a critical point where order vanishes statistically, while defining the statistical unit cell as the remaining signature of determinism.

Abstract

The classical dichotomy between crystalline order and amorphous disorder is increasingly challenged by novel states that lack conventional crystalline symmetries while retaining crystal-like properties. Quasicrystals occupy a distinctive position within this expanding framework by possessing long-range order without translational periodicity, thereby permitting arbitrary $N$-fold rotational symmetry. Paradoxically, far from their unique symmetry center, high-symmetry quasicrystals closely resemble disordered patterns, raising the question of how deterministic order can be detected. Here we show that increasing rotational symmetry progressively suppresses local statistical signatures of quasiperiodicity, while preserving its underlying exact long-range order. This order is thus concealed below an emergent crossover length that grows linearly with $N$. Therefore, as $N \rightarrow \infty$, the disorder-like regime expands without bound, defining a symmetry-controlled geometric critical point at which deterministic order and randomness become statistically indistinguishable over any finite observation window. For finite $N$, however, quasiperiodic order becomes detectable beyond this crossover, revealing a second emergent length scale that we identify as the size of a \textit{statistical unit cell} -- finite patches over which statistical properties recur despite the absence of conventional translational periodicity. In one dimension, the statistical-unit-cell size coincides with the crossover length, whereas in two dimensions it grows as $N^2$, remaining smaller than the size of typical approximants and establishing a hierarchy of emergent length scales. Together, the disorder-to-order crossover and statistical unit cells provide a quantitative framework connecting crystals, quasicrystals, and amorphous matter, showing how apparent disorder can emerge from purely deterministic geometry.

A Novel Parallel QCNN Architecture with Efficient Classical Simulability

Authors: Lawrence Nguyen, Hiu Yung Wong
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.08928v1

Summary. The paper introduces a novel parallel architecture for Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs) designed to overcome the exponential memory scaling problem when simulating large qubit systems. By partitioning input images and combining results through a binary reduction tree structure, the authors enable training models up to 128 qubits on classical hardware. This demonstrates that scalable quantum machine learning is possible without prohibitive computational resources.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses resource limitations in quantum machine learning by proposing a scalable simulation technique for complex quantum circuits, which is highly relevant for developing practical quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is implementing Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks (QCNNs) for image classification on large datasets like MNIST while circumventing the exponential memory scaling limitations of classical simulation.

Main result. A novel hierarchical partitioning and binary reduction architecture allows simulating QCNNs with up to 128 qubits, achieving competitive accuracy comparable to smaller, single-process models.

Method. The method employs a parallelization strategy where the input image is partitioned into independent sub-images. These partitions are processed in parallel and then combined using a binary reduction tree structure involving partial tracing over density matrices.

Model / system. The model is a QCNN built upon parameterized quantum circuits, utilizing Z-feature maps for encoding pixel data. The architecture mimics classical CNNs by alternating convolutional layers (using 2-qubit unitaries) and pooling layers that condense information via partial tracing.

Key observables. Binary classification accuracy on the MNIST dataset, measured by the Pauli Z expectation value ($\langle \sigma_Z angle$) of the final qubit.

Important parameters / regimes. The model was tested up to 128 qubits; parameter counting formulas were derived for convolutional and reduction blocks.

Assumptions / limitations. The simulation relies on approximating the full state vector using density matrices ($ ho$) and assuming that pooling layers allow for discarding irrelevant information via partial tracing. The process requires the number of processes to be a power of two.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the QCNN structure, showing how an image is partitioned (e.g., 128 qubits split into smaller blocks) and combined through a binary reduction tree, with comparisons made between single-process vs. parallel accuracy results for various qubit counts.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the need to scale QCNNs beyond classical simulation limits. It details the novel hierarchical partitioning approach using convolutional and pooling circuits. The core is demonstrating the feasibility of simulating large systems (up to 128 qubits) via a binary reduction tree, culminating in performance comparisons showing maintained or improved accuracy.

Abstract

This work presents a study of an implementation of a novel Quantum Convolutional Neural Network (QCNN) for binary classification of images from the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) dataset. Using a novel architecture inspired by previous QCNN and classical convolutional neural network (CNN) implementations, we use a hierarchical partitioning approach to implement a QCNN circuit that can be approximated and simulated efficiently on a classical machine for a large problem. First, the original image is partitioned such that each process handles a smaller portion of the image, which is encoded into independent states. Then, these partitions merge and combine, resulting in states that contain information from both partitions while halving the number of processes. After repeating this until one process remains, we reduce the dimensionality of the state until a single qubit remains for measurement. Using this approach, we can use multiple processes in parallel to simulate a large QCNN program without the need for exponentially growing hardware requirements as the number of qubits increases. In our work, we use this scheme to train a 128-qubit model, which is impossible to run on any classical supercomputer without the novel architecture. We also explore the impact of this new model architecture on prediction accuracy by training it to perform binary classification on the MNIST dataset with a small number of qubits, and comparing it to a model without partitioning. Our initial findings show that partitioning images into smaller sub-images with this architecture does not degrade the model's performance and sometimes even improves it, likely because it reduces the Barren plateaus issue in the partitioning process.
