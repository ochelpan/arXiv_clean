Efficiency of Continuous-Time Quantum Monte Carlo Updates in the Ferromagnetic State of the Doped SU(3) Fermi-Hubbard Model

Authors: Juntaro Fujii, Kazuki Yamamoto, Akihisa Koga
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25494v1

Summary. This work analyzes the efficiency of various Monte Carlo update schemes for simulating the doped SU(3) Fermi-Hubbard model using DMFT and CT-HYB. By calculating the integrated autocorrelation time, the authors determine that the optimal update depends critically on the magnetic phase: double-flip updates are best for the ferromagnetic state, while flavor-permutation updates excel in the paramagnetic state. This highlights that understanding the physics of the accepted updates, rather than just acceptance ratios, is key to improving sampling algorithms.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter, the rigorous analysis of sampling efficiency and the development of specialized update algorithms (like double-flip) are highly relevant to developing robust numerical techniques for strongly correlated quantum systems, which share methodological overlap with quantum simulation approaches.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the sampling efficiency of various update schemes within a Quantum Monte Carlo solver applied to the doped SU(3) Fermi-Hubbard model.

Main result. The double-flip update is most efficient in the ferromagnetic state, while the flavor-permutation update is superior in the paramagnetic state, demonstrating that the nature of the required configuration change dictates the optimal update scheme.

Method. The authors compare different Monte Carlo update schemes by calculating the integrated autocorrelation time ($\tau_{int}$) of the majority-flavor occupation, relating it to the variance of the MC average.

Model / system. The system is the doped SU(3) Fermi-Hubbard model on a hypercubic lattice. The problem is mapped onto an effective impurity problem solved using the Continuous-Time Hybridization-Expansion Quantum Monte Carlo (CT-HYB) method within Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT).

Key observables. Integrated autocorrelation time ($\tau_{int}$) of the majority-flavor occupation, flavor-resolved particle densities ($n_\sigma$), and the magnitude of occupation changes ($\Delta x$).

Important parameters / regimes. Low-temperature and strong-coupling regime; specific simulation parameters used were $T/D = 0.005$ and $U/D = 300$.

Assumptions / limitations. The variance of the MC average is approximated as $\text{Var}(\langle X \rangle_{MC}) \simeq 2\tau_{int}(X) / N_{MCS}$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison of $\tau_{int}$ for different updates in FM and PM states, alongside probability distributions of occupation changes ($\Delta x$) caused by accepted updates.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the model and the CT-HYB/DMFT framework, compares three update schemes (simple, simple+flavor-permutation, simple+double-flip) by analyzing $\tau_{int}$ in both FM and PM regimes, and concludes by analyzing the mechanism—the characteristic scale of configuration changes—that dictates efficiency.

Abstract

We investigate the sampling efficiency in a segment-based continuous-time hybridization-expansion quantum Monte Carlo solver within dynamical mean-field theory for the doped SU(3) Fermi-Hubbard model. Focusing on the low-temperature and strong-coupling regime, where a ferromagnetic state appears upon hole doping away from one-third filling, we compare several update schemes. Adding the double-flip update to the basic local update yields the smallest integrated autocorrelation time of the majority-flavor occupation in the ferromagnetic regime, whereas adding the flavor-permutation update is the most effective in the paramagnetic regime. We further clarify the origin of this difference by analyzing the characteristic occupation changes caused by accepted updates.

On the Complexity of Finding Decoherence Free Subspaces

Authors: Evan Borras
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26769v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This work analyzes the computational difficulty of finding Decoherence Free Subspaces (DFS) in open quantum systems described by the Lindblad equation. By mapping the problem to specific complexity questions, the authors prove that determining the existence of a DFS is QMA-hard for sufficiently local interactions. This suggests that such a characterization is intractable even for quantum computers.

Why it may be interesting. It establishes a fundamental computational barrier for characterizing coherence preservation in realistic, dissipative quantum systems, linking quantum information theory to quantum computation complexity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper studies the computational complexity of determining whether an open quantum system admits a Decoherence Free Subspace (DFS).

Main result. Both the $k$-Local Lindbladian Pure Steady-State problem and the $k$-Local Lindbladian problem are shown to be hard for the complexity class QMA when the locality $k \geq 5$.

Method. The authors use complexity theory techniques, generalizing Kitaev's clock Hamiltonian construction to the open quantum system setting to establish QMA-hardness.

Model / system. The system is an open quantum system governed by the time-independent Lindblad master equation. The analysis focuses on the steady-state structure (DFS) preserved under this dissipation.

Key observables. The existence of a steady-state subspace (DFS) and the decay rates of purity for history states.

Important parameters / regimes. Locality parameter $k$, and the threshold for QMA-completeness ($k \geq 5$).

Assumptions / limitations. The hardness results suggest intractability even for quantum computation, relying on the assumption that BQP $ eq$ QMA.

Paper structure. The paper defines the problem, introduces the $k$-Local Lindbladian problems, and then proves the QMA-hardness of both problems using specialized constructions.

Abstract

Decoherence free subspaces are a steady-state structure of the open quantum system which preserves quantum coherence between the states lying with in it and thus has found a variety of applications throughout quantum information science and technology. In this paper we study the computational complexity of deciding whether an open quantum system admits a decoherence free subspace or not. More specifically we study this problem with in the context of Markovian open quantum systems, governed by the time-independent Lindblad master equation. Along the way we introduce the $k$-Local Lindbladian problem, which captures the difficulty of computing purity decay rates under Lindbladian dynamics. We show that both problems are hard for the complexity class Quantum Merlin Arthur (QMA) when the locality $k \geq 5$, with the first under perfect completeness and the second being complete for QMA. Our hardness construction generalizes Kitaev's clock Hamiltonian construction to the open quantum system setting by encoding the execution of a quantum circuit into the steady subspace of a Lindbladian containing both pure and mixed history states. This subspace is then mixed depending on the output of the encoded circuit. Our results suggest that deciding whether a generic Markovian open quantum system admits a decoherence free subspace is intractable even for quantum computation.

Summary. This paper theoretically investigates the role of interaction range in stabilizing magnetic hedgehog lattices, which are key topological spin textures. Using Monte Carlo simulations, the authors show that intermediate-range interactions are optimal for stabilizing these structures. Method. The research employs extensive Monte Carlo simulations to systematically vary the range and spatial decay of the exchange interactions.

Model / system. The system models magnetic hedgehog lattices, which are 3D topological spin textures hosting magnetic monopoles, studied using an effective bilinear spin model for cubic chiral magnets.

Key observables. Stability of HLs, number of monopole-antimonopole pairs per unit cell, magnetic moments ($m_\eta$), and scalar spin chirality ($\chi_s$).

Important parameters / regimes. Interaction range (short, intermediate, long), characterized by parameters like $\gamma$ and $r_c$.

Paper structure. The paper establishes a theoretical framework by modeling the Hamiltonian, then uses MC simulations to map out phase diagrams by interpolating between short-range (insulating) and long-range (metallic) interaction profiles.

Abstract

Topological spin textures have attracted considerable attention through their emergent electromagnetic properties, including the topological Hall effect and the topological Nernst effect. Among them, magnetic hedgehog lattices (HLs) are three-dimensional topological spin textures that host emergent magnetic monopoles and antimonopoles. HLs have been identified in metallic compounds, such as $\mathrm{MnSi}_{1-x}\mathrm{Ge}_x$ and $\mathrm{SrFeO_3}$. Various theoretical models have been proposed, with either short-range or long-range interactions, which are typically regarded as effective descriptions of insulating and metallic systems, respectively. However, the role of the interaction range in stabilizing the HLs remains only partially understood. To address this gap, we investigate the stability of HLs by systematically varying the range and spatial decay of the exchange interactions, thereby interpolating between interaction profiles commonly associated with localized-spin insulating models and itinerant-electron metallic models. Based on extensive Monte Carlo simulations, we find that HLs are stabilized over a broad interaction range. Intriguingly, among the interaction profiles examined, intermediate-range interactions are particularly favorable for stabilizing HLs. They give rise to two distinct types of $3Q$-HLs that differ in the number of monopole-antimonopole pairs per magnetic unit cell, and their relative stability depends sensitively on the interaction range. Our results provide a unified framework for tuning phase diagrams from microscopic interaction models, serving as a guideline for interpreting experiments in candidate materials.

Abstract

Differential equations can be encoded as ground-state problems by constructing a positive-semidefinite effective Hamiltonian whose minimum-energy state represents the solution. We extend this framework in several directions. First, we show how multiple nonzero initial, boundary, and data conditions can be incorporated into the homogeneous formulation $A | f\rangle=0$ using a known nonzero reference condition. We then analyse an alternative formulation based on $A |f\rangle = | b\rangle$, with Hamiltonian $H_b=A^\dagger(I-|b\rangle\langle b|)A$, which incorporates source terms and nonzero constraints through an augmented system. For nonlinear differential equations, we examine the unphysical ground-state degeneracy introduced by tensor-product representations and consider both Hamiltonian constraints and ansatz-level restrictions for targeting physically valid product states. Finally, we develop grid-value amplitude-encoded versions of both Hamiltonian constructions and compare them with spectral coefficient encoding. Through linear and nonlinear examples, we assess how the choice of Hamiltonian formulation and function representation affects solution recovery, spectral gap, degeneracy, and readout. These results broaden the applicability of ground-state-based quantum differential-equation solvers while clarifying their principal practical trade-offs.

Abstract

Abstract

A boolean function $f$ has Fourier dimension $k$ if its nonzero Fourier coefficients span a subspace of dimension $k$. We consider the property testing task of determining whether a function has Fourier dimension at most $k$, or is $ε$-far from being so. Summary. This paper develops Spatiotemporal Multi-Party Computation (MPC), a cryptographic extension that ensures computations use inputs consistent with the parties' actual physical locations and times. It achieves this by defining 'spatiotemporal knowledge' operationally through verification protocols, leading to new UC-secure constructions for privacy-preserving computations in physical settings.

Why it may be interesting. While highly cryptographic, the focus on binding computation to physical reality (location/time) has implications for quantum networking and distributed quantum sensing where physical state verification is paramount.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper extends Secure Multiparty Computation (MPC) to Spatiotemporal MPC, requiring protocols to not only hide private digital inputs but also guarantee that the computation's inputs are physically consistent with the parties' actual locations and times.

Main result. The authors define a new notion of 'argument of spatiotemporal knowledge' and provide constructions for Universally Composable (UC) secure spatiotemporal MPC, applicable in both quantum and classical settings.

Method. The core methodology involves defining physical presence operationally through the success of an auxiliary spatiotemporal verification protocol, rather than through a direct mathematical relation.

Model / system. The model is a cryptographic framework applied to multi-party computation involving inputs that are a combination of private digital data and physical spatiotemporal coordinates (location, time, trajectory).

Key observables. Physical consistency of inputs, privacy of digital and spatiotemporal data, and composability of the computation.

Important parameters / regimes. Quantum security models (e.g., LWE, QROM), entanglement availability, and the required level of spatiotemporal precision.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem gap between standard MPC and physical constraints, defines the 'argument of spatiotemporal knowledge' based on operational verification, and then proceeds to construct the necessary UC-secure protocols.

Abstract

Secure multiparty computation (MPC) enables mutually distrustful parties to compute on private digital inputs. We initiate the study of spatiotemporal MPC, extending this paradigm to functionalities whose inputs additionally depend on physical facts such as the parties' locations, times, or trajectories. Such protocols must simultaneously hide spatiotemporal information and ensure its physical consistency: a malicious party should not be able to make the functionality operate on a spatiotemporal input inconsistent with its actual physical state. The main conceptual challenge is to formulate extraction of spatiotemporal information within the simulation-based security framework. We introduce arguments of spatiotemporal knowledge, following the principle "I prove, therefore I am:" rather than defining physical presence directly through a mathematical relation, we define it operationally through the ability to complete a sound spatiotemporal verification protocol. Accordingly, an extractor recovers a spatiotemporal point from a successful prover and certifies its physical validity by using the extracted prover to succeed in an auxiliary spatiotemporal verification protocol. Building on this notion, we define universally composable (UC) security for spatiotemporal MPC, capturing privacy, physical consistency, and composability. We provide constructions achieving this new MPC notion. We first construct UC-secure commit-and-prove of spatiotemporal knowledge: in the CRS model under LWE against quantum provers without pre-shared entanglement, and in the QROM against quantum provers with unbounded pre-shared entanglement. Using these protocols, we obtain UC-secure spatiotemporal MPC from semi-honest post-quantum MPC. We also extend our framework to support quantum functionalities with classical spatiotemporal input.

Abstract

Quantum correlations have recently attracted significant attention in collider physics, with Nonlocal Advantage of Quantum Coherence (NAQC) representing the strongest form of quantum correlation studied so far. Owing to its strength, NAQC is considerably more challenging to observe than Bell nonlocality since it is only present in narrower regions of phase space. Unlike other systems, tau-lepton pair ($τ^+τ^-$) production in electron--positron ($e^+e^-$) collisions provides an ideal testing ground, as it exhibits strong quantum correlations and, in particular, NAQC over a large region of phase space. In this paper, we investigate NAQC in $e^+e^- \to τ^+τ^-$ production at center-of-mass energies of 10.58 and 91.19~GeV, corresponding to the ongoing Belle~II and future FCC-$ee$ experiments, respectively. Remarkably, for our experimental proposal, we develop a dedicated NAQC witness, which allows to certify NAQC presence by measuring one single magnitude. A 5$σ$ observation of NAQC requires a precision of approximately 1\% at Belle~II, while a precision of a few percent is sufficient at FCC-$ee$. The resulting observation would constitute the strongest form of quantum correlation measured in a collider to date. In addition, we design an experimental scheme to implement the steering game leading to the NAQC definition, representing the first implementation of a steering game in a high-energy collider. Our results are easily extendable to other electron--positron colliders with different center-of-mass energies.

Abstract

Trapdoor claw-free functions (TCFs) underpin much of classical-quantum cryptographic interaction, yet every TCF-based protocol states its guarantees relative to an honestly generated key. We give a family-agnostic abstraction of key certification for (noisy) TCF constructions, built on two notions: a certifiable key relation, an NP relation capturing a family's honest keys with witnesses recoverable from the trapdoor; and certified key generation, which emits with each key a certificate of membership satisfying completeness, certificate soundness with extractability, and key privacy. Finally, we delimit the primitive's reach: for protocols resting on injective invariance, an accepting certificate is itself a family distinguisher, leaking exactly the bit such protocols must hide.

Using sophisticated tools like random-current moment bounds and correlation inequalities, the authors establish a precise power-law relationship between these two fundamental spectral quantities near the critical point. This work represents a significant mathematical advance in the understanding of phase transitions in lattice models.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical statistical mechanics, the rigorous comparison of spectral gaps (mass gap vs. Lee-Yang gap) provides deep mathematical tools applicable to understanding spectral properties in quantum many-body systems, especially near critical points.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to rigorously compare the subcritical mass gap ($m_eta$) with the Lee-Yang gap ($L_eta$) in the planar Ising model.

Main result. The authors prove that the mass gap and Lee-Yang gap are comparable near criticality, specifically showing $c L^{8/15}/eta \le m_eta \le C L^{8/15}/eta$ for the subcritical regime.

Method. The proof combines advanced techniques including random-current moment bounds, correlation inequalities, finite block expansions, and analysis of the Lee-Yang product zeros.

Model / system. The system is the planar, $\mathbb{Z}_2$ Ising model, studied in the sub-critical regime ($0 < eta < eta_c$). The analysis extends to the Random Field Ising Model (RFIM) in some parts.

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation relies on several advanced inequalities (e.g., correlation inequalities, Griffiths–Kelly–Sherman inequalities) and involves conjectures regarding the RFIM limit.

Abstract

We prove that the subcritical mass gap is comparable to the Lee-Yang gap over free-boundary square boxes raised to the power $8/15$. Random-current moment bounds, a correlation inequality and a finite block expansion give the upper bound. The lower bound follows from the Lee-Yang product, the susceptibility and the critical magnetization. We also obtain uniform factorial bounds on the field derivatives of the pressure.
