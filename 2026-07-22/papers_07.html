Blind Transpiler: An open-source library for universally blind and homomorphic quantum computations

Authors: Mohit Joshi, Manoj Kumar Mishra, S. Karthikeyan
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.17131v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · entanglement & information structure 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces 'Blind Transpiler,' an open-source library designed to solve the lack of tools for prototyping Blind Quantum Computation (BQC). It works by transpiling standard quantum circuits into secure, blind instruction sets suitable for delegation between a client and a remote server. This enables practical testing of advanced cryptographic applications like secure machine learning in quantum settings.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly about AMO physics, the underlying concepts of secure computation and resource management are highly relevant to quantum networking and distributed quantum computing architectures where trust boundaries must be strictly enforced.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary problem addressed is the lack of software tools for rapidly prototyping Blind Quantum Computation (BQC) protocols, which hinders academic investigation into secure quantum computation.

Main result. The authors developed 'Blind Transpiler,' an open-source library that successfully transpiles standard Qiskit circuits into their blind counterparts suitable for client-server delegation.

Method. The method involves a modular, layered architecture where the library translates general quantum circuits into specific Blind Operation (BOP) instructions tailored for different BQC primitives like Quantum Homomorphic Encryption (QHE) and Universal Blind Quantum Computation (UBQC).

Model / system. The focus is on abstract quantum computation protocols rather than a physical hardware realization. The model involves simulating the delegation of circuits from a client to a remote server, assuming only basic gates are available locally.

Key observables. Key observables include the resulting BQCInstruction object, which encodes the list of Blind Operations (BOPs), and metrics like the number of client/server gates and communication rounds.

Important parameters / regimes. The library supports various protocols including QHE, UBQC, FDQC, and SSDQC. Key parameters involve key sizes for cryptographic security and approximations needed for non-parametric gates (e.g., Solovay-Kitaev decomposition).

Assumptions / limitations. It is assumed that the client side only has access to basic gates ($\{X, Z, ext{Swap}, ext{Measure}\}$), while complex operations are delegated to the server. Parametric gates often require approximation.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the library flow: Input Qiskit circuit $ ightarrow$ BQCInstruction object (BOPs) $ ightarrow$ Blind Circuit. Examples show successful blind classification on the IRIS dataset, contrasting correct vs. incorrect decryption outcomes.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem gap, details the modular, layered architecture of the 'Blind Transpiler' library, describes the transpilation process for various BQC primitives (QHE, UBQC), demonstrates its application via a blind variational classifier on IRIS, and concludes with software availability information.

Abstract

Blind quantum computation is a cryptographic primitive that allows a limited-capability client to delegate its complex computation to a remote server without revealing its data and/or computation. This branch of quantum cryptography has been bifurcated into two distinct primitives, quantum homomorphic encryption (concerning the security of only data) and universal blind quantum computation (concerning the security of data and the computing algorithm). These primitives have immense applicability in problems like secure cloud computing, secure quantum variational algorithms, quantum federated learning, and secure multiparty computation. However, no software tools exist for the rapid prototyping of such protocols, hindering the academic interrogation for potential applications. In this paper, we describe the development of the first such library for transpiling circuits written in Qiskit to its blind counterpart, which can then be delegated in a client-server architecture without revealing the client's data and/or computation. The proposed library is designed in modular and reusable component layers, enabling easier scalability to newer BQC primitives and robustness against changes in underlying primitives. We show the implementation of these primitives to a blind variational quantum classifier for the IRIS dataset.

DFT+U+V is equivalent to DFT+U with density-dependent hybridized projectors

Authors: Edward Linscott, Alberto Carta, Nicola Marzari
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18071v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · Kondo & dissipative impurity 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This work mathematically proves that the inter-site $+V$ correction in $ ext{DFT}+U+V$ can be understood as an effective modification of the on-site Hubbard interaction ($+U$). This equivalence holds when using density-dependent, hybridized projectors, providing crucial physical insight into how electron correlation and covalency interact in strongly correlated materials.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on solid-state physics, understanding how an effective Hamiltonian (like DFT+$U$) can be rigorously mapped onto a more complex correlated model ($ ext{DFT}+U+V$) using projection techniques is highly relevant for developing accurate many-body Hamiltonians in quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The authors aim to provide a formal justification for inter-site $+V$ corrections added to DFT+$U$, which are necessary because standard $ ext{DFT}+U$ over-localizes charge and breaks covalent bonds.

Main result. To first order in $V/U$, the full $ ext{DFT}+U+V$ potential is shown to be exactly equivalent to an on-site $ ext{DFT}+U$ calculation performed using density-dependent, hybridized Hubbard projectors.

Method. The equivalence is demonstrated by defining a specific anti-Hermitian generator ($\hat{S}$) that relates the energy calculated with hybridized projectors to the full $ ext{DFT}+U+V$ energy in a perturbative expansion.

Model / system. The study applies to materials containing localized $d$ or $f$ electrons, such as transition metal oxides (e.g., NiO). The theoretical framework involves extending standard DFT+$U$ with an inter-site potential term ($\hat{V}$).

Key observables. Potential matrix elements comparing $ ext{DFT}+U+V$ to approximations using hybridized projectors; the degree of covalency enhancement.

Important parameters / regimes. $U$ (Hubbard parameter), $V$ (inter-site correction strength); the perturbative limit $V/U \ll 1$.

Assumptions / limitations. The equivalence is rigorously proven only to first order in $V/U$; using frozen projectors, a common practice, causes the formal equivalence to break down.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the evolution of Hubbard projectors (original vs. hybridized) for NiO, showing how hybridization incorporates neighboring orbital weights. Other figures compare potential matrix elements across different approximations and k-points.

Paper structure. The paper develops the equivalence by first establishing the formal relationship using a continuous parameter $\alpha$, then demonstrating that frozen projector approaches fail to reproduce the full potential, ultimately confirming the validity of the hybridized projector approach to first order in $V/U$.

Abstract

Hubbard-corrected density-functional theory (DFT+$U$) is a popular tool for first-principles modeling of materials with localized $d$ or $f$ electrons, but its on-site corrections tend to over-localize charge and break covalent bonds. Inter-site $+V$ corrections were introduced to counter this and are now widely used, but a formal justification has been lacking. Here we show that -- to first order in $V/U$ -- inter-site corrections are exactly equivalent to on-site DFT+$U$ evaluated on a density-dependent redefinition of the Hubbard projectors, hybridized with those of neighboring sites, providing insight into the explicit mechanism by which $V$ affects covalency. If the projectors are held frozen, as is common practice, the equivalence partially breaks down. Beyond reinterpreting the formalism, these results sharpen the questions of how $V$ should be computed and what the Hubbard subspaces fundamentally are.

Formal Verification of Continuous-Variable Quantum Programs

Authors: Stefanie Muroya, Thomas A. Henzinger
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.17714v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This paper introduces a novel formal semantics and the first Hoare logic specifically designed for Continuous-Variable Quantum Computing (CQC). By restricting state spaces and using polynomial assertions, the authors overcome mathematical obstacles related to infinite dimensions. The resulting tool allows rigorous verification of CQC algorithms and quantifies the resources needed for classical simulation.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides the necessary mathematical rigor—a verifiable programming model—for advancing quantum computation theory beyond discrete qubits into continuous variable systems relevant for modern photonic hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to establish a formal semantics and develop the first Hoare logic for Continuous-Variable Quantum Computing (CQC). This addresses the technical challenges posed by infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces and unbounded measurement outcomes inherent to CQC.

Main result. The authors successfully provide a sound and relatively complete Hoare logic for CQC, along with a symbolic weakest-precondition calculator. This tool has been shown to verify textbook CQC algorithms and compute resource requirements for classical simulation.

Method. They overcome technical hurdles by restricting the state space to the Schwartz subspace and basing assertions on finite polynomials over canonical observables (position and momentum). The logic interprets implication as the subset relation on sets of quantum states.

Model / system. The model is Continuous-Variable Quantum Computing (CQC), which operates on qumodes described by infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Key components include canonical observables ($X, P$) and the use of Schwartz density operators to ensure convergence for expected values.

Key observables. Canonical observables ($\hat{b}_X, \hat{b}_P$), polynomial assertions built from these observables, and the number operator ($\hat{N}_{q0}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The accuracy of classical simulation is bounded using the Chebyshev-Cantelli inequality, which determines the required truncation dimension based on desired error bounds.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes that CQC programs are straight-line (no loops or conditionals) and restricts analysis to states within the Schwartz subspace to ensure mathematical tractability.

Figures summary. Figure 11 illustrates the weakest precondition derived for the number operator under various single-mode instructions, alongside the Chebyshev-Cantelli inequality used to bound simulation accuracy. Table 1 lists verified gate decompositions.

Paper structure. The paper develops a formal semantics by defining the logic's components (state space restriction, assertion language) and then implements a symbolic calculator to derive weakest preconditions for various CQC operations, culminating in verification examples and resource estimation.

Abstract

We provide a formal framework for Continuous-Variable Quantum Computing (CQC). While CQC is supported by photonic quantum hardware, we are not aware of a formal semantics for continuous-variable quantum programs nor of a unary Hoare logic for their verification. There are several technical obstacles to extending to CQC any of the formal frameworks available for Discrete-Variable Quantum Computing (DQC). Most importantly, continuous-variable quantum programs act on {\em infinite-dimensional} Hilbert spaces; their measurement outcomes are often {\em unbounded} and have expected values that are defined by an improper integral (or an infinite series), which may not converge. We overcome these challenges to give a formal semantics to a universal programming language for CQC and to provide the first Hoare logic for CQC. The assertions of our logic are built from polynomials over canonical observables. Besides proving relative completeness, we implement a symbolic weakest-precondition calculator for CQC based on our logic. Our tool has successfully verified CQC algorithms from textbooks and calculated their approximation errors for physically realizable implementations, proved the correctness (i.e., equivalence) of gate decompositions for CQC hardware, and computed the resource requirements (i.e., number of photon-number states) for achieving a desired accuracy in the classical simulation of continuous-variable quantum programs.

Summary. This paper develops a sophisticated model to calculate the residual micromotion experienced by trapped ions due to imperfections in the trap's RF driving fields. By combining circuit theory with finite-element simulations, the authors provide a geometry-resolved projection that quantifies how small capacitance mismatches translate into measurable ion motion. This is vital for achieving high-precision quantum operations in trapped-ion quantum computing architectures.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial theoretical tool for experimentalists working with trapped ions, moving beyond simple resonator characterization to predict systematic errors arising from electrode geometry and RF imbalance.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study addresses how residual RF field imbalances, which are not captured by common-mode metrics, project onto measurable micromotion of trapped ions in a dual-RF blade trap.

Main result. The authors successfully developed a geometry-resolved projection method showing that even small capacitance mismatches (fF scale) can lead to tens of nanometers of projected ion micromotion.

Method. The methodology combines a two-node differential RF circuit model with single-electrode finite-element simulations to factorize the error source into geometric and electrical components.

Model / system. The physical system is a trapped ion setup utilizing a same-phase dual-RF blade trap geometry. The analysis models the coupling between the RF driving fields, electrode capacitances, and the resulting force on the trapped ion.

Key observables. Projected micromotion amplitude (nm), differential field coefficients ($ ext{V m}^{-1}$ per $V_d$), and effective differential capacitance scale ($C_{ ext{diff,bal}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Capacitance mismatch ($\Delta C$, e.g., 10 fF), loaded quality factor ($Q_{\mathrm{loaded}}=600$), and common peak voltage (e.g., 100 V).

Assumptions / limitations. All reported displacements are projections based on the model; no direct RF-bench or ion-side validation is claimed.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the factorization of the RF-to-ion mapping ($ ext{Eres} = \alpha_{ ext{diff}} H_{ ext{diff}} V_c$) and quantify how a specific capacitance mismatch translates into measurable micromotion, comparing balanced vs. unbalanced scenarios.

Paper structure. The paper establishes that common metrics are insufficient, develops the combined circuit/geometry model to factorize the error source, calculates key coefficients ($\alpha_{ ext{diff}}, H_{ ext{diff}}$), and then applies this framework to quantify micromotion for representative mismatch values. Supplementary sections analyze thermal and DC bypass sensitivities.

Abstract

Common-mode metrics of a high-$Q$ helical resonator do not determine the residual ion-side field in a dual-electrode drive. We combine a two-node differential RF model with single-electrode finite-element bases to obtain a computation-only, geometry-resolved projection for a same-phase blade trap. For a 3.5 pF external load per branch, the model gives a total effective branch capacitance of 7.640 pF and an HWHM-equivalent full branch-difference scale of 12.7 fF at $Q_{\mathrm{loaded}}=600$. The seven-segment geometry gives center and axial-RMS differential field coefficients of 640 V m$^{-1}$ and 635 V m$^{-1}$ per differential peak volt. A representative 10 fF mismatch with an effective 0.1 pF balance scale projects to 44.5/44.1 nm center/RMS $^{171}\mathrm{Yb}^{+}$ micromotion at 100 V common peak voltage. Supplementary thermal, bypass-admittance, and tested numerical cases characterize model sensitivity. All reported displacements are projections; no RF-bench or ion-side validation is claimed.

Summary. This work develops a computational pipeline to convert optical images of nanowire networks into mathematical graphs. Main result. Experimental assemblies exhibit topological similarities to both small-world and modular structures, suggesting complex connectivity patterns beyond simple random models.

Model / system. The system under study is Silver Nanowire Networks (AgNWNs), which are self-assembled structures studied for their signal-processing capabilities, mimicking neuromorphic elements. Self-assemblies of tunable units are being intensively studied as physical systems with signal-processing capabilities. It is thus possible to study graphs' metrics, such as the degree distribution, community size, clustering coefficient, and path-length, to compare the experimental assemblies' attributes to those of topological models of reference. Small-world, modular, and scale-free are well-known structures in the field of mathematical graphs. For that reason, this communication also studies the impact of artificially removing junctions from the resulting graphs on the previously calculated clustering coefficient and path length.

Summary. This work uses inelastic X-ray scattering and DFT calculations to examine phonon behavior in Chromium as pressure approaches its antiferromagnetic quantum critical point. The key finding is that the observed phonon anomalies are robustly linked to Fermi surface nesting, suggesting that the SDW ordering mechanism is electronic rather than being driven by lattice instabilities (phonons) near $P_c$. This refines our understanding of itinerant magnetism at QCPs.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter magnetism, the interplay between lattice dynamics (phonons) and electronic instabilities (SDW/CDW) near a quantum critical point involves many-body physics relevant to strongly correlated systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates phonon dynamics in Chromium under high pressure, specifically addressing whether lattice anomalies (Kohn anomalies) play a major role near the quantum critical point (QCP).

Main result. The persistence of Kohn anomalies and the robustness of Fermi surface nesting suggest that these anomalies do not drive the antiferromagnetic ordering at high pressures.

Method. The research combines inelastic X-ray scattering experiments with ab-initio Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations using DFPT for phonon dispersion analysis.

Model / system. The system is Chromium (Cr), an archetype material exhibiting itinerant antiferromagnetic spin density wave (SDW) ordering, studied under varying pressures up to 15.6 GPa.

Key observables. Phonon dispersion curves, Kohn anomalies (phonon softening), and the evolution of the Fermi surface nesting function ($\chi''(\mathbf{q})$).

Important parameters / regimes. Critical pressure ($P_c \approx 10$ GPa), high pressures up to $15.6$ GPa, and low temperatures.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the SDW formation is the primary order parameter, and Kohn anomalies are primarily driven by electronic susceptibility rather than direct lattice instability near the QCP.

Figures summary. Figures show phonon spectra/dispersion curves at ambient and high pressure along $\Gamma ext{H}$ and $\Gamma ext{N}$, illustrating the persistence of softening features despite crossing $P_c$.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the background on SDW formation in Cr, presents experimental measurements of phonon anomalies under pressure, compares these findings with DFT calculations showing robust nesting, and concludes that electronic susceptibility dictates the QCP behavior.

Abstract

Fermi surface nesting is key to understanding the origin of the itinerant antiferromagnetic spin density wave in Chromium. At very low temperature, this density wave is destroyed above the critical pressure $P_c\approx$10~GPa, defining a quantum critical point. Strong electron-phonon coupling induces a small phonon softening close to the $H-$point of the Brillouin zone as well as close to the $H$-point. These phonon anomalies are known as Kohn anomalies. They remain unchanged down to 50K and are known to be present in both the paramagnetic and the antiferromagnetic phases. This paper presents inelastic X-ray scattering experiments on the phonon dispersion curves under pressure, up to $P=$15.6~GPa far above the critical pressure $P_c$. The softening of the well-known phonon anomalies is still present which leads to conclude that they do not play a major role at the antiferromagnetic ordering. Based on ab-initio calculation, the robustness of the Fermi surface nesting under pressure, explain the presence of these Kohn anomalies above the critical pressure.

Representation-Dependent Machine Learning of the Isotropic-Nematic Transition in the Lebwohl-Lasher Model

Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.16481v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper uses machine learning to study the isotropic-nematic phase transition in the Lebwohl-Lasher model. It demonstrates that unsupervised methods fail if raw molecular data are used because global rotational symmetries obscure the physical signal. However, by transforming the input into a rotationally invariant representation, the ML techniques successfully recover clear signatures of the phase transition.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical statistical mechanics, the core methodological lesson—that input representation must respect underlying symmetries for feature extraction—is highly relevant when designing quantum state representations or observables for ML analysis in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates whether machine learning detection of phase transitions depends critically on whether the input representation preserves the underlying symmetries of the physical system.

Main result. Unsupervised ML methods (PCA, CAE) fail to detect the transition using raw molecular configurations but successfully recover transition signatures when the input is transformed into a rotationally invariant local-correlation representation.

Method. The authors compare unsupervised learning techniques (PCA and CAE) against supervised regression (CNN) applied to data from Monte Carlo simulations of the Lebwohl-Lasher model.

Model / system. The system studied is the three-dimensional Lebwohl-Lasher (LL) model, which models nematic liquid crystals exhibiting a weak first-order isotropic--nematic phase transition. The Hamiltonian interaction is apolar and possesses continuous rotational degeneracy.

Key observables. Scalar nematic order parameter ($S$), bimodal coexistence distributions, leading PCA scores, and CAE latent coordinates.

Important parameters / regimes. Lattice sizes ($L=50, 60, 80$) and the coupling constant ratio $J/k_B T = 1$.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption tested is that for unsupervised ML to work on systems with continuous symmetries, the input representation must be invariant under those symmetry transformations.

Figures summary. Figures compare thermodynamic benchmarks ($\langle S angle$ vs $T$, $P(S)$) against results from PCA/CAE using raw data (showing failure) versus bond-$P_2$ data (showing success), alongside CNN regression plots.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical model and transition, then systematically tests ML methods: first on raw configurations (failure), followed by symmetry-adapted representations (success for unsupervised learning), concluding with a comparison to supervised prediction accuracy.

Abstract

Machine-learning detection of phase transitions depends not only on the learning algorithm, but also on whether the input representation preserves the symmetries of the system. We examine this for the weak first-order isotropic--nematic transition of the three-dimensional Lebwohl--Lasher model, whose apolar and continuously degenerate nematic phase makes raw molecular configurations a challenging input for unsupervised learning. Principal component analysis (PCA) and a convolutional autoencoder (CAE) fail to identify the transition from raw configurations because rotationally equivalent nematic states can appear far apart in the input space. When the same configurations are transformed into a rotationally invariant local-correlation representation, both methods recover transition-sensitive signatures and bimodal coexistence distributions. A supervised three-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN), by contrast, accurately predicts the scalar order parameter from raw configurations when given order-parameter labels. The Lebwohl-Lasher model therefore separates unsupervised phase discovery from supervised order-parameter regression and shows that symmetry-respecting input representations are needed for unsupervised machine learning in orientationally ordered systems.

The History of Hilbert-Space Formulations of Classical Physics

Authors: Jacob A. Barandes
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.17408v1

Summary. This paper reviews two ways of applying Hilbert space techniques—Koopman-von Neumann and 'classical wave functions'—to describe classical mechanics. It clarifies that although mathematically equivalent, these approaches are conceptually distinct, and it corrects historical inaccuracies regarding their origins. The work is vital for understanding the mathematical foundations connecting classical phase space dynamics to quantum formalism.

Why it may be interesting. It provides deep insight into the formalisms used to bridge classical dynamics with quantum mechanical tools, which is crucial for understanding semiclassical approximations in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to explain and distinguish between two distinct Hilbert-space formulations—the Koopman–von Neumann (KvN) approach and the method of 'classical' wave functions—applied to classical Hamiltonian mechanics.

Main result. While both methods yield mathematically equivalent Hilbert spaces, they are conceptually distinct; furthermore, the paper provides a detailed historical correction regarding the attribution of the classical wave function formalism.

Method. The analysis involves comparing mathematical structures derived from Koopman's operator theory with those developed by later researchers like Schönberg, focusing on their respective evolution equations and associated inner products.

Model / system. The system is classical Hamiltonian mechanics evolving on a phase space ($\Omega$ or $(q, p)$). The dynamics are governed by the Poisson bracket $\{H, \cdot\}$ leading to the Liouville equation for probability density $ ho$.

Key observables. Probability density ($ ho$), Koopman functions ($\varphi$), and complex 'classical' wave functions ($\psi$).

Important parameters / regimes. The Hamiltonian $H$ dictates the dynamics; the comparison highlights the formal analogy to quantum mechanics, particularly in the classical limit.

Assumptions / limitations. The paper critically analyzes historical claims, noting that Koopman and von Neumann did not postulate the existence of classical wave functions evolving via a Schrödinger-like equation.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by first detailing the KvN formulation, then introducing the conceptually separate 'classical wave function' method (citing Schönberg et al.), and finally comparing their mathematical structures while correcting historical misattributions regarding who developed which formalism.

Abstract

Hilbert-space techniques are widely used not only for quantum theory, but also for classical physics. Two important examples are the Koopman-von Neumann (KvN) formulation and the method of ``classical'' wave functions. As this paper explains, these two approaches are conceptually distinct. In particular, the method of classical wave functions was not due to Bernard Koopman and John von Neumann, but was developed independently by a number of later researchers, perhaps first by Mario Schönberg, with key contributions from Angelo Loinger, Giacomo Della Riccia, Norbert Wiener, and E. C. George Sudarshan. The primary goals of this paper are to explain these two approaches, describe the relevant history in detail, and give credit where credit is due.

Summary. This paper tackles the Lee-Yang property for the Blume-Capel model, a system whose behavior is theoretically challenging due to its anisotropy term. The authors prove that strong ferromagnetic interactions can mathematically restore this crucial property—which governs phase transition analysis—even in parameter regimes where standard theorems predict failure. This provides significant theoretical insight into the critical nature of complex spin systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant for theoretical physicists studying phase transitions in complex quantum or classical spin systems, as understanding when such mathematical properties hold dictates the reliability of mean-field approximations and critical exponents.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is extending the Lee-Yang property—a condition on the zeros of the partition function related to phase transitions—to the Blume-Capel model in regimes where standard theorems fail.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that the ferromagnetic interaction can induce the Lee-Yang property for the Blume-Capel model even when the single-spin anisotropy term ($eta\Delta$) exceeds the threshold guaranteed by existing theorems.

Method. The analysis employs advanced mathematical techniques involving differential operators, stability conditions on derived functions (like $\Psi$), and relating these to the partition function zeros in the thermodynamic limit.

Model / system. The system is the Blume-Capel model, an extension of the Ising spin model with spins $S_i \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$, governed by a Hamiltonian including nearest-neighbor ferromagnetic interactions ($J_{ij}$) and a single-ion anisotropy term ($\Delta S_i^2$).

Key observables. The Lee-Yang property itself (zeros of the partition function lying on the unit circle) is the key diagnostic, alongside the stability of derived functions $G$ and $\Psi$.

Important parameters / regimes. Key parameters include the coupling constants ($J_{ij}$), the anisotropy field ($\Delta$), and the inverse temperature ($eta$). The critical regime involves comparing $eta\Delta$ to $\ln 2$.

Assumptions / limitations. The results are presented as sufficient conditions; the proof relies on establishing the stability of certain mathematical constructs derived from the partition function, which is an extension beyond the strict bounds of existing theorems.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the phase diagram of the BC model and compares simulation/theoretical predictions for transition lines. Other figures compare different functions related to the Lee-Yang property across varying parameters.

Paper structure. The paper builds upon the established Lee-Yang theory, first identifying the failure point in the BC model ($eta\Delta > \ln 2$). It then develops a mathematical framework using differential operators and stability analysis (Chunks 1 & 2) to prove that ferromagnetic interactions can restore the property. Abstract

Abstract

Equivariant graph neural networks have proven effective tools for inference of material's properties directly from their structure. Traditionally, these have been applied such that they respect full $O(3)$ equivariance, so that any rotation or reflection of the input structure is respected in the model's output. While this works for general arrangements of atoms, additional symmetries of atomistic systems are left unleveraged. Furthermore, any symmetries of the filter functions are implicitly learned from the full dataset and not strictly enforced. In this work, we introduce point-group symmetry aware equivariant graph neural networks (PGEqNN) for materials science, with filter functions aligned with symmetry-aware indices for greater granularity in predictive tasks. With this architecture, we show that most of the predictive power of equivariant networks for tensorial elastic and dielectric datasets lies in the trivial subspaces of the point-group adapted bases. Exploiting this, an $A_1$-restricted variant matches or improves on its full point-group and $SO(3)$-partitioned counterparts while training fewer active parameters, yielding leaner models of equal accuracy.
