Summary. This paper introduces a novel, pump-free method for generating microwave-optical Bell pairs, which is crucial for quantum transduction linking superconducting circuits to optical networks. By leveraging spin or atomic systems coupled to optical and microwave cavities, the authors demonstrate protocols that maintain high fidelity and operational rates while circumventing the noise issues associated with traditional optical pumping techniques.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it tackles the fundamental engineering challenge of quantum interconnects by providing detailed theoretical protocols for generating high-quality entangled pairs between microwave and optical domains using solid-state and atomic platforms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving quantum transduction—the coherent conversion of quantum states between microwave and optical frequencies—while avoiding detrimental effects from strong optical pumping.

Main result. The authors propose and analyze a pump-free microwave-optical Bell-pair source (MO-NODE) that generates heralded Bell pairs using spin or atomic systems, achieving high fidelity and kilohertz-range rates.

Method. The method involves engineering the entanglement generation via resonant coupling between a spin/atom system and microwave/optical cavities, utilizing time-bin encoding and cavity QED principles.

Model / system. The work models three distinct physical implementations: single color centers, atomic ensembles (e.g., 87Rb), and phonon-mediated systems. The underlying physics is described using cavity QED Hamiltonians and decay rate analyses.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on approximations such as the critical-coupling limit, adiabatic elimination of cavity/phonon modes, and assuming the coupling rate exceeds intrinsic decoherence rates.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the need for pump-free transduction, detailing the single-spin protocol, generalizing to ensemble and phonon-mediated systems, and analyzing leakage probabilities in inhomogeneous environments.

Abstract

The coherent conversion between microwave and optical photons, known as quantum transduction, is critical for connecting superconducting processors to optical networks. Existing methods are limited by complications associated with optical pumping. We propose a pump-free microwave-optical Bell-pair source for teleportation-based transduction. Using a spin or atomic system resonantly coupled to optical and microwave cavities, the scheme generates loss-robust heralded Bell pairs. Across color centers, atomic ensembles, and phonon-mediated systems, this assembly achieves kilohertz-range heralding rates with high fidelity.

Summary. This work theoretically analyzes quantum critical solids characterized by 'floppy' transverse phonons, leading to a Lifshitz quantum critical point. Using RG techniques in $d=4-\epsilon$, the authors determine universal critical exponents and show how the dimensionality dictates the nature of the critical interactions. The findings are crucial for understanding the non-linear response of quantum materials near phase transitions.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed analysis of nonlinear elastic response and the RG flow near quantum critical points provides a powerful theoretical framework for understanding emergent quantum phases in condensed matter systems, which is relevant to many-body dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the universal critical behavior of quantum solids, specifically focusing on the fate of 'floppy' solids whose transverse phonons have a vanishing velocity.

Main result. The system is driven toward a strongly-coupled Lifshitz quantum critical point (QCP) in $d=3$, although the transition is generically first-order. The analysis reveals distinct dimensional behaviors, notably the vanishing of cubic nonlinearity in $d=2$.

Method. The study employs the Renormalization Group (RG) technique within an $\epsilon = 4-d$ expansion to analyze the effective field theory of elasticity.

Model / system. The system is modeled as a quantum solid described by an effective Lagrangian for transverse phonons, leading to a Lifshitz QCP. The analysis distinguishes between the vector nature of the phonons, which introduces cubic nonlinearities, and the scalar case.

Key observables. Nonlinear elastic response ($\epsilon \sim \sigma^{1/\delta}$), specific heat ($C_V \sim T d/z$), and the dynamic shear modulus ($\mu(\omega, T)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Dimensionality ($d$), the dynamic exponent ($z$), and the correlation length exponent ($ u$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the $\epsilon$-expansion and assumes that the critical behavior is visible within the Ginzburg region, despite the transition being generically first-order.

Figures summary. The figures illustrate the stability regions and RG flows of the coupling constants ($c_2^L, u^2, w_3$) in the $d=2$ limit, constrained by stability conditions.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by establishing the EFT for floppy solids, analyzing the RG flow in $d=4-\epsilon$ to find critical exponents, and then dedicating significant sections to the specific, non-trivial behavior observed in $d=2$ due to the vanishing of the cubic coupling.

Abstract

We study the fate of quantum solids with a vanishing velocity of transverse phonons. Such floppy'' solids exhibit a cubic elastic nonlinearity that is relevant in spatial dimensions $d<4$ and drives the system to a strongly-coupled Lifshitz quantum critical point in $d=3$, that we analyze in an $ε= 4-d$ expansion. The associated phase transition is generically first-order, preempting the system's full access to the strongly-coupled fixed point. Nevertheless, the critical data of this quantum critical point is accessible within a Ginzburg region in the vicinity of the transition. This cubic nonlinearity vanishes exactly in $d=2$, where the leading interactions are instead quartic and are marginal, leading to weak logarithmic singularities. We discuss possible realizations of thiscrystal Lifshitz criticality'' in quantum materials.

Summary. This paper investigates the holographic dual of a local Unruh-DeWitt detector in AdS space using the HKLL reconstruction program. It calculates entanglement harvesting by relating bulk detector interactions to smeared boundary operators. The work is significant for understanding how quantum information processes are encoded across the AdS/CFT boundary.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects detector physics (Unruh effect) in curved spacetime to holographic principles, providing a concrete calculation of quantum information measures like entanglement harvesting using advanced mathematical tools.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is determining the boundary dual description for a local Unruh-DeWitt detector situated in the bulk of Anti-de Sitter (AdS) space.

Main result. The boundary dual is found to be a smeared detector on the boundary, whose smearing profile is dictated by the HKLL reconstruction kernel. The framework successfully analyzes entanglement harvesting in AdS$_3$.

Method. The authors employ the HKLL reconstruction program to map bulk field correlators to boundary correlators, calculating entanglement harvesting via the two-detector density matrix to quadratic order in the coupling constant.

Model / system. The system involves Unruh-DeWitt detectors coupled to quantum fields in AdS space, analyzed using the AdS/CFT correspondence framework.

Key observables. Entanglement harvesting amount, the two-detector density matrix, and the relationship between bulk microcausality and boundary detector overlap.

Important parameters / regimes. The coupling constant $\lambda$, the dimension $\Delta$ of the dual operator, and the specific choice of the HKLL smearing kernel.

Assumptions / limitations. The reconstruction is treated as an approximate notion, valid in a semiclassical regime, and the analysis focuses on free quantum fields on a fixed AdS background.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the support of the kernel $K_{D_i}$ (the causal diamond $D_i$) and the corresponding bulk region $W[D_i]$ (the causal wedge).

Paper structure. The paper reviews bulk reconstruction, introduces the detector formalism, performs the entanglement harvesting calculations, and discusses the resulting physical implications.

Abstract

What is the boundary dual of an Unruh-DeWitt detector in anti-de Sitter space? We argue that this question is naturally answered by the HKLL reconstruction program. A local detector in the bulk can be associated with a smeared detector in the boundary, where the choice of smearing depends on the choice of HKLL kernel. We use this formalism to analyze entanglement harvesting in the Poincaré patch of AdS$_{3}$ for a bulk Unruh-DeWitt detector paired either with a boundary detector or with a second bulk detector. We compute the amount of harvesting in the standard way, namely by evaluating the two-detector density matrix to quadratic order in the coupling $λ$. Additionally, we address an apparent tension between microcausality for spacelike-separated bulk detectors and the fact that the dual HKLL detectors may directly overlap in the boundary theory.

Summary. This theoretical work investigates how entropy can drive first-order thermal transitions between competing Quantum Hall phases, such as integer and fractional QH states. By analyzing the free energy contributions from collective excitations like magnons and magnetorotons, the authors propose a mechanism explaining the observed thermal evolution in moiré graphene. This highlights the crucial role of thermal fluctuations in determining the ground state of strongly correlated electron systems.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on entropy-driven phase transitions in strongly correlated electron systems, particularly using soft modes like magnetorotons, provides a rich theoretical tool applicable to understanding emergent quantum phases in novel condensed matter platforms like moiré heterostructures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the mechanism driving first-order, finite-temperature thermal transitions between competing Quantum Hall (QH) regimes, specifically between extended integer and fractional QH states.

Main result. The transition is shown to be driven by the difference in the entropy associated with low-energy excitations (like Goldstone or magnetoroton modes), providing a plausible bulk mechanism for thermal evolution in moiré graphene.

Method. The analysis involves calculating the free-energy density difference ($\Delta f$) between the competing states, incorporating contributions from gapless Goldstone modes (e.g., magnons) and soft gapped modes (e.g., magnetorotons).

Model / system. The general framework is applied to Quantum Hall systems, with a specific focus on moiré rhombohedral graphene (mRG) exhibiting both integer and fractional QH states. The competition is analyzed using free energy minimization.

Key observables. Free-energy difference ($f_F - f_I$), transition temperature ($k_B T_c$), and the Hall conductivity jump at the first-order boundary.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$), energy gaps ($\Delta_R$), warping energy ($W$), and interaction strengths ($J$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on approximations such as treating magnetorotons as noninteracting bosons and assuming specific energy scale hierarchies (e.g., $\Delta_R \gg k_B T \gg W$).

Figures summary. Figure 1 schematically illustrates the T-g phase diagram for two competing QH regimes, and Table I summarizes the entropy-driven mechanisms for various QH transitions.

Paper structure. The paper develops a general theoretical framework for entropy-driven transitions, applies this to specific physical models (Coulomb, short-range, magnetoroton), and concludes with a detailed application to moiré graphene, analyzing the role of different excitation modes.

Abstract

Electronic states with coexisting Wigner-crystal order can sometimes exhibit a quantum Hall effect over a finite range of electron densities---i.e., exhibit ``extended'' quantum Hall (QH) plateaus in the absence of disorder. Such an extended quantum Hall state can then compete with other QH states over the same density range, allowing a first-order thermal transition between them. Here, we analyze several settings in which the entropy associated with Goldstone modes (e.g., magnons or phonons) or soft gapped modes (e.g., magnetoroton) drives a finite-temperature transition between competing QH regimes. Applying this framework to moiré rhombohedral graphene, we argue that a soft magnetoroton in a fractional quantum anomalous Hall state provides a plausible bulk mechanism for the observed thermal evolution from an extended integer QH to a fractional QH regime.

Summary. This paper introduces a novel, systematic computational method to calculate nonlinear electron-phonon interactions of any order directly from first principles. By extending beyond linear approximations, the authors show that these higher-order couplings are crucial for accurately predicting key properties like polaron binding energies. This methodology promises to advance the study of charge transport and excited-state dynamics in various materials.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter, the rigorous, systematic, and ab initio nature of developing a general computational framework for higher-order interactions is highly relevant to modeling complex, non-adiabatic dynamics in quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop a first-principles theory and computational method to calculate nonlinear electron-phonon interactions of arbitrary order, overcoming the limitations of existing linear-coupling approximations.

Main result. The authors present a systematic framework to compute second-order (and higher) electron-phonon coupling matrix elements, demonstrating that these nonlinear terms are essential for achieving quantitative accuracy in polaron formation energies.

Method. The method combines unit-cell calculations of electron wavefunctions with supercell calculations of phonon perturbations, allowing for the calculation of complex Born-von-Kármán integrals.

Model / system. The physics centers on electron-phonon coupling, which governs phenomena like superconductivity, polaron formation, and carrier dynamics. The calculations are applied to representative materials including diamond, lithium fluoride, and graphite.

Key observables. Second-order electron-phonon coupling matrix elements ($g^{(2)}$), electron quasiparticle energy shifts (e.g., Debye-Waller correction), polaron formation energies, and hopping barriers.

Important parameters / regimes. The calculation is sensitive to the order of coupling (second-order being a key focus) and the nature of the exchange-correlation functional used (semilocal or nonlocal).

Assumptions / limitations. The method is designed to overcome the limitations of the rigid-ion approximation (RIA) and the computational difficulty of calculating off-diagonal, nonlinear coupling terms.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes, but the methodology involves mapping unit-cell wavevectors to the reciprocal space of the BvK supercell.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical necessity of nonlinear couplings, then details the novel computational framework combining unit-cell and supercell calculations to evaluate the required coupling integrals, and finally validates the method by computing $g^{(2)}$ for three distinct material classes.

Abstract

Electron-phonon interactions underpin a variety of phenomena, ranging from transport and superconductivity to polarons and ultrafast carrier dynamics. Despite being one of the most intensely studied subjects in condensed matter physics, research on electron-phonon physics mostly focused on linear, first-order couplings. Second- and higher-order nonlinear couplings are commonly ignored because their calculations are too demanding and we lack computational frameworks that can provide both diagonal and off-diagonal coupling matrix elements. In this work, we report a theory and computational method for computing nonlinear electron-phonon interactions of any order in real materials. Our approach combines the advantages of unit-cell calculations of electron wavefunctions and supercell calculations of phonon perturbations, is systematically improvable, can be used with either semilocal or nonlocal exchange-correlation functionals, and is amenable to Wannier-Fourier interpolation. As a first proof of concept, we illustrate this method by computing second-order electron-phonon coupling matrix elements in diamond, lithium fluoride, and graphite as representative nonpolar semiconductors, polar semiconductors, and metals, respectively. Furthermore, we generalize the ab initio polaron equations to second-order electron-phonon couplings, and we show that second-order couplings are essential to achieve quantitative accuracy in polaron formation energies and hopping barriers. The present methodology will find application in the study of all properties and phenomena that are currently being investigated within the linear electron-phonon coupling approximation, from phonon-mediated superconductivity to excited-states dynamics, both in harmonic and anharmonic systems.

Summary. This paper introduces MEOWN, a novel, physics-informed machine learning framework designed to predict where a muon will stop inside a crystal lattice. Abstract

Muon-spin rotation/relaxation is one of the most powerful microscopic probes for understanding magnetic order, spin dynamics, superconductivity, and complex magnetic phases in emerging quantum materials. Quantitative interpretation depends critically on the accurate identification of the muon stopping site, a problem traditionally addressed using density functional theory-based structural relaxation. However, conventional DFT approaches are computationally demanding, time-consuming, and require extensive calculations. Here, we develop MEOWN (Muon Engine for Optimized Weighted Networks), a machine-learning-based, physics-informed computational framework that combines a Polarizable Unperturbed Electrostatic Potential (P-UEP) model with machine-learning-guided optimization and symmetry-driven relaxation to predict energetically favorable muon stopping sites in crystalline solids. MEOWN explicitly incorporates electrostatic interactions, electronic screening, polarization effects, and zero-point motion into the learning workflow, providing physically interpretable predictions with substantially reduced computational cost while maintaining physical consistency and scientific rigor. To validate the framework, we investigated several well-established benchmark materials, including MnSi, CoF2, CaF2, LiF, and NaF. The predicted muon stopping sites and dipolar fields are in close agreement with previously reported DFT+mu results, demonstrating the reliability and transferability of the approach across chemically diverse systems. Notably, MEOWN predicts the equilibrium muon stopping site within a few minutes, offering a significant speedup over conventional DFT-based calculations. This manuscript presents the theoretical foundations, computational methodology, and validation of MEOWN as a general-purpose software for rapid and reliable muon stopping-site prediction in crystalline materials.

Geometric View of Iterative Fixed-Node Dynamics

Authors: Pranav Kairon, Bryan K. Clark
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16308v1

Summary. This work provides a geometric and stability analysis of iterative fixed-node dynamics used to approximate quantum ground states in the presence of the Fermion sign problem. By analyzing the fixed points of the iterative map, the authors prove that the ground state is the stable attractor, while other approximations flow away from the true solution. This clarifies the theoretical foundations for sign-problem mitigation techniques.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous mathematical analysis of fixed-point dynamics provides deep insight into the convergence properties of variational methods used to tackle sign problems in strongly correlated quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the Fermion sign problem (FSP) in quantum many-body systems, specifically analyzing the convergence and stability of iterative fixed-node approximations.

Main result. The self-consistent iteration is shown to drive the system towards the true ground state fixed point, proving that the ground state fixed point is stable while excited state fixed points are unstable.

Method. The analysis involves studying the fixed points and stability of an iterative map derived from the lattice fixed-node Hamiltonian, utilizing linearization techniques and spectral radius analysis.

Model / system. The study applies to quantum many-body systems governed by Hamiltonians, comparing continuum electronic structure models with lattice models (e.g., spin-1/2 models, $J_1-J_2$ chains).

Key observables. Sign structure, energy convergence ($E_k - E_{k+1}$), spectral weight concentration, and the spectral radius ($ ho(J)$) of the Jacobian matrix.

Important parameters / regimes. The sign structure (chamber) of the ground state, the system size ($L$), and the initial sign-flip probability ($p$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis focuses on the flow of the iterative map independent of practical algorithmic implementation, and relies on linearization around fixed points.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the iterative flow towards the ground state in the correct sign chamber, show energy and spectral weight convergence, and plot the spectral radius $ ho(J)$ to confirm stability.

Paper structure. The paper develops the iterative map, proves the stability of the ground state fixed point, analyzes the instability of excited states, and confirms the reconstruction of ground state amplitudes through self-consistent iteration.

Abstract

The sign-structure of a quantum many-body ground state is a central quantity in fixed-node approaches to mitigating the Fermion sign problem. For continuum electronic-structure Hamiltonians, the correct sign-structure is sufficient to compute exact ground state properties; on the other hand, lattice fixed-node approaches retain an additional dependence on trial wave-function amplitudes which are improved upon non-optimally while preserving their sign structure. Here we consider an iterative map obtained by repeatedly replacing the fixed-node trial state with the ground state of its associated lattice fixed-node Hamiltonian (independent of whether and how a practical algorithm could implement this iteration). We show the fixed points of this map are eigenstates of the Hamiltonian and reduced Hamiltonian resulting in at most one fixed point in the interior of each sign chamber. We prove that the ground state fixed point is stable and that the boundary of the ground state sign chamber is repulsive. This shows the amplitude dependence beyond having the correct ground state sign-structure disappears under self-consistent iteration. In other sign chambers, energy descent can be obstructed by the imposed sign structure; this tension leads to the iteration driving select amplitudes to zero producing `support collapse' onto chamber boundaries and corresponding to reduced Hamiltonians. Excited-state fixed points are therefore provably unstable and flow toward lower-energy boundary fixed points. We show sign chamber boundaries have a directional stability; boundary points attractive in one direction are repulsive under a sign flip. By revealing the geometry and stability structure of iterative fixed-node dynamics, our results clarify the foundations of a central approach to the Fermion sign problem and motivate potential new algorithmic strategies.

Summary. This paper develops a quantum metrology protocol for atomic clocks limited by spontaneous emission noise. It identifies Spin Grid States (SGSs) as near-optimal probes that surpass standard entangled states like GHZ states. The authors propose a practical readout technique that leverages the grid structure of SGSs to nearly saturate the ultimate quantum sensitivity limit.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides concrete, experimentally implementable quantum states (SGSs) that overcome the limitations imposed by realistic noise channels (spontaneous emission) in quantum sensing platforms like atomic clocks.

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of maximizing precision in phase and frequency estimation using atomic clocks that are fundamentally limited by spontaneous emission noise.

Main result. Spin Grid States (SGSs) are identified as practical, near-optimal quantum probes that significantly enhance metrological sensitivity compared to standard states, especially for intermediate and large atomic ensembles.

Model / system. The system is an ensemble of N identical two-level atoms (qubits) undergoing spontaneous emission, modeled by a master equation incorporating jump operators. The analysis compares SGSs to GHZ and SSS states.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information ($F_\phi$), phase estimation variance ($\Delta\phi^2$), and the success probability of state discrimination ($P_{ ext{succ, PGM}}$).

Abstract

Entanglement can enhance precision in phase estimation and in frequency estimation with atomic clocks, but it remains a central challenge to identify useful states and measurements under realistic noise processes. Here, we study quantum metrology with an ensemble of atoms subject to spontaneous emission, which limits frequency estimation by constraining the useful interrogation time. We identify spin grid states (SGSs) as the relevant near-optimal probes beyond a crossover at an ensemble size of 51, where GHZ-like states cease to be optimal. SGSs display a periodic grid on the Bloch sphere and can be generated with two one-axis-twisting (OAT) operations separated by a collective rotation. By optimizing the quantum Fisher information over permutationally symmetric states, we find that SGSs perform close to the globally optimal probes for intermediate and large ensembles, which turn out to be spin GKP states. We further present a sequential readout strategy that nearly saturates the corresponding quantum Fisher information. This strategy uses an OAT echo to map spontaneous-emission events onto collective rotations, making the corresponding jump sectors distinguishable and allowing the measurement basis to be conditioned on the number of decay events. Together, these results show that SGSs provide a practical route to quantum-enhanced phase and frequency metrology in atomic ensembles limited by spontaneous emission.

Summary. This paper investigates the fundamental limits of frequency stability in mechanical resonators, finding that the dominant $1/f$ noise floor scales universally with the material and geometry, independent of the device's physical volume. The research establishes a key invariant quantity that suggests the noise is governed by intrinsic material properties rather than simple geometric scaling. Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical mechanics and materials science, the analysis of noise sources, scaling laws, and fundamental limits of measurement precision touches upon concepts of decoherence and noise floor characterization relevant to quantum sensing.

Main result. The excess $1/f$ noise floor, characterized by the product $h^{-1} V_{\epsilon}$, is found to be remarkably independent of the device's physical volume over many decades of size.

Method. The study analyzes a large dataset of mechanical resonators, fitting the scaling of the noise floor ($\sigma_A$) against device volume ($V$) and comparing results against theoretical scaling predictions.

Figures summary. Figures plot the $1/f$-floor noise versus participation volume, demonstrating a volume-invariant trend, and analyze scaling trends against theoretical predictions.

Abstract

Mechanical frequency-shift sensors have never reached their fundamental thermomechanical or quantum limits. Every material, size, and transduction scheme yet examined yields a $1/f$ floor 1-3 orders higher, with a flicker-noise coefficient $h_{-1} \propto 1/V_{\varepsilon}$. Surveying the published record, I find the invariant $h_{-1}\,V_{\varepsilon} \equiv σ_A ^2\, V_{\varepsilon}/(2\ln 2)$ independent of device size across 13 decades; volume-independent noise is excluded by 9$σ$, site-averaging by 3.8$σ$. No $Q$ dependence appears: reported $Q^{-n}$ laws scale equivalently when $Q$ and volume co-vary; only volume scaling survives when they do not. Abstract

The mechanical response of a moving probe in an active bath reflects both the dynamics of the bath and how the probe couples to it. For a probe moving at velocity $\mathbf V$ and interacting weakly with an ideal active bath, we show that the trajectory statistics of a free bath particle determine a density response from which the bath force on the probe follows. The probe-particle interaction determines how this response is weighted over wave vector $\mathbf q$, while the probe motion selects frequencies $ω_{\mathbf q}=\mathbf q\cdot \mathbf V$. This trajectory-response relation separates the bath dynamics from the wave-vector weighting, so neither the stationary state nor force correlations need to be recalculated for each probe interaction and velocity. Applying this relation to active-particle models, we prove that positive power transfer is excluded for active Ornstein--Uhlenbeck particles in all dimensions and for complete-reset run-and-tumble particles in $d\geq2$, while one-dimensional run-and-tumble particles and two-dimensional active Brownian particles possess modes that can transfer positive mechanical power. For a given probe, this response predicts drag reversal, spontaneous probe motion, and motion-induced density distortions, all in quantitative agreement with simulations. Varying the probe geometry changes how the same bath response is weighted, thereby selecting different moving states. Beyond the leading weak-probe, ideal-bath limit, higher orders in probe strength involve multi-interval trajectory statistics, while finite-density corrections involve interacting-particle dynamics. Our results connect the trajectory statistics of active bath particles to the mechanical response of a passive probe, providing a route to predict probe response from single-particle trajectory statistics measured in the absence of the probe.
