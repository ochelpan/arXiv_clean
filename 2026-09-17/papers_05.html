Eigenstate thermalization beyond the envelope: exact two-point overlap statistics in random free fermions

Authors: Zhiqiang Huang
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17037v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 1/5 · scars & prethermalization 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper solves the microscopic statistics of eigenstate overlaps in random free fermions, going beyond the smooth energy dependence predicted by ETH. By calculating the exact two-point covariance, the authors prove that the fine-grained quantum fluctuations are not determined by the smooth energy envelope. This provides a crucial, exactly solvable benchmark for understanding thermalization in many-body quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous, exactly solvable model to test the limits of ETH, offering concrete, non-trivial statistics for quantum fluctuations that are crucial for understanding thermalization in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine the microscopic statistics of overlaps between many-body eigenstates and a basis, going beyond the one-point statement provided by Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis (ETH).

Main result. The two-point covariance closes exactly at the Gaussian fixed point, demonstrating that the smooth ETH envelope does not determine the fine-grained two-point fluctuations.

Method. The authors use an exactly solvable random free-fermion ensemble to derive exact moment statistics for channel overlaps, comparing them to established random-matrix theory benchmarks.

Model / system. The system is random free fermions whose eigenstates are Slater determinants built from eigenvectors of a Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) single-particle Hamiltonian. This ensemble serves as an exactly solvable benchmark for testing theoretical structures.

Key observables. Two-point overlap covariance, normalized second-moment ratio ($q$), and the structure of the multi-resolvent correlation levels ($g_{ji}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The depth of the determinant ($k$), the number of fermionic modes ($N$), and the correlation correction terms ($g_{11}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the framework of a 'multi-resolvent dictionary' to separate the ETH function into a smooth baseline and fluctuation terms.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes reference tables comparing analytic ETH constructions with exact free-fermion results across different theoretical sectors.

Paper structure. The paper builds a multi-resolvent hierarchy, first analyzing the one-point statistics (flat intensity distribution), then deriving the exact two-point covariance using random-matrix theory, and finally establishing the structural identities that show the independence of the mean and fluctuation sectors.

Abstract

Eigenstate thermalization constrains the smooth dependence of observables on energy, but it does not by itself fix the microscopic statistics of the overlaps between many-body eigenstates and a chosen basis: the smooth envelope is a one-point statement, and the fluctuation field it leaves undetermined carries a structured two-point covariance. We establish this distinction, and solve that fluctuation field exactly, in random free fermions---an ensemble of Slater-determinant eigenstates built from a Gaussian orthogonal single-particle Hamiltonian, whose eigenstate thermalization was established by Magán. In this ensemble every channel overlap is a minor of a Haar-distributed orthogonal matrix, so its statistics follow from classical random-matrix theory. The one-point law is exactly flat for every channel, with an exact moment hierarchy that is not Porter--Thomas: small intensities are enhanced algebraically rather than by the exponential Porter--Thomas form, and the mean sector carries a negative, order-one correlation correction of purely normalization origin. The two-point covariance closes exactly at the Gaussian fixed point: it is organized by the number of one-body modes shared by two eigenstates and by the number of modes shared by two channels, and its energy-resolved form factorizes into this geometry times the classical convolution of the single-particle semicircle, from which the two-resolvent covariance follows by an integral transform. Exact finite-size computations confirm all closed forms, and the structural identities of the dictionary hold at machine precision. The results provide an exactly solvable microscopic realization of the two-point fluctuation sector underlying multi-resolvent descriptions of eigenstate thermalization: a smooth envelope does not determine fine-grained fluctuations.

Summary. This paper presents Clifford-stabilizer simulation, a novel, efficient algorithm for classically simulating the noisy preparation of logical magic states crucial for fault-tolerant quantum computation. By mapping complex non-Clifford noisy circuits onto equivalent Clifford circuits, the method allows for the exact benchmarking of large-scale quantum error correction protocols. This provides a vital tool for assessing the feasibility of useful FTQC.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on quantum computation, the development of efficient classical simulation techniques for complex, error-prone quantum circuits is a significant computational tool relevant to understanding resource scaling in quantum information.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is developing an exact and efficient classical simulation method to predict the performance of fault-tolerant quantum computation (FTQC) by simulating noisy logical magic state preparation.

Main result. The authors introduce Clifford-stabilizer simulation, an efficient algorithm that exactly maps non-Clifford noisy circuits to equivalent Clifford circuits, enabling benchmarking of large-scale magic state preparation up to fault distance 7.

Method. The method involves tracking the evolution of a Clifford-stabilizer group ($ ext{GC}$) to simulate noisy protocols, mapping non-Clifford operations to an equivalent Clifford circuit representation.

Model / system. The context is quantum error correction codes (e.g., surface codes) used in FTQC, focusing on preparing logical magic states required for non-Clifford gates. The simulation incorporates circuit-level Pauli errors and Pauli-stabilizer codes.

Key observables. Measurement outcome distributions, logical error rates, and acceptance rates for magic state cultivation protocols.

Important parameters / regimes. Fault distance ($f=3, 5, 7$), circuit-level Pauli error rates, and the size of the stabilizer group.

Assumptions / limitations. The simulation assumes a specific noise model (depolarizing channel) and utilizes a systematic approach to map non-Clifford dynamics onto a Clifford-stabilizer framework.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate gate-teleportation circuits for T-gate implementation and the structure of surface codes for various distances, while tables detail the cultivation protocols for $f=3, 5, 7$.

Paper structure. The paper builds conceptually by first defining the problem, introducing the Clifford-stabilizer group ($ ext{GC}$), establishing update rules, and finally applying the simulation framework to detailed protocols for magic state cultivation and error correction.

Abstract

The preparation of high-fidelity logical magic states is a crucial subroutine for universal fault-tolerant quantum computation (FTQC). Predicting the performance of FTQC and developing improved protocols rely on numerical methods to classically simulate logical magic state preparation in the presence of noise. Clifford logic on Pauli-stabilizer codes with circuit-level Pauli errors can be efficiently simulated using Pauli-stabilizer formalism, but the non-Clifford operations required to prepare logical magic states render generic simulation inefficient. We introduce Clifford-stabilizer simulation, an exact and efficient algorithm based on updating a Clifford-stabilizer group to simulate noisy preparation protocols for a broad class of logical magic states used to implement non-Clifford gates in the third level of the Clifford hierarchy under circuit-level Pauli errors. Clifford-stabilizer simulation applies to a range of operations that commonly appear in preparation protocols for such logical magic states, including Pauli-stabilizer measurements, logical Clifford measurements, and transversal non-Clifford gates. Our algorithm for Clifford-stabilizer simulation maps a non-Clifford circuit with sampled circuit-level Pauli errors to a Clifford circuit that exactly reproduces its measurement outcome distribution, achieving time and space complexities polynomial in relevant protocol parameters. We perform exact simulation of magic state cultivation up to fault distance 7 by Clifford-stabilizer simulation. Our method provides a route to perform exact benchmarking of large-scale logical magic state preparation protocols required for useful FTQC.

Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.16435v1

Summary. This paper provides an exact analytical method to calculate the characteristic function of work for bosonic systems driven by time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians. By reformulating the problem using symplectic geometry, the authors bypass the complex time-ordering issues inherent in quantum evolution. This framework successfully reproduces fundamental fluctuation theorems, making it a powerful tool for studying non-equilibrium quantum thermodynamics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, general, and exact framework for calculating non-equilibrium work statistics in quadratic bosonic systems, directly connecting quantum dynamics (symplectic geometry) to core principles of non-equilibrium thermodynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Deriving an exact analytical expression for the characteristic function of work in closed many-body bosonic systems governed by general time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians.

Main result. An exact analytical expression for the work characteristic function is derived, which allows for the calculation of key thermodynamic quantities like the free energy and work variance.

Method. The authors employ a formalism based on the inhomogeneous Metaplectic group, replacing the difficult time-ordering problem with the determination of a symplectic path in phase space.

Model / system. The system consists of closed many-body bosonic systems described by general time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians, which are analyzed using canonical variables in phase space.

Key observables. Characteristic function of work ($\chi(u)$), Helmholtz free energy, mean work, work variance, and the nonequilibrium lag.

Important parameters / regimes. The initial thermal equilibrium state ($ ho_0$), the time dependence of the Hamiltonian, and the symplectic path parameters.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary derivation assumes the initial state is an instantaneous thermal equilibrium state, although generalizations to non-equilibrium states are discussed.

Paper structure. The paper builds from the theoretical necessity of calculating work statistics in non-equilibrium processes, develops the mathematical machinery using the Metaplectic group and symplectic paths to derive the characteristic function, and finally applies this function to recover fundamental fluctuation theorems and thermodynamic relations.

Abstract

We derive an exact analytical expression for the characteristic function of work in closed many-body bosonic systems governed by general time-dependent quadratic Hamiltonians. This is made possible by adopting a unified prescription in which all operators appearing in the characteristic function are treated as elements of the inhomogeneous Metaplectic group. The esulting expression involves a single symplectic matrix, a single phase-space displacement, and a single accumulated phase. It holds for any number of degrees of freedom, arbitrary time dependence of the quadratic Hamiltonian, and any initial thermal equilibrium state of the system. The formalism trades the explicit time-ordering problem associated with the unitary evolution for the determination of a symplectic path in phase space, from which the corresponding driving Hamiltonian can be reconstructed. We also discuss the generalization to arbitrary nonequilibrium initial states within the two-projective-measurement scheme for quantum work. The same construction yields Loschmidt echoes and mixed-state total phases. Using the derived characteristic function, we obtain formulas for the Helmholtz free energy, the Jarzynski equality, the mean work, the work variance, and the nonequilibrium lag. Undisplaced Hamiltonians, sudden quenches, constant-Hessian protocols, and the adiabatic limit follow as special cases of the general result. As an illustration, we analyze a one-dimensional system constructed from an unconventional symplectic path and exhibiting physically relevant limiting cases. We then show how affine and purely symplectic contributions affect work statistics and irreversibility.

GAUGE: A Formal Framework for Measuring Cryptographic Security under Heterogeneous Adversary Cost Models

Authors: Bhanwar Gupta, Sanjeev Rana
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17281v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This paper introduces GAUGE, a formal framework to measure cryptographic security not by a single bit-count, but by a 'security profile' that maps across all possible adversary cost models. It mathematically proves that different cost models can lead to contradictory security rankings, providing an explicit, auditable method to quantify this model dependence. This is crucial for standards bodies to report security honestly when underlying assumptions about attacker resources are uncertain.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly about physical quantum systems, the rigorous mathematical framework for uncertainty quantification and model dependence (risk measures, profile analysis) is highly relevant to any field where underlying assumptions or measurement models introduce systematic, non-Gaussian uncertainty.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Current cryptographic security reporting relies on a single scalar bit-strength number, which is misleading because it depends entirely on the unstated adversary cost model used for pricing resources like time and memory.

Main result. The authors introduce GAUGE, a framework that models security as a 'security profile' (a function over all admissible cost models), proving that ranking reversals are structural properties rather than measurement errors.

Method. The framework formalizes price functionals over a cone of cost models, proving that security profiles are piecewise-linear and concave, and uses linear programming to certify the robustness of security rankings.

Model / system. The model is abstract, dealing with cryptographic schemes and the space of admissible adversary cost models ($\kappa$). It defines security as a function of these models, leading to the concept of a 'security profile' $S_K(\kappa)$.

Key observables. Security profile, Rating Trilemma, Robust/Reversing/Incomparable ranking status, Lattice-sieving cost drift (e.g., 9.79 bits/year).

Important parameters / regimes. Adversary cost model parameters (time, memory, quantum resources); time horizons ($ au$); decay rates ($\delta$); and the structure of the cost cone.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes the existence of a cone of admissible cost models and uses concepts like anchor-relative profiles to normalize comparisons.

Figures summary. Figure 1 conceptually maps cost models to security profiles; Table 1 compares schemes under multiple cost models, showing varying relative security costs.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem with scalar reporting, introduces the GAUGE profile framework, proves mathematical properties (concavity, linearity), develops the linear-programming certification procedure, and concludes with concrete evaluations on PQC standards and risk measures.

Abstract

Standards bodies report cryptographic security as a single number of bits, but this value depends on the adversary cost model used to price time, memory, and quantum resources. Different conventions can therefore produce different rankings of cryptographic schemes. GAUGE represents security as a function over admissible cost models, called a security profile. Comparisons then become comparisons between profiles, and ranking reversals become an explicit structural property rather than a measurement error. We formalize price functionals over a cone of adversary cost models, show that security profiles are piecewise-linear and concave, and prove a rating trilemma: when two profiles cross, no rating can simultaneously be faithful to underlying costs, total over comparable pairs, and independent of the chosen cost model. We provide a polynomial-time linear-programming procedure that certifies whether the ranking of two schemes is robust, reverses under admissible models, or is genuinely incomparable. We extend GAUGE with a two-layer risk measure combining stochastic cryptanalytic decay with uncertainty over the appropriate cost model. We evaluate the framework on NIST post-quantum standards, classical anchors, and a 25-year chronology of cryptanalytic breaks. The analysis certifies a ranking reversal for ML-KEM-512 versus AES-128 from a 4-5% shift in memory pricing, and measures a lattice-sieving cost drift of 9.79 bits per year over eight years. A hybrid X25519 + ML-KEM-768 handshake reduces combined-break probability twenty-fold at a 2.3 kilobyte cost. The artifact reproduces all tables and figures in under seven seconds. GAUGE provides an explicit and auditable framework for reporting cryptographic security under competing cost models.

Type: both · Category: disordered systems and neural networks · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17213v1

Summary. This work introduces SpiKIsing, a latent-variable model that separates the collective dynamics of neurons (modeled by an Ising process) from the continuous-time spiking process. It uses a variational EM scheme to jointly infer the underlying network couplings and emission parameters from raw spike data. The method is robustly validated, showing it can accurately recover network structure despite modeling mismatches.

Why it may be interesting. The framework uses concepts from statistical physics (Ising models, mean-field theory) to model complex, non-equilibrium biological dynamics, providing a rigorous mathematical structure for analyzing emergent collective behavior.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the inverse problem of inferring the directed effective interactions governing collective neuronal dynamics solely from recorded spike trains.

Main result. The proposed SpiKIsing model successfully recovers the sparse connection structure and correctly classifies neuronal types (excitatory/inhibitory) even when tested on data generated by different underlying dynamics.

Method. It employs a variational mean-field expectation-maximization (EM) scheme to jointly infer latent network activity (modeled by an Ising process) and continuous-time emission parameters from the observed spike trains.

Model / system. The system models neural spiking using a two-level latent variable approach: a discrete-time asymmetric kinetic Ising model governs the unobserved collective state, while continuous-time, history-dependent point processes generate the observed spikes conditioned on this latent state.

Key observables. Inferred coupling strengths (J), local fields (h), and emission parameters (e.g., $\lambda^+, \lambda^-$).

Important parameters / regimes. Latent time step ($\Delta t$), regularization strengths ($\gamma_1, \gamma_2$), and prior parameters ($eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary approximation is using a variational mean-field approach, which treats the continuous-time point-process likelihood as an effective observation field for the discrete Ising dynamics.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the full generative mechanism, parameter recovery validation on synthetic data, and the final inferred interaction matrix compared to ground truth.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the latent variable model, derives the variational EM scheme, details the updates (including forward-only approximations), and validates the method using both synthetic and complex conductance-based LIF network simulations.

Abstract

Inferring directed effective interactions from neuronal spike trains is a central inverse problem in statistical physics and computational neuroscience. Kinetic Ising models provide a tractable framework for this task, but their application to neural data typically requires binning spike trains into binary activity variables, discarding within-bin timing and conflating collective network dynamics with single-neuron history effects. We introduce SpiKIsing, a latent-variable model that separates these two levels of description. A discrete-time asymmetric kinetic Ising model describes collective network activity, while continuous-time, history-dependent point processes generate the observed spikes conditional on the latent states, accounting explicitly for refractoriness and post-spike recovery. We derive a variational mean-field expectation-maximization scheme in which the point-process likelihood enters as an effective observation field, enabling joint inference of latent activity, network couplings, and emission parameters. The framework extends naturally to maximum-a-posteriori inference with structured priors, including sparsity and a hierarchical extension favouring Dale-consistent outgoing interactions. We validate parameter recovery on matched synthetic data and test the method on spike trains generated by a recurrent conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire network. In this setting, SpiKIsing recovers sparse connection structure and correctly classifies all excitatory and inhibitory neurons despite the substantial mismatch between the data-generating dynamics and the SpiKIsing model.

Summary. This paper introduces a Quantum Lattice Boltzmann Method (QLBM) that replaces the non-unitary BGK collision operator with a unitary rotation operator. This approach successfully addresses the unitarity constraint while maintaining second-order accuracy relative to the classical method. The framework is fully realized at the gate level, providing a pathway to simulate fluid dynamics on quantum computers.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, unitary quantum circuit implementation for a classical computational method (LBM), which is highly relevant for simulating complex many-body dynamics on near-term quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is developing a unitary quantum analogue for the non-unitary BGK collision operator used in the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM).

Main result. The authors propose a novel Quantum Lattice Boltzmann Method (QLBM) using a unitary rotation collision operator that correctly approximates the BGK collision to $O(\delta t^2)$ in the continuum limit.

Method. The non-unitary BGK relaxation is replaced by a unitary rotation in the amplitude space spanned by the current and equilibrium distribution states, leading to gate-level quantum circuit construction.

Model / system. The method is applied to solve fluid dynamics equations using the LBM framework, specifically implemented for the D2Q5 (convection-diffusion) and D2Q9 (Navier-Stokes) models.

Key observables. Relative velocity errors ($E_u$), mass conservation ($\sum f^*_i = ho$), and the contraction of the state deviation angle $\phi$.

Important parameters / regimes. Relaxation time ($ au$), time step ($\delta t$), and the dimensionality/model (D2Q5 vs D2Q9).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the unitary analogue of the BGK collision is a rotation contracting the angle $\phi$ by a factor of $(1 - 1/ au)$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the sequential T-step quantum circuit for the D2Q5 model, showing state-dependent collision blocks interspersed with fixed streaming operators, and show error scaling confirming $O(\delta t^2)$ accuracy.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem (non-unitary BGK), proposes the unitary rotation operator, derives its form for D2Q5 and D2Q9, details the gate-level circuit construction (including reusable components for D2Q5), and validates the method via numerical error analysis.

Abstract

In the existing quantum algorithms for the lattice Boltzmann method, resolving the unitarity problem of the BGK collision operator from first principles remains a fundamental challenge, and a systematically constructed unitary alternative that reproduces the BGK relaxation structure to leading order has not yet been established in the literature. In this work, we propose a novel quantum lattice Boltzmann method (QLBM) based on the rotation collision operator, in which the non-unitary BGK relaxation is replaced by a unitary rotation in the amplitude space of the distribution function, and the corresponding gate-level quantum circuits are also constructed. Specifically, the rotation collision operator is developed for both the D2Q5 model of the convection-diffusion equation and the D2Q9 model of the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. It is worth noting that, for the D2Q5 model, the equilibrium-state preparation circuit can be precompiled and reused, and the resulting unitary blocks can be sequentially composed once the state-dependent collision parameters are specified. Finally, some numerical experiments are performed to validate the developed QLBM, demonstrating its accuracy and effectiveness.

Summary. This paper generalizes Holonomic Quantum Computation to $d$-dimensional qudits using the $d$-pod atomic configuration. It shows that this setup enables universal quantum gate sets for qudits using only simple optical or microwave pulse sequences. The work is significant for its resource efficiency and its systematic extension of geometric quantum control principles.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, resource-efficient theoretical blueprint for scaling quantum computation beyond qubits, directly relevant to experimental quantum control using trapped atoms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the systematic generalization of nonadiabatic Holonomic Quantum Computation (HQC) from standard qubit systems to higher-dimensional qudits ($d$-level systems).

Main result. It demonstrates that the $d$-pod configuration allows for the realization of universal holonomic single- and two-qudit gates using only optical or microwave pulses on trapped atoms or ions.

Method. The computation relies on exploiting non-Abelian geometric phases accumulated during cyclic evolution in the $d$-pod system, analyzed using the Grassmannian manifold.

Model / system. The physical model is the $d$-pod configuration, consisting of a single excited state coupled to $d$ ground states forming the computational subspace. The dynamics are governed by a time-dependent Hamiltonian.

Important parameters / regimes. The required cyclic evolution condition is $\int_0^ au \Omega(t)dt = \pi$. The complexity increases with $d$, with the qutrit ($d=3$) case analyzed in detail.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the use of the rotating wave approximation and monochromatic fields, focusing on the $d$-pod generalization of the $\Lambda$-scheme.

Abstract

Holonomic quantum computation (HQC) realizes quantum gates through non-Abelian geometric phases, providing an experimentally accessible approach to quantum control. While the nonadiabatic HQC framework has been extensively developed for three-level $Λ$ systems encoding qubits, its systematic extension to higher-dimensional qudits remains largely unexplored. In this work, we generalize nonadiabatic HQC to a $d$-pod configuration, where a single excited state is coupled to $d$ ground states, the latter forming the computational subspace. This scheme enables universal holonomic single- and two-qudit gates using only optical or microwave pulses on trapped atoms or ions, offering an efficient route to implement a discrete universal gate set with minimal pulse coordination. As an explicit example, we analyze in detail the qutrit ($d=3$) case, demonstrating compact realizations of single- and two-qutrit holonomic gates, each gate requiring at most two loops in the Grassmannian generated by at most three pulses.

Abstract

Hardware noise has been shown to significantly impact the accuracy of ADAPT-VQE, a ground state preparation algorithm. While previous work has studied the impact of noise on its parameter optimization step, its impact on the critical operator selection step remains comparatively unexplored. In this work, we examine the impact of a variety of noise channels on this step, using a linear H$_3$ molecule as a test case. Our results highlight how error mitigation can improve the performance of ADAPT-VQE and enable convergence in the presence of hardware noise, offering valuable insights into the implementation of the algorithm on near-term quantum hardware.

Summary. This theoretical work quantifies 'quantum imaginarity,' a measure of nonreal coherence, in the quantum atmosphere of a black hole. Abstract

Summary. This paper adapts the Density-Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) algorithm to solve the computationally demanding Hartree-Fock equations for very long chains. By compressing both the electronic state and the long-range interactions, the authors achieve near-linear scaling, making large-scale electronic structure calculations feasible on standard desktop hardware.

Why it may be interesting. This work demonstrates a powerful algorithmic adaptation of DMRG, extending its utility beyond standard correlated wavefunctions to tackle mean-field theories like HF for extended solids, which is crucial for simulating large-scale material properties.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the computational challenge of applying Hartree-Fock (HF) theory to very large electronic systems, which typically suffers from high polynomial scaling due to long-range interactions.

Main result. The authors successfully formulated HF as a Density-Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) sweep, achieving near-linear scaling for chains up to 100,000 atoms in a feasible time frame.

Method. They adapt the DMRG framework by compressing the occupied state and the long-range electron-electron interaction separately, reducing the complex HF calculation into a sequence of small, local Fock problems.

Model / system. The method is tested on uniform antiferromagnetic hydrogen chains, retaining the physical Coulomb interaction and its $1/r$ tail, using a basis set of Gaussian-type orbitals.

Key observables. Convergence time (hours for $10^5$ atoms), memory usage ($\approx 21$ GB), and the linear scaling of computation time with chain length.

Important parameters / regimes. Chain length up to 100,000 atoms; one-body tolerance of $10^{-8}$ Ha; local Fock matrix size remaining small ($\approx 45$ rows).

Assumptions / limitations. The use of the density-density electron-electron interaction approximation and the assumption that state compression (one-particle and MPO compression) is sufficient for accuracy.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the DMRG sweep process, showing the partitioning of the basis into blocks and the storage/retrieval of environments. Other figures detail time/memory scaling and energy convergence plots.

Paper structure. The paper details the formulation of HF as a DMRG sweep, describes the specific compression techniques (one-particle, MPO, density-space), presents computational results on scaling, and concludes with analysis of energy convergence and implementation details.

Abstract

We formulate Hartree--Fock as a density-matrix renormalization group (DMRG) sweep of a single Slater determinant, assuming a density--density electron--electron interaction. We compress the occupied state and long-range interaction separately, reducing each sweep step to a small local Fock problem. Exchange is retained throughout. Uniform antiferromagnetic hydrogen chains provide an almost ideal setting for this approach, while retaining the Coulomb interaction and its $1/r$ tail. We initialize and converge a chain of 100,000 electrons in 1.8 million spatial basis functions in about two hours on a 64-GB Mac mini, using less than a third of its memory. The work per sweep is nearly proportional to chain length, and the number of sweeps needed for convergence changes little from 1000 to 100,000 atoms. DMRG's compression of states and environments thus makes large HF calculations with long-range Hartree and exchange practical on an ordinary desktop computer.

Abstract

Nonlocal magic quantifies the minimum nonstabilizerness attainable under independent local unitary transformations on the two subsystems. Here, we use min-relative nonlocal magic (NLM) to characterize the crossover from ergodicity to many-body localization (MBL) in the random-field XXZ chain. These results show that NLM resolves Schmidt-spectrum structure not visible in the entanglement entropy and provides a complementary probe of ergodicity breaking.

Summary. This paper rigorously analyzes how conditioning a Branching Random Walk (a model for log-correlated Gaussian fields) on its endpoints being confined to a small region affects its correlations. Using Renormalization Group techniques, the authors prove that this conditioning induces an exponential decay of correlations with a characteristic length scale proportional to the confinement radius $r$. The results are generalized to important physical models like the sinh-Gordon and $\phi^4$ theories.

Why it may be interesting. The methods developed for analyzing correlation decay under strong spatial constraints (small ball events) are highly relevant for understanding critical phenomena and confinement in quantum field theories, which often involve Gaussian or log-correlated fields.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the correlation structure of a Branching Random Walk (BRW) when conditioned on all its endpoints lying within a small interval (a 'small ball event').

Main result. The conditioning leads to an exponential decay of correlations between the endpoints, with an optimal correlation length of $O(r)$.

Method. The core technique employed is the Renormalization Group (RG) analysis, which separates the effect of endpoint conditioning into relevant and irrelevant parts.

Model / system. The primary model is the BRW, which serves as a prototypical log-correlated Gaussian field and a mathematical stand-in for the Discrete Gaussian Free Field (DGFF). Generalizations apply to the sinh-Gordon model and $\phi^4$ models.

Key observables. Correlation $ ext{Cov}(\phi_{ au_1}, \phi_{ au_2})$ and the correlation length.

Important parameters / regimes. The radius $r$ of the small ball event, the depth $n$ of the walk, and the potential parameters ($\lambda, \alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. The conditioning on $\|\Phi\|_\infty \le r$ is an extremely low-probability event, and the analysis relies on separating the RG flow into relevant and irrelevant components.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the BRW conditioned on endpoints in $[-r, r]$, showing the initial steps behave like a branching Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) walk.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the main result for the BRW using RG, then generalizes this result to other hierarchical field theories, specifically applying it to the sinh-Gordon model and generalized $\phi^4$ models.

Abstract

Consider a branching random walk (BRW) of depth $n$ with standard Gaussian increments, conditioned on all endpoints lying in an interval of radius $r$ (which may depend on $n$), i.e. a small ball event. We prove that this conditioning results in exponential decay of correlations between the endpoints, with correlation length $O(r)$. Our proof is based on the renormalization group, which in this context captures the effect of the endpoint conditioning on earlier steps in the walk. This effect can be separated into so-called relevant and irrelevant parts, and in our setting the relevant part makes the conditioned BRW behave like a branching Ornstein-Uhlenbeck walk for the first $n-O(r)$ steps, leading to the correlation decay. The irrelevant part shrinks in a nontrivial manner, and the simplest argument to bound it uniformly only yields correlation length $O(r^2)$. To achieve the optimal $O(r)$ bound, we establish that the irrelevant part shrinks horizontally in space before decaying uniformly. The BRW is a prototypical example of a log-correlated Gaussian field, and it forms the basis of many hierarchical field theories in the mathematical formulation of quantum field theory. As such, we present our results in a general form which is applicable to other hierarchical field theories with confining potentials, of which the small ball conditioned BRW is an example. We apply our general result to two other models: first, the sinh-Gordon model which has a strictly convex potential, so at the level of covariances the model behaves like one with a quadratic potential, i.e. the model has a positive mass. Second, we consider generalizations of the $φ^4$ model with any power $α\geq 2$; as $α$ increases from $2$ to $\infty$, these power potentials naturally interpolate between the massive (quadratic) potential and the infinite square well potential of the small ball event.

Abstract

This paper reports the proof of a strong converse for the unconstrained quantum capacity of the pure-loss bosonic channel. At every fixed rate above capacity, the entanglement-generation fidelity of every code is bounded by a constant times the reciprocal of the number of channel uses. The bound holds without an energy constraint and for arbitrary encoded states, including states correlated across all input modes, and arbitrary joint decoders. Abstract

We introduce a robust quantum communication protocol that integrates classical error-control coding, entanglement distribution, and superdense coding. Classical error-correcting codes are used to mitigate dark counts and photon losses by determining the positions of qubit transmissions and protecting the data embedded through superdense coding. We derive conditions on the employed codes that guarantee successful error correction under a bounded error-frequency model. Moreover, upper bounds are derived on the performance of conventional superdense coding protected by classical error correction. It is shown that, under the same constraints on error frequency, suitable code configurations of the proposed protocol can exceed those upper bounds both in terms of data rate and energy efficiency. Finally, we develop a physical error model based on fiber attenuation, detector efficiency, dark counts, and time-slot duration, and use it to evaluate the effective performance of different configurations of error-correcting codes. The proposed approach is primarily suited for short-distance quantum links, as in Quantum Local Area Network (QLAN) where it can provide high communication throughput while integrating entanglement distribution directly into the communication process.
