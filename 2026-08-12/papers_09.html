Two-copy distillability of one-copy-undistillable negative-partial-transpose states in every dimension

Authors: Gelo Noel M. Tabia, Kai-Siang Chen, Min-Hsiu Hsieh
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08836v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. The paper investigates the distillability of negative-partial-transpose quantum states by testing if one-copy-undistillable states become distillable with multiple copies. Using fixed witnesses and tensor product constructions, the authors prove that a specific state family is two-copy distillable for all dimensions $d \geq 3$. This result significantly refines our understanding of entanglement boundaries in quantum information theory.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous mathematical proofs regarding entanglement properties (distillability) in structured quantum states, which is foundational knowledge for understanding resource theories in quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is determining if negative-partial-transpose (NPT) states, which are undistillable from a single copy, become distillable when multiple copies are available.

Main result. The authors prove that a specific one-copy-undistillable state within the canonical family is two-copy distillable in every dimension $d \geq 3$, contradicting previous conjectures about its persistence of undistillability.

Method. The study employs constructing fixed quantum witnesses (e.g., $|\Psi_d angle$) whose expectation values are calculated on tensor products of the state to test for negative entanglement measures indicative of distillability.

Model / system. The analysis focuses on bipartite quantum states $ ho$ exhibiting NPT, specifically utilizing a canonical two-parameter family introduced by DiVincenzo et al. The results hold for arbitrary local dimensions $d \geq 3$.

Key observables. Distillability status (one-copy vs. two-copy), and the sign of expectation values calculated using fixed witnesses.

Important parameters / regimes. The local dimension $d$, and the state parameters $(b, c)$ defining the canonical family $ ho_{b,c}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The derived bounds are based on analytic fixed witnesses and do not necessarily represent the fully optimized distillability boundaries.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the parameter plane for NPT states in $d=3$, showing regions certified as two-copy distillable (blue lobe) versus those whose status is unresolved, comparing results from two-copy vs. three-copy witnesses.

Paper structure. The paper systematically investigates the distillability of canonical NPT states by first establishing a two-copy witness for general $d \geq 3$, then constructing and analyzing stronger three-copy witnesses, and finally mapping out the resulting certified regions on the parameter plane.

Abstract

Whether negative-partial-transpose (NPT) states that are undistillable from one copy become distillable from finitely many copies remains a basic open problem in entanglement theory. We study the canonical two-parameter family of DiVincenzo \textit{et al.}, introduced as a symmetry-reduced testbed for this question. We prove that a distinguished one-copy-undistillable state in this family is already two-copy distillable in every local dimension $d\geq 3$. A uniform equal-norm tight-frame construction gives explicit Schmidt-rank-two certificates in every dimension, thereby disproving the conjecture that the entire one-copy-undistillable region of the canonical family remains undistillable for arbitrarily many copies. The same witnesses certify an open two-copy-distillable neighborhood around the counterexample, while separately constructed three-copy witnesses enlarge the inner bounds for the distillable region in the surrounding parameter space. In contrast, recent results for Werner states, together with the propagation argument of DiVincenzo \textit{et al.}, establish a neighboring region of one-copy-undistillable states that remains two-copy undistillable. Thus a single symmetry-reduced family contains rigorously certified states with opposite two-copy behavior, separated by a substantial region whose finite-copy distillability remains unresolved.

By introducing a novel mixer Hamiltonian and carefully constructing the cost function, the authors overcome scaling issues associated with standard QUBO formulations. This demonstrates practical applicability for optimization tasks like scheduling and resource allocation.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly applies quantum optimization techniques (QAA/QAOA) to a fundamental combinatorial problem, offering insights into how complex constraints can be mapped onto physical Hamiltonians suitable for near-term quantum hardware.

Main problem. The paper addresses the NP-hard problem of graph coloring, aiming to find the chromatic number ($\Gamma$), which is crucial for resource allocation tasks.

Key observables. The primary observable is the ground state energy, which corresponds to the optimal coloring solution, and the probability $P_c$ of finding a feasible coloring with exactly $c$ colors.

Abstract

Many industrial optimization tasks can be modeled as graph coloring, where adjacent vertices must have different colors. This NP-hard problem is challenging for large graphs. We present a quantum encoding requiring qubits that scale logarithmically with the number of colors and linearly with vertices. Using adiabatic evolution with a novel mixer Hamiltonian and vertex terms, we compute the chromatic number and demonstrate robustness by solving constrained truck loading problems.

An upper bound for the purity of absolutely positive partial transpose states

Authors: Anh T. Tran
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09832v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper determines an upper bound for the purity ($ ext{Tr}( ho^2)$) of bipartite quantum states satisfying the Absolutely Positive Partial Transpose (APPT) condition. By reformulating the problem as a constrained optimization over the eigenvalues, the authors derive explicit bounds dependent on the dimensions of the subsystems. This work contributes to understanding the fundamental limitations on state purity for highly structured quantum correlations.

Why it may be interesting. Understanding purity bounds for specific classes of quantum states like APPT is crucial for characterizing entanglement and resource theories in quantum information, directly impacting protocols relying on state distinguishability.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to derive an upper bound for the purity ($ ext{Tr}( ho^2)$) of quantum states that are Absolutely Positive Partial Transpose (APPT). This bound also applies to absolutely separable bipartite states.

Main result. The paper establishes a general, piecewise-defined upper bound for the maximum purity based on $k = mn \pmod{4}$, providing explicit formulas derived from analyzing the vertices of an associated convex polytope.

Method. The analysis involves formulating the problem as maximizing a quadratic function of eigenvalues ($\sum \lambda_i^2$) subject to linear constraints defining a polytope, which is solved by checking the extreme points (vertices) of this set.

Model / system. The study concerns bipartite quantum states $ ho$ acting on $H_A \otimes H_B$, where $m = \dim H_A$ and $n = \dim H_B$. The concepts revolve around separability criteria like PPT and APPT.

Key observables. Purity ($ ext{Tr}( ho^2)$), which quantifies how close the state is to a pure state, and the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of the density matrix $ ho$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensions $m$ and $n$, and their product $k=mn$, particularly its value modulo 4.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the maximum purity is attained at a vertex of the defined polytope, allowing for optimization over these extreme points.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the definitions of separable and APPT states, then formulates the problem as an optimization task on the eigenvalue space. It proceeds by analyzing the constraints defining a convex polytope $S_k$ and deriving explicit bounds (Theorem 1) based on the congruence class of $mn$.

Abstract

A quantum state is called absolutely separable (resp. absolutely positive partial transpose (APPT)) if it remains separable (resp. positive partial transpose (PPT)) under any global unitary transformation. It is known that the set of all absolutely separable states is a subset of the set of all APPT states. Moreover, these two sets are identical for qubit-qudit systems. In this note, we give an upper bound for the purity of APPT bipartite states (and therefore for the purity of absolutely separable bipartite states). For qubit-qudit systems, this upper bound becomes the maximum purity of APPT (and absolutely separable) states.

Catching Transpilation Drift with a CI/CD Workflow in Quantum Software Development

Summary. This paper introduces a Quantum DevOps workflow designed to monitor 'transpilation drift,' which occurs when compiler updates change the compiled form of a quantum circuit even if the source code remains identical. By calculating structural metrics and integrating checks into CI/CD pipelines, it provides an essential, auditable guardrail for ensuring reproducibility in quantum software development.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on software engineering rather than physical dynamics, understanding how compilation choices affect circuit structure is crucial for designing robust quantum algorithms that are portable across evolving hardware platforms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is detecting 'transpilation drift,' where changes in evolving quantum compiler toolchains cause an unchanged source circuit to compile into a structurally different target realization.

Main result. The authors propose and demonstrate a practical Quantum DevOps workflow that uses structural metrics to detect these compilation-induced changes before hardware execution, making the process observable and auditable.

Method. They implement a CI/CD guardrail that transpiles circuits against target profiles, calculates a structural drift metric (D_struct) comparing current artifacts to stored baselines, and reports status via configurable thresholds in GitHub Actions.

Model / system. The focus is on the compilation process itself, using abstract quantum circuits (like QFT10 or BV12) as inputs that are mapped onto specific hardware backends defined by target profiles and SDK versions.

Key observables. Structural metrics including circuit depth, total gate count, two-qubit gate counts, and the calculated structural drift score (D_struct).

Important parameters / regimes. Configurable warning ($ au_{ ext{warn}}$) and failure ($ au_{ ext{fail}}$) thresholds used to classify the CI status.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation is that the measured drift is a structural proxy; it does not guarantee semantic non-equivalence or predict actual execution fidelity, and threshold calibration is left for future work.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the full workflow pipeline (transpile -> measure -> compare to baseline -> pass/warn/fail) integrated with MLflow logging. Table II provides a detailed comparison of metrics across different transpiler versions.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, details the proposed Quantum DevOps workflow and structural drift metric calculation, demonstrates its utility using scenarios like seed drift and toolchain upgrades (e.g., Qiskit 2.1.2 vs 2.4.2), and concludes by establishing the CI guardrail.

Abstract

Quantum software workflows rely on compiler and provider toolchains that evolve independently of application source code. Consequently, an unchanged quantum circuit may transpile into a different target-specific realization after changes in SDK versions, optimization settings, basis gates, coupling maps, or backend descriptions. Such transpilation drift can affect circuit depth, gate composition, qubit mapping, and execution behavior, yet it is rarely monitored in CI/CD pipelines. This paper proposes a Quantum DevOps workflow for detecting transpilation drift before execution. The workflow transpiles source circuits against configured target profiles, computes structural drift metrics, records provenance and artifacts in MLflow, and raises configurable warnings or failures in GitHub Actions. Using representative circuits and target profiles, we show how drift checks can expose toolchain-induced changes and support reproducibility audits. The contribution is a practical CI/CD guardrail for making quantum compilation behavior observable, testable, and auditable.

Decoupling 2D translation-invariant topological CSS codes

Authors: Yifei Wang, Zhongyi Ni, Mingxin He, Jinguo Liu, Yingfei Gu
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09915v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper mathematically proves that 2D translation-invariant topological CSS codes can always be locally unitarily decoupled into a combination of toric codes and product states. The authors provide an efficient algorithm based on module theory over Laurent polynomials to construct this decoupling map, simplifying the understanding and implementation of these quantum error-correcting codes.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous mathematical tools (module theory) to simplify complex quantum error-correcting codes into known, simpler structures (toric codes), which is fundamental for building scalable quantum computation architectures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To prove that no further obstruction exists beyond removing anyon-permuting translations when decoupling 2D translation-invariant topological CSS codes into toric codes and product states.

Main result. The authors prove this non-existence of obstruction, showing every such code admits a local unitary decoupling into toric codes and product states after passing to the maximal anyon-preserving superlattice.

Method. The core method involves applying module theory over Laurent polynomials ($R = \mathbb{F}_2[x\pm 1, y\pm 1]$) and advanced linear algebra techniques to find required chain isomorphisms between complex structures.

Model / system. The study focuses on two-dimensional (2D) translation-invariant topological Calderbank-Shor-Steane (CSS) codes defined by Pauli Hamiltonians. These are analyzed using module theory over the Laurent polynomial ring, relating them to toric codes and product states.

Key observables. Logical error rate, support spreading $s(Q)$, number of e-anyons ($t$), and the existence/invertibility of chain isomorphisms.

Important parameters / regimes. The density of qubits per unit cell ($q$) and the dimensions related to stabilizer generators ($r$ and $s$).

Assumptions / limitations. Previous constructions break more symmetry than necessary; the analysis relies on module theory over non-field rings, which complicates standard linear algebra techniques.

Figures summary. One figure summary mentions a speed-accuracy tradeoff comparison between decoding methods (unitary-decouple vs. BP-OSD), and another table quantifies support spreading for transported CNOTs.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mathematical framework using module theory over Laurent polynomials, proves the existence of the decoupling map via chain isomorphism construction, provides an efficient algorithm to compute this map, and discusses applications like gate construction and decoding performance.

Abstract

Two-dimensional translation-invariant topological CSS codes on qubits are known to be locally equivalent, after coarse-graining, to stacks of toric codes. However, existing constructions generally break more translation symmetry than is required to remove anyon-permuting translations, leaving open whether any further obstruction exists. We prove that no such obstruction occurs: after passing to the maximal anyon-preserving superlattice, every such code admits a local unitary decoupling into toric codes and product states. We further provide an efficient algorithm for explicitly constructing the decoupling map, together with bounds on the required supercell size and operator spreading. The decoupling requires no additional ancillas in generic cases and extends to finite systems with suitable boundary conditions.

Deep Holes in the Clifford Hierarchy

Authors: Ian Teixeira, David Meyer
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08403v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper calculates the covering radius associated with the single-qubit Clifford gate set embedded in SU(2). By mapping the problem onto a minimization task on SO(3), the authors precisely determine this radius to be arccos(sqrt(5)/6). This result quantifies a fundamental geometric limit on the fidelity achievable by sequences of Clifford gates.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep mathematical insights into the fundamental structure of quantum gate sets, which is crucial for understanding universal quantum computation and resource estimation in quantum information theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the covering radius of the topological closure of the single-qubit Clifford hierarchy within SU(2) (identified with S^3). This is equivalent to finding the minimum all-level Clifford fidelity.

Main result. The covering radius is found to be arccos(sqrt(5)/6), and the extremal points, called 'deep holes', form a single orbit of size 192 under Clifford gate multiplication.

Method. The problem is solved by mapping the geometry from S^3 to SO(3) via a quaternionic double cover. This reduces the covering problem to an exact minimax statement concerning the L-infinity norm on SO(3).

Model / system. The system models the single-qubit quantum gate space, represented by SU(2) or S^3. The structure analyzed is the closure of the Clifford hierarchy, which forms a union of 18 great circles in this space.

Key observables. The covering radius $ ho(U_{18})$ and the minimum all-level Clifford fidelity (which equals $5/6$).

Important parameters / regimes. The size of the deep hole orbit (192) and the value $\sqrt{5}/6$.

Assumptions / limitations. The proof relies on identifying the configuration of planes as a Grassmannian 3-design and utilizing the specific structure provided by the quaternionic double cover to map the problem onto SO(3).

Paper structure. The paper first defines the geometry (18 circles in S^3) and establishes an orthonormal basis for their projectors. It then maps the covering problem from S^3 to a minimax statement on SO(3), solving this final geometric minimization problem.

Abstract

We determine the covering radius of the topological closure of the single-qubit Clifford hierarchy in $\SU(2)\cong S^3$. This closure is a union of $18$ great circles --- the Clifford--Pauli circles --- and we prove that its covering radius is $\arccos\sqrt{5/6}$. The extremal points, which we call \emph{deep holes}, form a single orbit of size $192$ under left and right multiplication by Clifford gates, and are described in closed form. Equivalently, the minimum over one-qubit unitaries of the all-level Clifford fidelity is $5/6$. The proof rests on two structures attached to the configuration of $18$ planes in $\R^4$: their centered rank-two projectors form an orthonormal basis of the irreducible $\SO(4)$-module $\Sym_0(4)$, and the projection profile of a unit quaternion is exactly its image under the double cover $\SU(2)\to\SO(3)$. These reduce the covering problem to a minimax statement for the $\ell^\infty$-norm on $\SO(3)$ which we solve exactly, classifying its equality cases.

It establishes fundamental bounds and provides explicit examples derived from algebraic geometry codes, showing how shared entanglement allows for multiple independent recovery sets. This advances the field of fault-tolerant quantum memory by offering robust coding schemes for erasure correction.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly contributes to the theoretical framework of quantum data storage and fault tolerance, providing concrete construction methods that improve upon standard Quantum CSS code limitations by incorporating shared entanglement for enhanced local repair capabilities.

Main problem. The work focuses on defining, bounding, and constructing entanglement-assisted quantum locally recoverable codes (EAQLRCs) that allow for fault tolerance against erasures.

Main result. They establish a Singleton-like bound for EAQLRCs and provide explicit constructions using various classical code families, including those derived from algebraic geometry curves.

Method. The authors use techniques from classical coding theory (e.g., linear codes, folding) combined with quantum information concepts to construct the codes and derive their parameters.

Key observables. Code parameters such as length ($n$), quantum dimension ($k_Q$), locality ($r$), availability ($t$), and minimum distance ($d_{EA}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The key parameters are the erasure tolerance ($\delta-1$) and the number of local recovery sets available ($t$). The construction relies on shared entanglement.

Abstract

In this work, we define entanglement-assisted quantum locally recoverable codes with availability, in which any set of up to $δ-1$ erased qudits can be recovered from any one of $t$ local recovery sets, each of size at most $r+δ-1$, with the recovery sets intersecting exactly in the erased coordinates, where $r$ is a (small) positive integer. We show that shared entanglement permits $t>1$, meaning that multiple local recovery sets can be available for the same set of up to $δ-1$ erasures. We establish a Singleton-like bound for this family of codes and present random constructions based on classical linear codes with Vandermonde parity-check matrices. We also provide explicit constructions of entanglement-assisted quantum locally recoverable codes with availability from several classical code families and their folded versions, including Tamo-Barg codes, fiber-product codes, and algebraic-geometry codes such as one-point Hermitian and Suzuki codes.

Faster Algorithms for Multimarginal Optimal Transport

Authors: Brandon Augustino, Yue Sun, Atithi Acharya, Shouvanik Chakrabarti, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Shree Hari Sureshbabu, Charlie Che
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09513v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper tackles the computational challenge of approximating multimarginal optimal transport (MOT) distance between multiple probability distributions using advanced algorithms. It provides both classical and quantum solutions, achieving specific runtime bounds for different formulations (primal vs. dual). The work is significant for establishing theoretical speedups in solving complex multi-marginal optimization problems.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly about physical Hamiltonians, the development of quantum algorithms for solving complex optimization problems like MOT is highly relevant. Quantum speedups in linear programming solvers are a major area of theoretical interest that could impact simulations in physics (e.g., calculating free energy differences or correlation functions via optimal transport metrics).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is developing efficient algorithms, both classical and quantum, to approximate the multimarginal optimal transport (MOT) distance between multiple discrete probability distributions.

Main result. The authors present a classical algorithm with a specific polynomial runtime bound and two distinct quantum algorithms—a projected subgradient method and a Sinkhorn variant—that achieve dimension speedups over classical counterparts.

Method. The work uses techniques from convex optimization, formulating MOT as a linear program (LP) or its dual. Quantum approaches leverage quantum subroutines to accelerate the iterative solving of these LPs.

Model / system. The model is purely mathematical/algorithmic: approximating the distance between $m$ discrete probability distributions ($\mu_1, \dots, \mu_m$), each supported on at most $n$ points. The problem structure generalizes classical Optimal Transport (OT).

Key observables. The primary observable is the MOT distance itself, approximated within an additive error $\varepsilon$. Other key quantities include the coupling tensor $X$ and its expected transportation cost.

Important parameters / regimes. Key parameters are $m$ (number of marginals), $n$ (support size), and $\varepsilon$ (desired accuracy). The complexity analysis depends heavily on these values.

Assumptions / limitations. The quantum algorithms estimate the cost rather than returning an explicit coupling tensor, except in the case of the Sinkhorn algorithm which returns an implicit description. The classical bounds are derived for general MOT problems.

Figures summary. No specific figure summaries were extracted; the notes focus on algorithmic complexity and theoretical results presented across different methods (Primal/Dual LPs).

Paper structure. The paper is structured around formulating the MOT problem as an LP, presenting classical solutions for the primal form, and then detailing two distinct quantum algorithms—one based on projected subgradients applied to the dual, and another using a quantum Sinkhorn method for entropy regularization.

Abstract

We study algorithms for approximating the multimarginal optimal transport (MOT) distance, a generalization of the classic optimal transport distance, between $m$ discrete probability distributions each supported on at most $n$ points. We give a classical algorithm that computes a coupling between these marginals whose expected transportation cost is within an additive $\varepsilon > 0$ of the MOT distance in time $O(m^2 n^m \varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{polylog}(m,n,\varepsilon^{-1}))$. This is, to our knowledge, the first bound for general MOT problems with simultaneous linear dependence on the dimension $n^m$ and on the accuracy parameter $\varepsilon^{-1}$, improving the prior state of the art. On the quantum side, we give two algorithms that achieve speedups in dimension, though with worse accuracy dependence than classical approaches. First, we construct a quantum projected subgradient method for estimating the MOT distance within an additive $\varepsilon >0$ with runtime $O( m^3 n^{\frac{m}{2}+1} \varepsilon^{-2} \mathrm{polylog}(m,n,\varepsilon^{-1}))$. This algorithm works with the linear programming dual of the MOT problem, and does not return a coupling. We also give a quantum multimarginal Sinkhorn algorithm for entropy-regularized MOT. This algorithm returns an implicit description of an approximately optimal coupling with runtime $O(m^8n^{\frac{m+1}{2}} \varepsilon^{-5} \mathrm{polylog}(m,n,\varepsilon^{-1})))$ after the usual reduction from entropic MOT to unregularized MOT. We also record query lower bounds: for any precision $\varepsilon<1/2$, randomized classical algorithms require $Ω(n^m/(1+\varepsilon n))$ queries and quantum algorithms require $Ω(\sqrt{n^m/(1+\varepsilon n)})$ queries.

Microscopic Origin of Spin Splitting in Altermagnetic CrSb Thin Films

Summary. This work uses first-principles calculations to map the microscopic origin of spin splitting in altermagnetic CrSb thin films. The authors find that surface termination is crucial, showing that robust spin splitting requires specific long-range inter-unit-cell hopping pathways unique to certain surfaces. This establishes a general principle for controlling electronic properties by engineering low-dimensional crystal interfaces.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on magnetism, understanding how crystal symmetry and dimensionality fundamentally alter electronic band structure via orbital hybridization (hopping) provides a generalizable principle for engineering quantum properties in novel materials systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to resolve the microscopic origin of spin splitting in altermagnetic CrSb thin films, specifically investigating how this property changes when dimensionality is reduced from bulk to 2D.

Main result. The paper demonstrates that surface orientation dictates the electronic structure; while the (2$ar{1}ar{1}0$) slab retains strong spin splitting, others do not. This splitting fundamentally relies on long-range inter-unit-cell coplanar Cr--Sb hopping pathways.

Method. First-principles calculations were used to model CrSb slabs with various surface orientations and varying vacuum spacings to simulate dimensional reduction. Wannier tight-binding Hamiltonians were constructed to analyze the contribution of different hopping channels.

Model / system. The physical system is metallic CrSb, an altermagnet known for its giant spin splitting. The model involves constructing slab geometries with specific surface terminations ((2$ar{1}ar{1}0$), (0001), etc.) and systematically varying the inter-slab vacuum spacing to tune coupling strength.

Key observables. Momentum-dependent spin splitting magnitude, electronic band structures along high-symmetry k-paths, and the contribution of specific hopping parameters ($ ext{Cr–Sb}$, $ ext{Sb–Sb}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Dimensionality (3D bulk vs. 2D thin film limit), surface orientation, and inter-unit-cell coupling strength.

Assumptions / limitations. The calculations assume that the electronic structure can be accurately modeled by analyzing the contributions of specific hopping pathways derived from first-principles results.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the crystal structures for different planes, show the evolution of spin splitting as vacuum spacing changes (comparing surfaces), and detail how retaining specific inter-unit-cell hopping terms is necessary to reproduce the observed spin splitting in the most stable surface orientation.

Paper structure. The paper systematically compares the electronic structure of bulk vs. various slab geometries using first-principles calculations, then refines this analysis by constructing Wannier Hamiltonians and analyzing the necessity of specific long-range hopping terms ($ ext{Cr–Sb}$ vs $ ext{Sb–Sb}$) to explain the observed spin splitting in low dimensions.

Abstract

Altermagnets have recently emerged as promising materials for spintronic applications owing to their momentum-dependent spin splitting. Among them, metallic CrSb is particularly attractive owing to its giant spin splitting and high Néel temperature. However, the microscopic origin of the distinct spin-splitting behaviors in bulk and thin-film CrSb remains unresolved. Here, we systematically investigate the electronic structures of CrSb slabs with different surface orientations using first-principles calculations. Although all considered slabs preserve spin-group symmetries compatible with altermagnetism, they exhibit markedly different electronic structures: the (2$\bar{1}\bar{1}$0) slab retains pronounced altermagnetic spin splitting, whereas the (0001) and (10$\bar{1}$0) slabs display nearly spin-degenerate bands. We demonstrate that dimensional reduction fundamentally changes the microscopic origin of altermagnetic spin splitting. Unlike bulk CrSb, altermagnetic spin splitting in thin films requires long-range inter-unit-cell coplanar Cr--Sb hopping, while Sb--Sb hopping provides an additional contribution. The preservation or suppression of these hopping pathways explains the strong surface dependence of the spin splitting. Our findings establish a microscopic mechanism for understanding altermagnetism in reduced dimensions and provide a general principle for engineering spin splitting in low-dimensional altermagnetic materials.

Abstract

We investigate two parity-time-symmetric ring resonators coupled over a finite angular region described by a super-Gaussian profile. In the linear regime, analytical spectra are obtained in the homogeneous-coupling and fixed-amplitude narrow-contact limits, while the finite-width problem is treated numerically. Abstract

Majorana zero modes have emerged as one of the most promising platforms for topological quantum computation, since their non-Abelian braiding statistics allow quantum information to be encoded nonlocally and manipulated through braiding operations that are, in principle, protected against local perturbations. In practice, however, a braid is only as robust as its physical implementation: finite-time operation, residual couplings, and environmental noise can all convert local excitations into logical errors during the exchange process. Here, we address this question through a microscopic comparison of two representative braiding architectures, a nanowire trijunction and a quantum-dot-assisted setup, simulating the full time-dependent Bogoliubov--de Gennes dynamics under both noiseless and noisy conditions. We show that the dot-assisted architecture consistently achieves a lower error over a shorter timescale than the trijunction, owing to its more localized exchange mechanism. This advantage persists in the presence of noise, and a spatially resolved analysis further reveals that, in the dot-assisted geometry, fast noise localized on the dot produces a smaller error than equivalent noise on the wires, whereas slow, quasi-static noise on the dot becomes the dominant limitation. Taken together, these findings link the different error contributions directly to device geometry, pointing to concrete design principles for noise-resilient Majorana-based quantum gates.
