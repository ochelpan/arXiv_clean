A Universal Entanglement Witness Generator

Authors: Aiden R. Rosebush, Alexander C. B. Greenwood, Andi Shahaj, Li Qian
Type: both · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.07806v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · QC/QI experiment 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This paper introduces a universal machine-learning framework to generate highly robust entanglement witnesses for multipartite qubit and qudit systems. By optimizing witness construction using adversarial training, it overcomes limitations in measurement efficiency and noise tolerance compared to prior methods. This tool is vital for experimentally verifying genuine quantum entanglement across diverse physical platforms.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, automated tool for certifying entanglement in complex quantum systems, which is crucial for validating experimental results across various platforms (photonic/superconducting).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The challenge is constructing entanglement witnesses that are simultaneously noise-robust, measurement-economical, and applicable to complex systems beyond simple qubits.

Main result. The authors propose a general machine-learning pipeline that generates superior entanglement witnesses with greater noise tolerance using fewer local measurements than existing methods.

Method. A novel ML approach trains an entanglement witness by optimizing for noise tolerance via gradient descent and adversarial training, requiring only user-specified local measurement settings.

Model / system. The method is general, applicable to multipartite qubit and qudit systems (d-level systems), including non-stabilizer states. It tests on various structured states like Bell, GHZ, W, and hypergraph states.

Key observables. Entanglement witnesses ($W$), noise tolerance parameter ($p$), expectation values ($\langle W angle$).

Important parameters / regimes. Number of qudits ($N$), dimension ($d$), user-specified number of measurement settings ($M$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes that the required training set size becomes independent of system size under adversarial training, and it aims to surpass existing methods' limitations regarding setting count.

Figures summary. Not specified (Notes mention testing on various state types rather than describing figure content).

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the need for robust witnesses, then details the ML framework involving prototype witness generation ($W_0$), refinement via differential programming to find $ ho_s$, and finally strengthening it with adversarial training.

Abstract

Entanglement witnesses are essential for certifying entanglement, yet constructing ones that are both noise-robust and economical in measurement settings remains challenging - particularly beyond qubits and for non-stabilizer ("magic") states. We present a machine-learning method that, given a target state and a user-specified number of measurement settings, generates an entanglement witness optimized for noise tolerance in the neighborhood of that state, requiring only local measurements. The approach is fully general, applying to multipartite qubit and qudit systems alike, including non-stabilizer states. For N qudits of dimension d, we train on the fully-separable eigenstates of each qudit's SU(d) generators to find a prototype witness, then tune the witness's bias term via gradient descent to maximize noise tolerance. Adversarial training further strengthens the witnesses, delivering greater noise tolerance with even fewer settings; critically, under this scheme the required training-set size becomes independent of system size. We package the entire pipeline as an automated script that, in every case we tested, produces witnesses surpassing all existing methods in noise tolerance and/or number of measurement settings. We demonstrate the method on Bell, GHZ, W, and hypergraph states, along with a range of qudit states, spanning 2-6 qubits, bipartite qudits up to d=10, and tripartite qutrits. Our witnesses achieve perfect accuracy across both physical experimental test states and large numerical sets of separable mixed states-including 30 million test states for a 3-qubit W-state witness and 10 million for a 4-qubit hypergraph-state witness-and we experimentally confirm the noise tolerance of Bell- and hypergraph state witnesses on both photonic and superconducting platforms, respectively.

Summary. This paper develops a theoretical framework showing that by leveraging the mathematical concept of Gevrey regularity, one can efficiently solve high-dimensional linear Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) on a quantum computer. This approach overcomes classical scaling limitations and provides a pathway to achieving polynomial quantum speedups for simulating complex many-body systems in condensed matter physics.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses how mathematical properties (smoothness) dictate the feasibility and scaling of quantum computation for fundamental physics problems like electronic structure calculations. The explicit link between Gevrey classes and polynomial speedup is highly relevant to designing practical quantum algorithms for condensed matter theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is overcoming the 'Curse of Dimensionality' when using quantum algorithms to solve high-precision Partial Differential Equations (PDEs).

Main result. The authors establish a framework showing that utilizing Gevrey regularity allows for efficient, polynomial-degree quantum speedups in solving linear PDEs at varying target precisions.

Method. They connect function smoothness (via the Gevrey hierarchy) to the cost of preparing high-precision Fourier-basis amplitude encodings and apply this to solve discretized linear PDE systems using QLSA techniques.

Model / system. The methods are applied to general linear PDEs, including those arising in many-body quantum simulations (like the Sternheimer equation derived from DFT) for probing atomistic systems.

Key observables. Linear response observables such as density response ($\delta ho$), induced potentials ($\delta V_H$, $\delta V_{xc}$), and general correlation functions.

Important parameters / regimes. Target precision ($\epsilon$), Gevrey smoothness parameter ($s$), cutoff mode ($N$), and the dimensionality of the problem ($d$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies heavily on assuming $s$-Gevrey regularity for the solutions to guarantee polynomial scaling in dimension, and it requires using a closure approximation when extending to higher-order correlation functions.

Figures summary. Table I compares classical vs. quantum complexity bounds for solving PDEs; Table II relates quantum hardness to function smoothness classes via Fourier coefficient decay rates.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical link between Gevrey regularity and quantum encoding costs, then specializes this framework to solve general linear PDEs using Fourier methods, and finally applies this machinery to build a hierarchy of many-body simulation pipelines for atomistic systems.

Abstract

We connect different degrees of smoothness of real-valued periodic functions to the cost of preparing their high-precision Fourier-basis amplitude encodings as quantum states. Our central observation is that the Gevrey hierarchy, which stratifies the space between smooth and analytic functions, provides a natural class for high-precision quantum algorithms. We then specialize to solving general linear partial differential equations (PDEs), showing how our Fourier methods do so efficiently at varying target precisions on a quantum computer. This also demonstrates how our framework enables passage from query-complexity results to explicit elementary gate counts. As an application, we introduce a hierarchy of many-body quantum simulation pipelines that harness these high-precision algorithms to probe the linear response of atomistic systems in first quantization. Each level of the hierarchy unlocks a further polynomial-degree quantum speedup, yielding a gradual improvement in simulation efficiency as quantum computers scale.

Summary. This paper uses Density Functional Theory to model a binary mixture near a liquid-vapor interface. It calculates the effective interaction potential acting on dissolved nanoparticles, demonstrating strong attraction to the interface using two consistent theoretical approaches. The work advances understanding of interfacial thermodynamics in soft matter systems.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical fluids, the rigorous application of advanced statistical mechanics tools like FMT and the Widom theorem to inhomogeneous systems provides methodological insights applicable to quantum many-body problems involving interfaces or confinement.

Main problem. The study investigates the effective one-body interaction experienced by a dilute component (nano particles) due to the presence of a liquid-vapor interface formed by the solvent.

Main result. The calculated effective interaction strongly attracts nano particles to the liquid-vapor interface, and this result is confirmed using two independent theoretical routes.

Method. Classical Density Functional Theory (cDFT) is employed, utilizing novel perturbation theory contributions inspired by Fundamental Measure Theory (FMT).

Model / system. The system modeled is an additive binary square-well mixture containing a solvent undergoing phase separation and dissolved nano particles. The analysis focuses on the density profiles near the planar free interface.

Key observables. Effective one-body interaction potential ($V_{ ext{eff}}(z)$), density profiles ($ ho_1(z)$, $ ho_2(z)$), and interfacial surface tension ($\gamma_{s21}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Dilute limit of nano particle concentration ($ ho_{ ext{nano}} o 0$), solvent-particle interaction strength ($eta\epsilon_2$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the square-well potential model and assumes the neglect of two- and higher order terms in certain theoretical mappings.

Figures summary. Figure 4 illustrates the excellent agreement between the effective potential calculated via density profiles versus that derived from Widom's insertion theorem for different interaction strengths.

Paper structure. The paper introduces DFT for the model system, discusses bulk considerations and phase equilibrium, and then details the calculation of the effective interaction using both direct profile analysis and the Widom insertion theorem.

Abstract

In this study we investigate the behavior of additive binary square-well mixtures within the framework of classical density functional theory. By leveraging on the geometrical structure of the square-well interaction, we propose a novel form of the perturbation theory contribution to the density functional in terms of weighted densities, inspired by fundamental measure theory. We apply this functional in order to study the effective one-body interaction due to the liquid-vapor interface of the solvent acting on a dilute component of dissolved nano particles. The effective one-body interaction attracts the nano particles strongly to the interface. We show that this effective interaction potential can be calculated either from the density profiles of the full mixture, at low but non-vanishing concentrations of the nano particles, or by employing the Widom insertion theorem in the dilute limit of vanishing density of nano particles. Both routes display excellent agreement.

Quantum Decision Theory for Displacement Detection with Finite-Energy GKP States

Summary. This paper establishes a rigorous decision-theoretic framework to detect phase-space displacements using finite-energy GKP states. It derives optimal detection metrics by comparing GKP performance against standard Gaussian probes under realistic loss and amplification noise channels. The key finding is that GKP encoding offers superior sensing capabilities in specific noisy regimes compared to purely Gaussian approaches.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses quantum metrology limits by analyzing non-Gaussian probes (GKP states) in realistic noisy environments (lossy channels), which is crucial for practical quantum sensing applications beyond the ideal vacuum case.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a quantum-decision-theoretic framework to determine if an unknown phase-space displacement has occurred using finite-energy Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) probes.

Main result. Under lossy and finite-squeezing regimes, GKP probes can achieve lower Bayesian error probabilities and detect smaller displacements compared to optimized Gaussian receivers.

Method. The authors employ quantum decision theory metrics (Bayesian minimum-error probability and Neyman-Pearson ROC) applied to bosonic systems undergoing displacement channels.

Model / system. The system models a single bosonic mode subjected to unknown phase-space displacements, analyzed using finite-energy GKP states as probes. The analysis incorporates realistic noise channels like pure loss followed by quantum-limited amplification.

Key observables. Bayesian error probability ($P_e^\star$), Neyman-Pearson receiver operating characteristic ($p_{11}(p_{10})$), and the minimum detectable displacement.

Important parameters / regimes. Finite squeezing level, logical dimension ($d$), channel transmissivity ($\eta$), and amplification gain ($G$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis treats pure loss followed by quantum-limited amplification as an effective Gaussian random-displacement channel; entanglement does not surpass the single-mode optimum in noiseless pure states.

Figures summary. Figure 5 compares ROC curves and minimum detectable displacement thresholds for GKP vs. Gaussian protocols under varying transmissivity, showing performance advantages for GKP in specific regimes.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the decision theory framework, derives optimal metrics using characteristic functions, models loss/amplification channels, analyzes single-mode versus entanglement-assisted GKP sensing, and concludes with numerical comparisons against Gaussian benchmarks.

Abstract

We develop a quantum-decision-theoretic framework for detecting phase-space displacements with finite-energy, $d$-level Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) probes. For single-mode and entanglement-assisted architectures, we derive the Bayesian minimum-error probability, the optimal Neyman-Pearson receiver-operating characteristic, and the corresponding minimum detectable displacement. Finite-energy effects are treated through exact theta-series displacement kernels, while pure loss followed by quantum-limited amplification is mapped to an effective Gaussian random-displacement channel. Entanglement removes preparation-dependent blind directions and preserves both logical displacement labels, although it does not surpass the pointwise optimized single-mode strategy in the noiseless pure-state setting. We benchmark the resulting protocols against coherent-state, direction-matched squeezed-vacuum, and twin-beam schemes at equal nominal squeezing. Numerical results identify finite-squeezing and lossy regimes in which GKP probes achieve both a lower Bayesian error and a smaller minimum detectable perturbation than the selected Gaussian receivers.

Quantum uncertainty in a macroscopic domain

Authors: Othman Q. Malhas, Bacim Alali
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09262v1

Summary. This work develops a model-theoretic framework using partial Boolean algebras to mathematically encode concepts of quantum uncertainty. By analyzing the structure of these algebras, the authors show how classical logical consistency can yield analogues of measurement back-action and contextuality. It provides an abstract structural understanding of quantum constraints rooted in propositional logic.

Why it may be interesting. It offers a highly abstract, logic-based approach to contextuality and quantum constraints, which could provide novel mathematical tools for understanding non-classical correlations without invoking Hilbert space formalism directly.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to formulate quantum-like uncertainty using purely classical model-theoretic tools, specifically by representing partial Boolean algebras.

Main result. It establishes that certain structural properties (KS-colourability) of these abstract algebras correspond to physical constraints like the destruction of sharpness upon measurement back-action.

Method. The authors develop a framework mapping propositional logic theories onto partial Boolean algebras, using model theory to define concepts analogous to quantum compatibility and uncertainty.

Model / system. The system is an abstract mathematical model based on partial Boolean algebras derived from the core of consistent theories in classical propositional logic. These structures are then mapped analogously to propositions associated with physical observables.

Key observables. KS-colourability, sharpness (number of locally admissible atomic outcomes), and the structure of the underlying partial Boolean algebra.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension of the partial Boolean algebra (e.g., 12-vertex vs. 140-vertex) dictates whether certain structural properties hold.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes that quantum uncertainty can be captured by analyzing the organization and compatibility structure of propositions within a classical logical setting, rather than requiring nonclassical deduction itself.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the Hasse diagram of small partial Boolean algebras (like $V_{12}$) formed by gluing together smaller Boolean components.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the model-theoretic representation of partial Boolean algebras, to characterizing uncertainty via KS-colourability and measurement back-action in finite examples, concluding with connections to state support sets and limitations regarding probability assignment.

Abstract

We develop a classical model-theoretic representation of partial Boolean algebras and use it to formulate quantum-like uncertainty without abandoning classical propositional logic. Given a surjection from the initial formulæ of a propositional language onto a partial Boolean algebra $(\mathcal V,Π)$, we construct a consistent theory $\mathcal T_g$ whose core---the ordered set of equivalence classes of initial formulæ---is isomorphic to $(\mathcal V,Π)$. Its models are characterized as upward-closed clusters, yielding a model-theoretic formulation of KS-colourability: an $n$-dimensional partial Boolean algebra is KS-colourable exactly when the induced theory has a model meeting every pre-frame in one primitive formula. For finite-spectrum observables, uncertainty is defined by the number of locally admissible atomic outcomes. Dispersion-free models are characterized by the singleton pre-frame condition. With an additional measurement-update postulate, a finite example shows how measurement of an incompatible observable can destroy sharpness, providing a model-theoretic form of back-action. A $12$-vertex partial Boolean algebra is KS-colourable, whereas a rigorously constructed $140$-vertex, four-dimensional partial Boolean algebra is not, as shown by a parity argument; its incidence structure is isomorphic to the Peres $24$-ray, $24$-basis configuration in $\mathbb R^4$. Macroscopic interpretations show that these phenomena arise from the organization of propositions and models rather than from nonclassical deduction. Finally, we relate certain models to the probability-one propositions of pure states and density operators, while emphasizing that such certainty models do not determine the full quantum state.

Sub-Rayleigh Imaging of Unequal-Intensity Sources: Near-Quantum-Limit Multiparameter Estimation

Summary. This paper presents a method called SPLICE to overcome diffraction limitations when measuring two closely spaced light sources with unequal intensities. By adapting super-resolution localization techniques and using quantum information theory bounds, the authors demonstrate that their approach significantly improves the accuracy of estimating both separation and intensity compared to standard direct imaging methods. This work advances the field by achieving near-quantum-limit precision in complex multiparameter optical estimation.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to quantum optics and open quantum systems as it pushes measurement techniques beyond classical limits by exploiting quantum correlations in parameter estimation, which has implications for sensing and imaging.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is overcoming the diffraction limit (Rayleigh criterion) in optical imaging to simultaneously estimate both the separation ($\delta$) and relative intensity ($q$) of two spatially unresolved, unequal-intensity light sources.

Main result. The proposed SPLICE method significantly outperforms Direct Imaging (DI), achieving an estimation precision that approaches the quantum limit with up to a sixfold improvement over DI in tested regimes.

Method. The authors adapt Super-Resolved Position Localization by Inversion of Coherence along an Edge (SPLICE) technique, employing quantum parameter estimation theory based on Fisher Information matrices derived from measured photon counts.

Model / system. The system involves two incoherent light sources whose combined field is imaged. The analysis models the state as a statistical mixture and uses projectors onto specific spatial modes (Hermite-Gauss functions) to extract information about $\delta$ and $q$.

Key observables. Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for estimated separation ($\hat{\delta}$) and relative intensity ($\hat{q}$); Quantum Cramér-Rao Bound (QCRB).

Important parameters / regimes. Separation ($\delta$), relative intensity ($q$), beam radius ($\sigma$), quantum limit bounds, and cross-talk parameter ($\epsilon$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes single-photon detection dominates, the center of mass is known, and theoretical bounds rely on asymptotic unbiasedness (large photon number).

Figures summary. Figures compare estimated vs. true values for $\delta$ and $q$, showing SPLICE's superior performance relative to DI; one figure plots the ratio of CRBs demonstrating SPLICE's growing advantage as separation decreases.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the limitations of classical imaging, introduces the theoretical framework using quantum Fisher Information matrices (CRB), details the adaptation of SPLICE for multiparameter estimation, and concludes with experimental validation showing performance approaching the quantum limit.

Abstract

In optical imaging, diffraction strongly degrades the performance of conventional intensity-based estimation once the separation between two sources is below the Rayleigh-Abbe limit. Recent developments showed that this limitation can be surpassed using spatial-mode demultiplexing (SPADE), and this was demonstrated in several experiments for two equal-intensity spots of unknown separation. When there are multiple unknown parameters, as in the case of several unequal sources with unknown intensities and unknown separation, cross-talk among the parameters makes the multi-parameter estimation problem significantly more challenging. In this paper, we adapt super-resolved position localization by inversion of coherence along an edge (SPLICE) to estimate both the separation and relative intensity of two incoherent sources simultaneously. We demonstrate a clear advantage over direct imaging (DI), achieving a root mean squared error (RMSE) approximately $50 \%$ larger than the quantum limit and a sixfold improvement over DI within the range of parameters we tested. This improvement can be even greater for smaller separations and larger intensity imbalances when crosstalk is suppressed.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how strong, time-dependent space-time features affect the vacuum pair production rate of electron-positron pairs.

Main result. The spatiotemporal modulation provided by a relativistically moving potential well significantly reduces the laser intensity threshold required for pair creation. Method. The research employs Computational Quantum Field Theory (CQFT) alongside semi-analytical methods like the Instantaneous Resonance Field Method (IRFM). These techniques are used to solve the time-dependent Dirac equation for pair production probabilities.

We identify three distinct gate-tunable topological phases and establish a direct correspondence between the nearly quantized layer-resolved Chern numbers and the layer-locking behavior of chiral Majorana edge states, vortex-core Majorana zero modes, and nearly quantized thermal Hall responses.

Summary. This paper introduces 'distimation,' a novel protocol that merges state estimation with entanglement distillation in quantum networks. By reusing classical syndrome data from the purification process, it eliminates redundant testing stages required by older protocols. This results in significantly lower resource consumption and reduced latency, making quantum networking more feasible for current hardware limitations.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a blueprint for optimizing quantum network protocols by merging two distinct functional layers (estimation and purification) into one efficient physical process, which is crucial for near-term hardware deployment.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Traditional quantum network protocols require separate, resource-intensive steps for state estimation (quality checking) and entanglement distillation.

Main result. Distimation merges these two processes by extracting necessary state estimation data directly from the classical syndromes generated during entanglement distillation, significantly reducing required raw entangled pairs and latency.

Method. The core method is to eliminate the dedicated 'test-then-distill' stage by treating syndrome measurements as dual-purpose: simultaneously purifying entanglement and characterizing the underlying channel noise.

Model / system. The work models quantum network stacks, focusing on establishing high-fidelity entanglement between nodes using raw entangled pairs (e.g., Bell pairs) generated over noisy links.

Key observables. Expected throughput of distilled Bell pairs; characterization of Bell-diagonal coefficients ($q_i$); fidelity and Pauli error probability estimates.

Important parameters / regimes. Noise parameter ($\epsilon$); raw entanglement generation rate (e.g., 1 kHz or 1 MHz); target accuracy and failure probability.

Assumptions / limitations. Assumes that local noise processes are dominant and well-characterized; assumes the viability of distillation protocols; requires hardware capable of buffering pairs within coherence times.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the integration of distimation into quantum network stack models (QRSA, Dahlberg et al.'s), showing the flow from distillation to estimation, and comparing throughput vs. noise for different regimes.

Paper structure. The paper surveys the principles by contrasting the traditional 'test-then-distill' pipeline with the integrated distimation approach, quantifies resource gains using throughput calculations across various noise models, and outlines a research roadmap addressing hardware limitations and generalization challenges.

Abstract

Estimating the quality of raw entangled states and distilling high-fidelity entanglement traditionally require two separate link-layer protocols in a quantum network stack, each consuming its own share of fragile entangled pairs. The recently introduced distimation concept merges these two protocols by directly extracting state estimation data from the classical syndromes generated during entanglement distillation. By removing the dedicated "test-then-distill'' stage, distimation lowers the number of raw entangled pairs required to operate the network, reduces latency, and streamlines the network control plane. The paradigm is especially attractive for near-term hardware platforms, where entanglement generation remains a severe bottleneck. Alongside these promises, distimation introduces new engineering challenges, ranging from accurate local-device modeling to protocols for tracking time-varying sources. This article surveys the core principles of distimation, quantifies expected gains for realistic architectures, and outlines a research roadmap toward making distimation a standard building block in future quantum networks.

Summary. This paper explores localization in interacting, non-Hermitian quasiperiodic systems, establishing that the binding configuration of particles dictates their transport properties. By analyzing effective Hamiltonians, the authors show a controllable 'localization inversion' where composite pairs remain extended even when individual unpaired particles become localized. This highlights internal interactions as a powerful control mechanism beyond external potentials.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical framework showing how emergent collective behavior (the composite state) can decouple its transport properties from the single-particle physics dictated by external disorder or non-Hermiticity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates whether composite excitations can exhibit localization behavior opposite to their constituent particles within a non-Hermitian quasiperiodic environment.

Main result. The paper demonstrates that internal particle configuration acts as a reversible control parameter, allowing for controllable localization inversion where the composite state remains extended while unpaired constituents localize.

Method. The analysis uses effective Hamiltonian derivations via perturbation theory and diagnoses localization using the Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR) across varying system sizes.

Abstract

A composite excitation need not inherit the localization behavior of its constituents. We show that an interacting non-Hermitian quasiperiodic ladder realizes a controllable and reversible localization inversion between composite and unbound excitations, where internal configuration, rather than only the external potential, becomes a control parameter for localization. Opposite complex potentials on the two legs cancel at first order for a same-rung pair but act directly on separated particles, allowing extended composite states to persist while the unpaired sector becomes localized. A strong-coupling theory identifies the composite state as an emergent weakly modulated non-Hermitian quasicrystal generated by virtual unpaired configurations. Breaking the potential antisymmetry restores a direct modulation of the composite band and reverses the localization hierarchy. Engineering configuration-space pathways further stabilizes an extended composite band embedded within a localized continuum, the inverse of the conventional bound-state-in-the-continuum scenario. Our results establish internal configuration as a reversible control parameter for localization.
