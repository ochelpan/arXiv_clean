Summary. This paper develops a powerful Semidefinite Programming (SDP) framework to establish lower bounds on the Mean-Squared Error (MSE) for estimating parameters of quantum channels through sequential probing. It generalizes existing quantum estimation bounds to account for multiple time steps and correlated noise. The results provide new, rigorous benchmarks for designing optimal quantum sensing protocols in various physical systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous, computationally tractable bounds (via SDP) on the ultimate precision limits for quantum metrology in complex, realistic settings involving sequential measurements and correlated noise, which is crucial for designing practical quantum sensors.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the notoriously difficult problem of finding the optimal strategy to estimate parameters of a quantum channel when the channel is probed multiple times sequentially.

Main result. The authors propose a general lower bound for the Mean-Squared Error (MSE) of any physically implementable sequential sensing strategy, formulated as a Semidefinite Program (SDP). This bound is shown to be tight for single-parameter tasks.

Method. The core method involves deriving an SDP-based lower bound that generalizes the Nagaoka-Hayashi bound to the regime of sequential measurements, utilizing concepts like the quantum comb formalism.

Model / system. The model involves estimating parameters $ heta$ of a quantum channel $\Lambda_ heta$ by sequentially probing the channel multiple times ($T$ steps) using coherent ancillary systems and performing final measurements.

Key observables. Mean-Squared Error ($ ext{MSE}_ heta$), Quantum Fisher Information ($ ext{QFI}$), and the covariance matrix of the parameter estimates.

Important parameters / regimes. Number of time steps ($T$), number of parameters ($m$), and the specific noise model (e.g., correlated Pauli noise).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of an optimal strategy and provides local attainability results for the SDP bound in single-parameter cases.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the MSE lower bound for sequential estimation tasks (e.g., Pauli-Z or X/Y rotations) under various noise levels, comparing the bound to the expected Heisenberg scaling ($1/T^2$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general SDP lower bound for sequential sensing, then applying it to specific physical scenarios like single-parameter estimation (e.g., Pauli rotations) under both independent and correlated noise models, and finally comparing multi-step vs. single-step protocols.

Abstract

The most general strategy for estimating parameters of a quantum channel is to probe the channel many times sequentially with the help of coherent ancillary systems. However, finding the strategy that minimizes the mean-squared error (MSE) is a notoriously hard problem, requiring optimization over the initial probe state, intermediate evolutions, final measurement, and classical postprocessing. In this work, we study how the minimum attainable MSE can be bounded from below in this setting. We propose a lower bound for the MSE of any physically implementable sequential sensing strategy that applies to estimating parameters of any physical quantum evolution, including quantum channels probed over multiple time steps under temporally correlated noise and general temporally correlated quantum processes. We show that this lower bound can be formulated as a semidefinite program (SDP). Our bound generalizes the Nagaoka-Hayashi lower bound (that applies to parameter estimation tasks with separable measurements) to the regime where the quantum channel can be accessed sequentially multiple times. For regular finite-dimensional single-parameter sequential estimation tasks admitting an optimal strategy, we show that our SDP bound is tight and provide a procedure to obtain a locally optimal strategy from the SDP optimizer. Using our optimization bound, we compute the MSE lower bound and derive new protocols for several other physically motivated problems including error-corrected quantum sensing under an unknown correlated noise model, multiparameter estimation, and estimation with qudit systems.

Abstract

Strong light-matter coupling offers exciting opportunities for controlling molecular properties, yet resolving single-molecule behavior from collective effects remains a challenge. Recent experiments in scanning tunneling microscope break junctions (STM-BJs) have demonstrated strong light-matter coupling at the single-molecule level. Because STM-BJs provide direct access to molecular-junction currents, we investigate whether strong coupling leaves a measurable fingerprint in transport, potentially probing polaritonic states beyond optical spectroscopy. Using a semiclassical mapping approach for nonequilibrium quantum transport, we find that steady-state current indeed carries a direct signature of single-molecule strong coupling. We test the robustness of this signature against electrode connectivity, nuclear motion, spatially structured electromagnetic modes, and solvent effects. While environmental factors and nuclear motion reshape the signature, the current is not a passive response but is actively influenced by coupled molecular dynamics. Our work establishes current as a tool to interpret and control strong light-matter interactions in nanoscale junctions.

Summary. This paper analyzes the complex dynamics of the McKean model by incorporating both diffusion and stochastic resetting into a slow-fast framework. Using adiabatic approximations and Fokker-Planck methods, the authors determine how the system's long-term behavior is governed by the interplay between noise, resetting, and the system's inherent slow dynamics. This provides a theoretical understanding of noise-induced steady states in non-equilibrium systems.

Why it may be interesting. The techniques used—analyzing non-equilibrium steady states in slow-fast systems with external stochastic driving (resetting)—are highly relevant to open quantum systems and quantum dynamics where dissipation and external noise are key components.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the combined effects of stochastic resetting and diffusion on a slow-fast dynamical system described by the McKean model.

Main result. The slow variable's long-time behavior converges to a stable fixed point dependent on diffusion, resetting rate, and reset value, or exhibits noisy oscillations in the non-diffusive case.

Method. The analysis employs the adiabatic approximation, solving a modified Fokker-Planck equation for the fast variable's Non-Equilibrium Steady State (NESS), and then averaging this NESS to derive an equation for the slow dynamics.

Model / system. The system is a stochastic version of the piecewise-linear McKean model, a slow-fast planar system involving a slow variable (w) and a fast variable (v). The fast variable is subjected to Gaussian white noise (diffusion D) and stochastic resetting (Poisson process rate r).

Key observables. Non-Equilibrium Steady State (NESS) probability density, the stable fixed point of the slow variable ($w^*$), and the slow variable's time evolution (oscillations).

Important parameters / regimes. Diffusivity (D), resetting rate (r), reset value ($v_r$), and the timescale separation parameter ($\epsilon$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary approximations include the adiabatic approximation (assuming fast resetting) and analyzing the system in the small-diffusion limit.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the phase-plane dynamics, time series showing slow variable switching, and analytical profiles of stationary branch densities in the non-diffusive slow-resetting regime.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the deterministic slow-fast model, then analyzes the fast-resetting regime by deriving the NESS and averaging to find the slow fixed point. It extends this to the diffusive case and finally analyzes the slow-resetting regime, deriving analytical solutions for the resulting noisy oscillations.

Abstract

In this paper we investigate the combined effects of stochastic resetting and diffusion on a slow--fast dynamical system given by the piecewise-linear McKean model. That is, the fast variable $v$ is subject to Gaussian white noise with effective diffusivity $D$ and is reset to a fixed value $v_r$ at a random sequence of times generated from a Poisson process with rate $r$. Assuming that resetting occurs on the fast timescale, we freeze the slow variable \(w\) under an adiabatic approximation and determine the resulting non-equilibrium stationary state (NESS) of the fast variable as the solution of a modified Fokker--Planck equation. We show that the NESS can be expressed in terms of parabolic cylinder functions, whose asymptotic behavior allows us to recover the corresponding NESS without diffusion in the small-diffusion limit. The NESS is used to derive an averaged equation for the slow dynamics, whose solution converges to a stable fixed point \(w^*\) that depends on \(D\), \(r\) and \(v_r\). This fixed point effectively determines the long-time behaviour of the full system. Finally, we analyze the regime in which resetting occurs on the same timescale as the slow variable. We show how the slow variable now undergoes noisy oscillations due to resetting-induced switching between branches of the fast nullcline and derive the corresponding NESS for $w$ in the non-diffusive case.

Summary. This theoretical study analyzes angular momentum conservation in two-atom superradiance, finding that the total angular momentum is conserved only when the orbital angular momentum of the atoms is included. By comparing results from source-field and Schrödinger picture methods, the authors demonstrate that the missing angular momentum is precisely accounted for by the atoms' orbital motion.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed, rigorous treatment of angular momentum conservation in an open quantum system (superradiance), showing how non-local effects (orbital angular momentum) must be included for a complete picture, which is highly relevant for quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the conservation of total angular momentum during two-atom superradiance when the atoms are separated along the x-axis.

Main result. Angular momentum is not necessarily conserved in the field alone; the missing angular momentum is accounted for by the orbital angular momentum of the two-atom system.

Method. The authors employ two complementary theoretical approaches—a source-field approach and a Schrödinger picture approach—to calculate the final field and orbital angular momentum.

Model / system. The system consists of two identical atoms, each with a J=0 ground state and a J=1 excited state, excited by a $\sigma^+$-polarized laser pulse. The analysis models the interaction dynamics using coupled differential equations for state amplitudes and density matrices.

Key observables. Final angular momentum in the field ($\langle L_{ ext{field}} angle$), initial angular momentum in the two-atom system ($\langle L_{ ext{atoms}} angle$), and the final orbital angular momentum ($ ext{L}_{ ext{orb}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The separation distance $R_{21}$ relative to the wavelength $\lambda$ (parameter $x_0 = k_0R_{21}$), and the initial excitation conditions (Double Excitation (DE) vs. Single Excitation (SE)).

Assumptions / limitations. The calculations assume infinitely massive atoms (neglecting atomic motion) and utilize approximations like the Rotating Wave Approximation (RWA) and Weisskopf-Wigner Approximation (WWA).

Figures summary. Figures schematically illustrate the geometry of the two atoms on the x-axis and show the excitation exchange processes between magnetic sublevels.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by deriving evolution equations for state amplitudes and density matrices, solving these equations for different initial conditions, and then calculating the final field and orbital angular momentum using both source-field and Schrödinger picture formalisms.

Abstract

Two-atom superradiance is studied for atoms having a J=0 ground state and a J=1 excited state. The atoms lie on the x-axis and are excited by a σ_{+}-polarized laser pulse propagating in the z-direction. We use two, complementary approaches to obtain the final angular momentum in the field, a source-field approach and a Schrodinger picture approach. Both approaches give identical answers, but it is necessary to include terms in both calculations that, at first glance, might not be expected to contribute. We show that, owing to an exchange between a state in which atom 1 is in its m=1 excited state and atom 2 is in its ground state to one in which atom 2 is in its m=-1 excited state and atom 1 in its ground state, the final angular momentum in the field is not necessarily equal to the initial angular momentum in the two-atom system. The missing angular momentum appears as orbital angular momentum of the two atoms.

Summary. This paper details a novel, cost-optimized method for preparing the ground state of complex quantum systems using engineered dissipation. By combining linear programming with locality constraints derived from Lieb-Robinson bounds, the authors drastically reduce simulation errors and computational overhead compared to previous techniques. This advances the practical feasibility of quantum simulation for many-body physics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides concrete, resource-aware techniques for simulating open quantum systems, directly impacting the feasibility of quantum simulation algorithms for chemistry and materials science.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental challenge of preparing the ground state of a quantum many-body system using dissipative dynamics, while minimizing the computational cost associated with simulating the required quantum operations.

Main result. The authors introduce an optimized filter design that significantly reduces the worst-case heating response (leakage) by over 116-fold at equal cost, and achieves substantial cost reductions (up to 56%) across various molecular and chain systems.

Method. The approach combines linear programming to optimize filter weights in the time domain with leveraging Lieb-Robinson bounds to restrict the required quantum evolution to local spatial patches.

Model / system. The system involves quantum many-body Hamiltonians whose dynamics are driven to the ground state via a Lindbladian coupling to a bath. The analysis focuses on constructing and optimizing filtered jump operators that approximate the desired ground-state evolution.

Key observables. Ground-state fidelity, worst-case heating response (leakage), and computational cost (e.g., Toffolis per jump).

Important parameters / regimes. Spectral gap ($\Delta_{ ext{spec}}$), patch radius ($R$), and the time grid size ($T$).

Assumptions / limitations. The cost model excludes outer loop overheads, and the locality-exploiting algorithms are distinct from the patch bounds used for the jump operators.

Figures summary. The notes reference tables comparing costs for different filter types (e.g., TFIM, Heisenberg) and showing scaling laws based on lattice size and constraints.

Paper structure. The paper builds from defining the problem (ground-state preparation) to proposing three key observations (LP optimization, Lieb-Robinson bounds, stability under truncation), culminating in numerical validation and cost analysis.

Abstract

Ground-state preparation is a fundamental challenge in quantum many-body physics, chemistry and materials science. Dissipative state-preparation techniques engineer a Lindbladian coupling to a bath that drives the system to its ground state, needing no initial overlap. Their cost is dominated by the Hamiltonian simulations inside the filtered jump operators, since each jump is a weighted sum of Heisenberg-evolved copies, or branches, of a coupling operator. We reduce this cost by following three simple observations to their logical end. First, on a fixed time grid, linear programming optimises the branch weights, trading the heating response against the circuit cost. Second, Lieb--Robinson bounds let each branch evolve on a patch whose radius grows with its duration. Third, if the dynamics converges quickly and truncation changes the jumps only slightly, the stationary state stays near the ground state even on patches smaller than the light cone. We validate our approach numerically. On a grid of $81$ evolution times, the optimised filter reduces the worst-case heating response, or leakage, more than $116$-fold at equal cost. Across fifteen molecules and six chains, the cost falls by 27 to 56\% at matched design-grid leakage; at matched cost, the molecular leakage falls by a factor of $2.9$ to $2\times10^{3}$. Density-matrix simulations show high ground-state fidelity for both filters, and on Ising and Hubbard chains the cheaper filter keeps a ground-state population of at least $0.89$ on patches of three or four sites. The same patches fail on a Heisenberg chain whose truncation error falls more slowly with patch size and whose Lindbladian gap is 12 times smaller than the Ising chain's, pointing to a role for the gap in how far patches can be truncated. At fixed radius the per-branch cost becomes independent of system size, reducing the cost of dissipative state preparation.

Method. The approach involves developing a causal query compiler that tracks query history using techniques like clean return and active entry, combined with spatial decomposition for local gate estimation.

Model / system. The dynamics are governed by time-dependent Lindblad master equations, $\dot{ ho} = \Lambda(t) ho$, where $\Lambda(t)$ is defined by a Hamiltonian $H(t)$ and jump operators $L_\mu(t)$. The system can be modeled on $n$ lattice sites.

Key observables. Diamond-norm error ($\epsilon$), query complexity, and elementary gate count/depth/space.

Important parameters / regimes. Total time $T$, generator variation parameters ($K_t, B$), and the time scale $ au = T(\alpha_H + \sum_\mu \alpha_\mu^2)$.

Abstract

We construct a causal query compiler for time-dependent Lindblad dynamics and establish query and gate bounds under distinct input models. With coherent block encodings of the Hamiltonian and individual jump operators, a Lipschitz generator can be simulated to diamond-norm error $\varepsilon$ using $O(1+τ+\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log(e+\log(1/\varepsilon)/τ))$ controlled queries, where $τ=T(α_H+\sum_μα_μ^2)$. This bound is independent of the time-grid size and is worst-case optimal for $τ\ge1$. Weak active entry and clean return yield a factorial history tail; combining two reuse lengths removes the auxiliary input. A three-call polar correction supplies the Lindblad cells. The compiler also characterizes the minimum oracle action of bounded adaptive Markovian protocols by quantum query complexity and the general adversary bound, up to constant factors. For finite-range dynamics on $n$ lattice sites, efficient coherent evaluation of the local matrices and fixed local parameters give $O(n(T+1)\operatorname{polylog}X)$ elementary gates, where $X=\max\{e,n(T+1)(1+K_t+B)/\varepsilon\}$, for piecewise Hölder generators with fixed exponent, local variation bound $K_t$, and at most $B$ breakpoints per term. The jumps need not commute. This gate bound includes evaluation, arithmetic, selection, routing, and environment storage. Its proof combines a spatial decomposition on a common bath, compilation on occupied inputs, and routing on compressed records. Reusing bath storage between short segments gives depth $(T+1)\operatorname{polylog}X$ and space $n\operatorname{polylog}X$.

Abstract

Non-Hermitian skin effect, the accumulation of bulk eigenstates at edges under open boundary conditions, has been observed across classical, quantum, and synthetic platforms. Whether it can be generalized to the genuine time dimension remains open. We propose a spatiotemporal NHSE in the Floquet phase space via Floquet engineering of a ring-trapped ultracold atomic gas. Placing spatial and temporal degrees of freedom on an equal footing, we show that a single parent skin effect projects onto either domain. The spatial projection yields an NHSE with arbitrarily tunable skin locations, eliminating the need for physical open boundaries. The temporal projection gives time-domain nonreciprocity and periodic temporal funneling, consistent with causality. This establishes a unified spatiotemporal paradigm for NHSE and opens a route to non-Hermitian topological physics in time.

Abstract
