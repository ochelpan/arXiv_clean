Common causes for quantum identical particles

Authors: A. Hovhannisyan, S. Weigert, A. E. Allahverdyan
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21768v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper examines the Principle of Common Cause (PCC) applied to joint probabilities measured on two identical quantum particles. It finds that due to the required permutation symmetry of observables, a single common cause variable is either non-existent or trivial. This suggests that explaining all observed correlations requires assuming effective distinguishability between the particles.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles fundamental issues at the intersection of quantum foundations and causality (PCC), which is crucial for understanding how local hidden variables might manifest in multi-particle quantum systems, even when measurements are commutative.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether joint probabilities measured on two identical quantum particles can be explained by a single common cause, contrasting this with non-identical particles and addressing the constraints imposed by quantum mechanics.

Main result. The analysis concludes that either symmetric common causes do not necessarily exist (suggesting hidden distinguishability), or any existing symmetric screening variables are trivial, meaning no single common cause can explain all measured correlations.

Method. It applies the Probabilistic Common Cause (PCC) framework to joint probabilities derived from measurements on identical particles, comparing results obtained by assuming particle labels versus using label-free event descriptions.

Model / system. The system involves two identical quantum particles whose observables must be permutation-symmetric. The analysis uses projectors related to spatial and internal spin degrees of freedom within a tensor-product Hilbert space.

Key observables. Joint probabilities $p(A, B)$ derived from measurements; screening variables $C$ used in the common cause factorization.

Important parameters / regimes. The requirement for permutation symmetry on observables and the structure of the joint probability distributions.

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is testing the applicability of the PCC framework to quantum correlations, particularly when restricted by particle indistinguishability (permutation symmetry).

Figures summary. Not specified in the provided notes.

Paper structure. The paper moves from discussing non-identical particles and Bell violations to focusing on identical particles. It compares labeled vs. event-based common cause approaches, ultimately concluding that the constraints imposed by quantum indistinguishability severely limit or eliminate the possibility of a single common cause variable.

Abstract

Violations of Bell's inequalities imply that joint probabilities generated by non-commutative measurements on two (non-identical) quantum particles do not have a single common cause. But joint probabilities generated for such non-identical particles via commutative measurements do have non-trivial common cause variables. We focus on commutative measurements and consider two identical quantum particles, whose density matrices and observables (hermitian operators) are necessarily permutation-symmetric. It is natural to demand that the common cause describing joint probabilities is also permutation symmetric, i.e., it acts symmetrically on both particles. Looking at various ways of defining joint probabilities from the same measurement data, we conclude that either symmetric common causes need not exist (i.e., that the particles can be hiddenly distinguishable), or that symmetric screening variables exist, but they are trivial, i.e., no single common cause can explain all single-measurement correlations.

Quantum Metric Bound State of Light Confinement

Summary. The paper introduces the concept of the 'quantum metric bound state,' establishing that when perfect localization fails due to disorder, the spatial decay rate of confined waves is universally limited by the bulk quantum metric. By deriving this rigorous lower bound using advanced spectral methods, it provides a powerful, predictive design principle for engineering wave confinement in synthetic platforms.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects fundamental concepts from algebraic geometry (quantum metrics) directly to measurable physical properties like mode decay length in engineered photonic or electronic systems, providing a powerful new diagnostic tool for wave engineering.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To establish a universal, model-independent geometric lower bound for the spatial decay length of defect-induced bound states in flat-band lattices when perfect localization conditions are broken by generic impurities.

Main result. The exponential decay length ($\xi_b$) is rigorously proven to be lower-bounded by the bulk quantum metric ($\ell_{QM}$), establishing $\xi_b \gtrsim C \cdot \ell_{QM}$.

Method. The analysis uses Green's function formalism, complex momentum continuation (pole structure analysis), and Bernstein-type inequalities applied to band projection operators.

Model / system. The study models light confinement in photonic lattices, using the Lieb lattice as a primary example. The physics is governed by a tight-binding Hamiltonian analyzed via Schrödinger-like equations with local impurities breaking perfect symmetry.

Key observables. Confinement Length Scales ($\xi_b$), Quantum Metric Length ($\ell_{QM}$), and the decay rate derived from pole singularities in the complex momentum plane.

Important parameters / regimes. The bound depends on the algebraic degree $N$ of the projection operator, leading to a scaling $\xi_b \gtrsim 4/N^2 \cdot \ell_{QM}$.

Assumptions / limitations. Assumes weak-guidance conditions and that the flat band projection operator belongs to a rational trigonometric space for the rigorous bounding techniques.

Figures summary. Figures compare decay lengths in three regimes (dispersive, CLS, Quantum Metric Bound State) and show simulations confirming the geometric floor set by $\ell_{QM}$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the problem of confinement breakdown to deriving a mathematical lower bound using advanced spectral theory (Bernstein-type inequalities), culminating in demonstrating that this metric provides a universal, predictive design principle.

Abstract

The spatial confinement of defect-induced bound states is conventionally governed by the effective mass in dispersive bands. More recently, Compact Localized States (CLSs) arising from exact destructive interference have been utilized to achieve confinement in flat bands. However, CLSs rely on pristine lattice symmetries and fine-tuned defect profiles. The introduction of a generic local impurity inevitably breaks these strict phase-matching conditions, resulting in extensive bound states whose fundamental length scale has remained an open question. Here, we establish a third regime of confinement: the quantum metric bound state. We provide a rigorous mathematical proof demonstrating that in the absence of kinetic energy and CLS protection, the exponential decay length of these states is lower-bounded by the quantum metric of the unperturbed flat band. We demonstrate the tightness of this geometric limit by constructing a family of highly tunable flat-band generators, and we verify its universality across diverse realistic architectures. Ultimately, this classification establishes the independently measurable quantum metric as a predictive design principle for engineering confined modes in synthetic wave platforms.

Summary. This paper proposes a refined model for particle dispersal by combining subdiffusive fractional Brownian motion with stopping times whose rates are drawn from a Gamma distribution. This heterogeneity successfully generates the heavy-tailed power-law decay necessary to describe rare, long-distance events while retaining the localized clumping characteristic of the underlying subdiffusion process.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on physical dispersal, the mathematical techniques used—analyzing heavy tails arising from multiplicative noise or randomized rates in anomalous diffusion processes—are highly relevant to non-equilibrium statistical mechanics problems involving complex particle interactions or energy dissipation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a model for dispersal kernels that accurately describe both localized clumping and rare, long-distance dispersal events by generating heavy tails.

Main result. By introducing heterogeneity in the stopping rate (making it Gamma-distributed), the resulting radial density function exhibits a power-law decay $g(r) \sim r^{-1 - a/H}$, confirming a heavy-tailed distribution.

Method. The analysis involves deriving and performing asymptotic analysis on the radial probability density function by combining subdiffusive fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with randomized exponential stopping times.

Model / system. The system models particle dispersal using subdiffusive fBm ($H < 1/2$) where the stopping time rate ($\lambda$) is drawn from a Gamma distribution. This combination generates the required heavy-tailed statistics for realistic environmental modeling.

Key observables. Radial probability density function $g(r)$ and its tail behavior, specifically the power-law decay $r^{-1 - a/H}$ as $r o \infty$.

Important parameters / regimes. Hurst exponent ($H$), Gamma distribution parameters ($a, b$), and the diffusion parameter ($\sigma$). The regime of interest is subdiffusion ($0 < H < 1/2$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on approximations valid in the large-$r$ limit (e.g., $t \gg b$) and assumes that the underlying movement follows fBm.

Figures summary. Figures compare simulation results for randomized vs. constant stopping rates, showing clear heavy tails only when rate randomization is included. Hill plots and Mean-excess plots are used to diagnose this tail behavior.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the failure of simple models (exponential decay) for long-distance dispersal, proposes randomizing the stopping rate parameter ($\lambda \sim ext{Gamma}$), derives the asymptotic power-law tail $g(r) \sim r^{-1 - a/H}$, and validates this result through both analytical methods and numerical simulations.

Abstract

Subdiffusive fractional Brownian motions produce localized aggregation when particles are stopped at exponentially distributed times. In applications where clumping and long-distance dispersal events are observed simultaneously, such as in some instances of seed dispersal, this model fails to describe the tails of the data. The resulting redistribution kernel has only an exponentially decaying tail, whereas a heavier tail is needed for modeling the long-distance dispersal observed. Here we propose a model in which subdiffusive particles stop at exponentially distributed times, but with a rate parameter that is Gamma distributed. This heterogeneity in stopping rates causes the density of final radial positions to have a heavy-tailed distribution. Our model retains the strong localized clumping characteristic of subdiffusive fractional Brownian motion while simultaneously generating the heavy tails required for realistic long-distance dispersal.

Summary. This work analyzes stochastic Turing patterns in reaction-diffusion systems, showing that integrating fluctuations over time reveals a hidden large-scale order. This emergent structure exhibits effective hyperuniformity, meaning long-wavelength density variations are suppressed by a finite kinetic floor. The methodology uses advanced statistical tools like the structure factor analysis within the Linear Noise Approximation.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical statistical mechanics, the concept of emergent organization via filtering (time integration) is analogous to how quantum correlations or entanglement can be revealed by time evolution measurements in open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how robust, large-scale spatial organization emerges from stochastic Turing patterns when considering time integration of fluctuating local populations.

Main result. Time integration acts as a dynamical filter that reveals an emergent effective hyperuniformity in the density fluctuations, characterized by a $1/R$ decay in variance over intermediate scales.

Method. The analysis uses the Linear Noise Approximation (LNA) applied to stochastic reaction-diffusion equations and analyzes the time-integrated structure factor in Fourier space.

Model / system. The model is a generic two-species stochastic reaction-diffusion process, exemplified by the Levin-Segel predator-prey system, evolving on a lattice with periodic boundary conditions.

Key observables. Time-integrated demographic fluctuations ($\Xi(X)_r(T)$), structure factor $S(X)_q(T)$, and intensive variance $ ext{var}(X)(R; T)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio of diffusion rates ($ u/\mu$) relative to the deterministic instability point, and the integration time $T$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the Linear Noise Approximation (LNA) and assumes that the system reaches a steady state for the structure factor in the long-time limit ($T o \infty$).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the emergence of patterns in time-integrated vs. instantaneous snapshots, showing variance decay as $1/\ell$ over increasingly broad regions near instability.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mathematical framework using LNA for stochastic fluctuations, derives the structure factor for time-integrated configurations, and then applies this to the Levin-Segel model to demonstrate the transition from instantaneous noise to long-range hyperuniform order.

Abstract

Demographic noise generates stochastic Turing patterns even when reaction-diffusion systems are deterministically stable. We show analytically and verify numerically in the Levin-Segel model that temporal integration of configurations reveals emergent large-scale organization. The intensive number variance in a window of size $R \gg 1$ approaches a finite reaction-kinetic floor as $1/R$, over a spatial range growing by orders of magnitude near the Turing instability. This yields an effectively hyperuniform, fine-tuning-free regime previously unidentified in non-conserved multispecies stochastic systems.

Skewness tunes the small-drift record rate of random walks and Lévy flights

Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23553v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · non-equilibrium dynamics 1/5

Summary. This theoretical paper determines the precise small-drift scaling law for the rate at which random walks set new records. By utilizing Mellin transforms on a harmonic sum identity, the authors show that this record rate's exponent is continuously controlled by the skewness of the step distribution via the positivity parameter $ ho$. This unifies results across Gaussian, heavy-tailed, and skewed random walk regimes.

Why it may be interesting. While not directly quantum mechanical, the rigorous mathematical framework connecting asymptotic scaling laws (like record rates) to underlying distributional symmetries ($\alpha, eta$) is highly relevant for understanding critical phenomena or non-equilibrium dynamics in complex quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the rate at which a random walk or Lévy flight sets new records (the record rate, $\lambda(\mu)$) depends on small positive drift ($\mu$). It specifically analyzes this dependence as the drift approaches zero.

Main result. The record rate exhibits an asymptotic power-law scaling $\lambda(\mu) \sim K\mu^{(1- ho)/ u}$, where the exponent is continuously tuned by the skewness of the underlying step distribution through the positivity parameter $ ho$.

Method. The analysis heavily relies on using Mellin transforms applied to the harmonic sum derived from the Spitzer–Baxter identity, allowing for a unified treatment across different types of random walks.

Model / system. The model is a general random walk $S_n$ with i.i.d. steps $\xi_i$, which can be characterized by their limiting stable law index ($\alpha$) and skewness ($eta$). The analysis covers both diffusive (Gaussian) and heavy-tailed Lévy flight regimes.

Key observables. The record rate $\lambda(\mu)$, the expected maximum $E(M_N)$, and the exponent governing the small-$\mu$ decay of these quantities.

Important parameters / regimes. Drift ($\mu$), stable law index ($\alpha \in (1, 2]$), positivity parameter ($ ho = P(Y>0)$), and skewness ($eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The results are exact for Gaussian and strictly stable steps but extend to the domains of attraction at the leading power level. The analysis assumes the applicability of Mellin transform techniques to factorize the relevant harmonic sums.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates record setting in a biased random walk as $\mu o 0$. Figure 2 shows the poles of the Mellin transform, distinguishing the leading law from correction terms. Figure 3 and 4 map the exponent's dependence on skewness ($eta$) across different stable indices ($\alpha$).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the connection between the record rate and a harmonic sum via the Spitzer–Baxter identity. It then employs Mellin transforms to analyze this sum in the small-drift limit, deriving the leading power law dependence on $\mu$ and showing how skewness controls the exponent through $ ho$.

Abstract

A random walk with small positive drift $μ$ sets new records at a rate $λ(μ)$ that vanishes as $μ\to 0$. For centered steps attracted to a stable law $Y$ with index $1 < α\leq 2$ and positivity parameter $ρ= P(Y>0)$, we find $λ(μ) \sim Kμ^{(1-ρ)/ν}$, $ν=1-1/α$, as $μ\to 0$. The result is exact for Gaussian and strictly stable steps, and extends at the leading-power level to their domains of attraction. The exponent is set by the asymmetry only through $ρ$, sweeping the interval $[1,\,1/(α-1)]$ as the skewness varies. It recovers the Gaussian linear law with slope $\sqrt{2}$ and, for symmetric heavy tails, the power $μ^{α/2(α-1)}$; beyond the stable tail ratio, distributional details enter through the prefactor $K$, which is explicit for strictly stable steps. The result follows directly from one Mellin transform of the harmonic sum in the Spitzer-Baxter identity, which factorizes into a kernel transform and a Riemann $ζ$ factor whose poles deliver at once the leading law, its prefactor, and a correction ladder, unifying diffusive, heavy-tailed, and skewed walks. The same transform also yields the expected maximum, recovering Kingman's heavy-traffic law for queues and Siegmund's corrected-diffusion constant as adjacent poles.

Evaluation of Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC) for Cyberattack Detection in the NISQ Era

Authors: Angelos Thomos, Theodore Andronikos

Summary. This work evaluates Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC) for cyberattack detection using limited qubits. The study demonstrates that forcing high-dimensional network data into a small quantum state via amplitude encoding severely limits the model's representational capacity compared to classical neural networks. The authors conclude that future quantum advantage requires abandoning aggressive feature compression in favor of broader Hilbert space representations.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on cybersecurity, the core technical challenge—quantifying the expressibility gap between quantum circuits and classical models when data is compressed—is highly relevant to understanding resource limitations in variational quantum algorithms for complex physical simulations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the structural limits of Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC) for detecting network anomalies in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era.

Main result. Aggressive data compression via Amplitude Encoding into a small number of qubits severely restricts the VQC's expressiveness, leading to performance ceilings significantly below classical benchmarks.

Method. The authors developed and tested a 4-qubit VQC using amplitude encoding on the NSL-KDD dataset, comparing its performance against classical MLPs and analyzing optimization convergence.

Model / system. The model is a hybrid quantum-classical circuit consisting of state preparation (Amplitude Encoding), a trainable parameterized ansatz (RealAmplitudes), and Z-basis measurement. It operates on NISQ devices simulated via statevector simulators.

Key observables. Binary classification accuracy (88% achieved vs 97% classical MLP); in-sample diagnostic accuracy (9%); mode collapse behavior.

Important parameters / regimes. 4 qubits, 24 trainable parameters, 16 principal components (from PCA), COBYLA/SPSA optimizers.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation is the aggressive dimensionality reduction imposed by amplitude encoding; furthermore, the results do not isolate the encoding from ansatz depth or optimizer budget.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate t-SNE projections of class overlaps and confusion matrices comparing VQC performance against classical baselines (Tiny MLP).

Paper structure. The paper systematically details data preprocessing (PCA, Target Encoding), constructs the 4-qubit VQC pipeline, evaluates it using multiple optimizers, compares results to classical MLPs, and concludes by proposing a shift towards uncompressed angle encoding.

Abstract

This paper investigates the effectiveness and structural limits of Variational Quantum Classifiers (VQC) for detecting network anomalies in the era of Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) systems. Using the official 20\% research subset of the NSL-KDD dataset, a 4-qubit classifier featuring a 24-parameter trainable ansatz was developed, utilizing amplitude encoding to embed 16 principal components. On a held-out partition of this subset, the model achieved a consistent binary-classification accuracy of 88\%. A comparative evaluation with two fundamentally different optimizers, COBYLA and SPSA, found that the observed performance plateau is not attributable to convergence to local minima, a result we interpret as consistent with an encoding-related expressibility limit rather than an optimization artifact. An architectural parity comparison with a classical neural network (a Tiny MLP with four nodes, achieving 97\% accuracy) highlighted the expressiveness gap associated with data overcompression into restricted quantum states. The model was trained on a class-balanced set spanning the 22 raw NSL-KDD attack categories and evaluated in-sample as a probe of representational capacity: under this configuration the VQC reached only 9\% accuracy and exhibited a degenerate mode collapse onto a small subset of classes. We interpret this behavior as consistent with the loss of linear separability induced by excessive compression in the quantum probability space, while explicitly noting that our experiments do not isolate the encoding from the ansatz depth, optimizer budget, and measurement-decoding scheme (see Limitations). Motivated by these observations and by Cover's theorem, we outline an alternative paradigm: a 16-qubit VQC with angle encoding that expands the Hilbert-space representation rather than relying on aggressive classical dimensionality reduction.

Quantum Advantage in Tolerant Junta Testing

Authors: Avishay Tal, Weiqiang Yuan
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23194v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 2/5 · quantum measurements 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

Summary. This paper proves a super-polynomial quantum advantage for testing whether a Boolean function is close to being defined by only $k$ variables (a $k$-junta). It achieves this using an efficient quantum tester and establishes the lower bound classically by constructing hard distributions based on error-correcting codes. This work significantly advances the understanding of computational limits in property testing.

Why it may be interesting. Although focused on computational complexity theory, the techniques used—especially constructing hard distributions and proving query complexity gaps—are foundational to understanding the limits of information extraction from complex physical measurements or noisy data sets in quantum physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper establishes a super-polynomial quantum advantage for solving the tolerant $k$-junta testing problem in an adaptive setting.

Main result. It shows that the problem can be solved with $ ext{poly}(k)$ quantum queries, while any classical algorithm requires at least $k^{\Omega(\log k)}$ queries under specific parameter regimes.

Method. The quantum advantage is demonstrated using a specialized non-adaptive quantum tester adapted from prior work. The corresponding classical lower bound relies on constructing hard distributions for 'yes' instances based on error-correcting codes and applying Yao's minimax lemma.

Model / system. The model involves black-box access to a Boolean function $f: \{0, 1\}^n o \{\pm 1\}$, which is tested for its proximity (tolerance) to being defined by only $k$ variables (a $k$-junta).

Key observables. Query complexity (number of oracle calls), correlation $ ext{corr}(f, J_T)$, and the parameters defining closeness ($\varepsilon_1$) and distance ($\varepsilon_2$).

Important parameters / regimes. The regime requires $0 \le \varepsilon_1 < \varepsilon_2 \le 1/2$, particularly focusing on small gaps $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1$.

Assumptions / limitations. The quantum tester used is non-adaptive, and the super-polynomial classical lower bound requires a sub-constant gap between $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$.

Paper structure. The paper reviews standard junta testing, introduces tolerant testing for noisy data, presents Theorem 1 establishing the quantum advantage via both an efficient quantum tester and a novel classical lower bound based on hard distributions.

Abstract

We establish the first super-polynomial quantum advantage for the tolerant junta testing problem in the adaptive setting. Specifically, we show that within a certain parameter regime, tolerant $k$-junta testing with high precision can be solved using $\mathrm{poly}(k)$ quantum queries, whereas any classical algorithm requires at least $k^{Ω(\log k)}$ queries. The problem of tolerant $k$-junta testing is as follows: given parameters $(k, ε_1, ε_2)$, with $0\le ε_1<ε_2 \le 1/2$, and black-box access to a Boolean function $f$ (defined on $n$ variables), distinguish whether $f$ is $ε_1$-close to some $k$-junta or $ε_2$-far from every $k$-junta. We show the quantum advantage for a range of parameters close to $1/2$, for example, $ε_1 = 1/2-1/k$ and $ε_2 = 1/2-1/(2k^2)$. The (non-adaptive) quantum tester we use was given by a recent work of Bao, Liu, Yao, Ye, and Zhang (SOSA 2026). We slightly adapt their analysis to show that it holds in the above parameter regime. On the other hand, our classical lower bound requires substantial new ideas. Inspired by the lower bound techniques of Chen and Patel (FOCS 2023), we introduce a new hard distribution of yes'' instances (i.e., instances with distance at most $ε_1$ to $k$-juntas) that is based on planting anapproximate-junta'' as follows: we randomly pick $k$ out of $n$ coordinates, and for each fixing of the $k$ coordinates, the $2^{n-k}$ values in the restricted subcube are drawn randomly except for the set of points in an error-correcting code on which we place the same random bit. We show that this distribution is much closer to $k$-juntas than the uniform distribution, but on the other hand, they are indistinguishable with respect to any classical algorithm making $k^{o(\log k)}$ queries.

Summary. This paper tackles the practical challenge of Quantum Key Distribution when communicating parties lack a shared reference frame over noisy channels. The authors introduce two robust methods—PTM analysis and Sequential Basis Matching—to determine optimal local measurement bases. They prove these methods yield equivalent, enhanced key rates compared to standard protocols, significantly improving theoretical performance estimates for real-world quantum links.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to quantum optics and open quantum systems because it provides a rigorous framework for optimizing communication protocols under realistic, non-ideal environmental constraints (like relative motion in satellite links) that violate idealized assumptions of perfect alignment.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is establishing secret keys via Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) when communication occurs over an unknown, stationary, unital qubit channel without the communicating parties sharing a global reference frame.

Main result. Both proposed methods—Pauli Transfer Matrix (PTM) analysis and Sequential Basis Matching (SBM)—yield equivalent results, demonstrating that optimized local signaling can recover positive key rates even when standard assumptions about basis alignment fail.

Method. The authors propose two complementary theoretical approaches: one using the PTM derived from channel characterization via SVD, and another operational approach called SBM that iteratively optimizes measurement bases based on observed error probabilities.

Model / system. The system models QKD over a qubit channel characterized by unknown unitaries ($U_A, U_B$) and unital noise ($\mathcal{N}$). The analysis relies heavily on the Pauli basis representation of quantum states and channels.

Key observables. Secret key rates (for BB84 and six-state protocols), Quantum Bit Error Rates (QBERs) in X, Y, and Z bases, and the effective channel's PTM parameters.

Important parameters / regimes. The channel noise strength ($\epsilon$), which quantifies the deviation from ideal transmission; the number of measurement shots ($N_s$) for finite-sample analysis.

Assumptions / limitations. The quantum channel is assumed to be stationary and unital. The primary challenge addressed is the violation of the shared reference frame assumption, rather than modeling specific physical noise sources like loss or dark counts.

Figures summary. Figures compare secret key rates vs. channel parameter $\epsilon$ for standard versus optimized signaling; they also show QBER sets and finite-sample estimations demonstrating convergence to the theoretical PTM rate.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical challenge (QKD without shared reference frame). It then details two mathematical approaches: 1) Constructing the effective channel via PTM/SVD, and 2) Implementing SBM for sequential basis optimization. Finally, it compares the results and analyzes performance robustness.

Abstract

We consider a general and practical scenario of quantum key distribution (QKD) over an unknown, stationary, unital qubit channel. Furthermore, due to practical limitations, e.g., relative movement and rotation of communicating parties, a global shared reference frame cannot be established. This scenario can routinely appear in satellite QKD. We propose two methods to overcome the physical qubit noise and the lack of shared reference frame. The first proposed approach involves constructing the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) description of the channel, which we achieve without requiring a shared reference frame, by absorbing the lack of shared reference frame in the channel definition. This is followed by the identification of singular vectors of PTM as the Bloch vectors for optimal signal states. In the optimized local bases, the resulting correlations are equivalent, up to outcome relabeling, to those of a Pauli channel, allowing us to show the optimality of the BB84 and six-state QKD protocols under these conditions. The second approach, called the sequential basis matching (SBM) involves sequentially identifying the channel-optimized local bases that enable QKD. We show that both of these approaches result in the same effective key exchange rate for QKD.

Abstract

Understanding dynamical properties of quantum many-body systems remains a central challenge because excitations generally require information beyond a ground-state wave function. Here we introduce a neural polaron ansatz that represents quasiparticle excitations by neural many-body operators acting on a correlated ground state. Abstract

Abstract

Understanding particle-induced damage tracks in solid-state materials underpins emerging applications in rare-event detection and quantum defect engineering. Resolving these tracks requires multi-scale readout, from event localization at the millimeter scale to track-morphology reconstruction at the nanoscale. Nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centers in diamond provide such a platform, combining optical localization with quantum sensing of track morphology. Here, we implant sub-MeV carbon ions into nitrogen-rich diamond and detect individual recoil events via spatially localized NV formation. We develop a simulation framework that explains the observed NV yield and predicts that directional information is retained in the NV distribution after annealing. Measurements of NV spin coherence indicate compatibility with nanoscale track reconstruction via NV strain mapping and magnetic gradient-based techniques. These results identify promising pathways toward NV-diamond directional detectors for rare events, while the track-modeling framework has broader implications for paleodetection and quantum material synthesis.
