How Long-Range Tails Reshape Non-Hermitian Spectra

Summary. This paper demonstrates that even infinitesimally weak, long-range hopping terms can drastically reshape the energy spectrum of non-Hermitian quantum systems under open boundary conditions. It develops mathematical tools, such as the squeezed GBZ and amoeba formulation, to describe this spectral reconstruction. The findings are significant because they show that the system's fundamental properties are dictated by the competition between the hopping decay rate and the system's inherent localization scale.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a powerful, general framework for understanding how weak, non-local interactions can fundamentally change the spectral topology and transport properties of open quantum systems, which is highly relevant for engineered quantum devices.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how exponentially decaying long-range hoppings nonperturbatively reconstruct the spectrum and eigenstates of short-range non-Hermitian systems under open boundary conditions.

Main result. Long-range hoppings cause the original Generalized Brillouin Zone (GBZ) to be replaced by a 'squeezed GBZ' in 1D, and a 'squeezed amoeba formulation' in 2D, fundamentally altering the spectral density and dynamics.

Method. The analysis employs non-Bloch band theory, utilizing the Ronkin function and its minimization in the complex plane (amoeba formulation) to determine the spectral density of states.

Model / system. The model consists of non-Hermitian tight-binding systems, perturbed by long-range hopping terms decaying exponentially, $e^{-\alpha |n|}$. The analysis extends to Lindbladian open systems governed by a Liouvillian equation.

Key observables. The primary observables are the Open Boundary Condition (OBC) spectrum, the spectral density of states (DOS), and the Liouvillian gap ($\Lambda$).

Important parameters / regimes. The competition between the decay length of the long-range hoppings ($\alpha$) and the localization length of the non-Hermitian skin modes is crucial.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on treating the long-range hopping as a perturbation ($\delta H$) and using non-Bloch band theory techniques.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the squeezing of the GBZ in 1D, the comparison of DOS using the squeezed amoeba formulation in 2D, and the evolution of dynamics showing chiral damping affected by the long-range hopping strength $\alpha$.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by first analyzing the 1D case using the GBZ renormalization, then generalizing to 2D using the squeezed amoeba formulation for the DOS, and finally applying the concepts to the dynamics of open quantum systems via the Liouvillian formalism.

Abstract

Exponentially decaying long-range hoppings are ubiquitous in realistic tight-binding models and are often truncated to obtain a finite-range description. We show that this approximation can fail dramatically in non-Hermitian systems under open boundary conditions: an infinitesimal long-range hopping can nonperturbatively reconstruct the spectrum and eigenstates of a short-range non-Hermitian system. The mechanism is controlled by a competition between the decay length of infinitesimal long-range hoppings and the localization length of non-Hermitian skin modes, leading to a sharp transition as the decay rate is tuned. In one dimension, we show that a squeezed generalized Brillouin zone (GBZ) replaces the original GBZ of the short-ranged Hamiltonian, yielding the reconstructed open-boundary spectrum. In two or higher dimensions, we formulate a squeezed amoeba formulation describing the reconstructed spectral density. We further show that long-range hoppings can qualitatively reshape Green's function, which can be readily detected in experiments.

Summary. The paper investigates single-photon magnetometry using a $\Lambda$-system, comparing information extraction from photon loss versus frequency-resolved photon counting. It shows that environmental coupling can enhance sensitivity, and crucially, at critical coupling, frequency-resolved counting can approach the ultimate quantum limit by accessing information previously hidden in the spectral phase.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical framework for quantum sensing using cavity/atomic systems, specifically quantifying how different measurement observables (loss vs. spectral intensity) relate to the fundamental quantum limits (QFI) in the presence of environmental coupling.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine if frequency-resolved photon counting can extract magnetic-field information from a single-photon scattering process in a $\Lambda$-type three-level system, potentially surpassing the limits set by simple photon-loss measurements.

Main result. Frequency-resolved photon counting can capture nearly all the magnetic-field information encoded in the output state, especially at critical coupling where scattering amplitude zeros redistribute information to measurable spectral intensity.

Method. The analysis involves deriving the asymptotic output state and decomposing the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) into classical (photon-loss) and quantum (spectral-intensity/phase) contributions.

Model / system. The system is a Zeeman-sensitive $\Lambda$-type three-level atom probed by a single-photon pulse and driven by a classical control field. The dynamics are analyzed using the Hamiltonian in the rotating frame, incorporating environmental coupling.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), frequency-resolved Correlation Function of Intensity ($C_\omega(pB)$), spectral intensity, and spectral phase.

Important parameters / regimes. Environmental coupling rates ($\Gamma_g, \Gamma_\perp$), detunings ($\Delta_g, \delta$), and the ratio $\Gamma_\perp/\Gamma_g$ (especially at critical coupling).

Assumptions / limitations. Approximations include neglecting spontaneous branching from $|e angle o |s angle$ and lower-state coherence dephasing, and analyzing the long-pulse limit using Gaussian pulse profiles.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the schematic of the magnetometry setup, the spectral densities of the output fields at different magnetic fields, and the frequency-resolved CFI plotted against the ratio of coupling rates ($\Gamma_\perp/\Gamma_g$).

Paper structure. The paper progresses by setting up the physical model and Hamiltonian, deriving the asymptotic output state, decomposing the QFI, and then analyzing the specific measurement scheme of frequency-resolved photon counting, culminating in the comparison between the achievable CFI and the theoretical QFI.

Abstract

We investigate pulsed single-photon magnetometry with a Zeeman-sensitive $Λ$-type three-level system driven by a classical control field. We derive the asymptotic output state and decompose its quantum Fisher information into photon-loss, spectral-intensity, and spectral-phase contributions. Environmental coupling reshapes the scattering response and can increase magnetic-field information. At critical coupling, real-frequency zeros of the scattering amplitude redistribute information toward measurable spectral intensity, allowing frequency-resolved photon counting to capture nearly all of the magnetic-field information encoded in the output state when the zeros lie within the pulse bandwidth. For long Gaussian pulses with a smooth, nonzero central-frequency scattering amplitude, the additional spectral-intensity contribution and residual spectral-phase information gap decrease as $T^{-2}$ or faster.

Summary. This paper develops a novel theoretical method using QEDFT to incorporate cavity vacuum fluctuations into the description of vdW materials. Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges quantum optics (cavity vacuum fluctuations) with condensed matter structure (vdW materials), providing a theoretical tool to engineer material properties using fundamental quantum field effects, which is highly relevant to AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study addresses how vacuum fluctuations from optical cavities can modify the ground-state structural properties of van der Waals (vdW) materials without requiring external driving fields.

Main result. The authors predict that cavity vacuum fluctuations act as a tunable knob, causing cavity-modified stacking, increasing equilibrium interlayer distances, and softening layer breathing modes in materials like hBN and graphene.

Method. They introduce a periodic formulation of the photon many-body dispersion (pMBD) functional within the Quantum Electrodynamical Density-Functional Theory (QEDFT) framework.

Model / system. The system involves layered 2D vdW materials, specifically bilayer hBN and graphene. The theory models the interaction using a Hamiltonian adapted for periodic boundary conditions in reciprocal space (q-space).

Key observables. Binding curves, cavity-mediated energy ($\Delta ext{Exc}$), equilibrium interlayer distances, and layer breathing mode (LBM) frequencies.

Important parameters / regimes. Light-matter coupling strength ($\lambda$), cavity frequency ($\omega$), and the momentum transfer point (focusing on the $\Gamma$-point, $q=0$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes a periodic solid on a supercell and simplifies the calculation by assuming the cavity primarily influences the $\Gamma$-point ($q=0$).

Abstract

In the field of cavity quantum materials, vacuum fluctuations of optical cavities are used for modifying ground-state properties of quantum materials without external driving. Here, one example is the van der Waals (vdW)/dispersion interaction in layered 2D vdW materials, where non-additive long-range correlations can dominate the interlayer binding. While cavity-induced changes of such interactions have been predicted and described using ab initio methods for molecular systems, no efficient description exists yet for extended materials. In this Letter, we close this gap by introducing a periodic formulation of the photon many-body dispersion (pMBD) functional within quantum electrodynamical density-functional theory (QEDFT). Applying this method with efficient $\textbf{q}$-point sampling to bilayer hBN and graphene, we predict cavity-modified stacking, increased equilibrium interlayer distances, and softened layer breathing modes with increasing light-matter coupling strength. Our results establish cavity vacuum fluctuations as a tuning knob for the structural properties of vdW materials.

Summary. Researchers developed a diamond optomechanical crystal cavity to enhance quantum optomechanical coupling. By achieving self-sustained mechanical oscillations, the device generates a large-scale optical frequency comb. This demonstrates a high dynamic strain, making the platform highly promising for controlling diamond spin qubits using light.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the coupling of mechanical motion to optical fields in a solid-state quantum system (diamond), which is a cornerstone of cavity QED and optomechanics for quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The goal is to demonstrate a diamond optomechanical crystal cavity capable of generating a high-frequency optical frequency comb while achieving a large dynamic strain for future quantum control applications.

Main result. The system successfully generates an optical frequency comb spanning 143 GHz, corresponding to a maximum dynamic strain of $1.1 imes 10^{-3}$ in the mechanical oscillation.

Method. The study combines theoretical simulation (COMSOL) for device design and characterization with experimental measurements of cavity resonances and frequency comb generation under self-sustained oscillation.

Model / system. The platform is a diamond optomechanical crystal (OMC) cavity, which leverages diamond's high elastic modulus, low absorption, and inherent spin qubits. The system supports multiple optical and mechanical resonances that are coupled via the optomechanical interaction.

Key observables. Frequency comb spectrum (143 GHz span), peak mechanical oscillation amplitude (130 pm), total dynamic strain ($1.1 imes 10^{-3}$), and optomechanical coupling rate ($g_0$).

Important parameters / regimes. Mechanical resonance frequency ($\sim 12$ GHz), high mechanical quality factor ($Q_m imes f_m$), and the resolved-sideband regime ($\omega_m > \kappa_{tot}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the system operates in the resolved-sideband regime and requires careful modeling to account for photothermal shifts when determining the effective detuning.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the OMC cavity schematic, mode profiles (optical and mechanical), transmission spectra showing resonances, and the final frequency comb structure via sideband analysis.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from device design and mode characterization (simulations) to demonstrating the transition from thermal motion to self-sustained oscillation, culminating in the quantitative measurement and analysis of the generated frequency comb.

Abstract

Quantum optomechanical technologies benefit from mechanical oscillators that are high-frequency, can be coherently driven, and are capable of coupling to other quantum systems. Diamond supports all of these criteria: its large elastic modulus increases mechanical resonance frequency, its low nonlinear optical absorption increases the allowed intensity of fields used for coherent optomechanics, and it hosts spin qubits that interact with mechanical modes. Here we demonstrate a diamond optomechanical crystal cavity that supports multiple mechanical resonances with $\sim$12 GHz frequency and high $Q_\text{m} \times f_\text{m}$ product that can be coherently coupled to multiple optical modes. By exciting this sideband resolved system into mechanical self-sustained oscillations, we generate a frequency comb spanning 143 GHz. Analysis of the comb spectrum, combined with systematic characterization of the system's optomechanical coupling, allows us to quantitatively show that its mechanical oscillation amplitude reaches 130 pm. This corresponds to a maximum total dynamic strain of $1.1 \times 10^{-3}$ that is sufficiently high for future demonstrations of optomechanical control of diamond spin qubits.

Summary. This theoretical work explores how correlated disorder influences ordering in non-equilibrium active systems, specifically using the nonreciprocal XY model. By applying renormalization group techniques, the authors demonstrate that long-range disorder can stabilize long-range order in 2D and 3D, even when the clean system would otherwise exhibit only short-range order. This provides a quantitative framework for understanding disorder-induced order in driven matter.

Why it may be interesting. The study of non-equilibrium phase transitions driven by disorder is crucial for understanding complex active matter systems, which often lack simple thermal equilibrium descriptions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether quenched disorder can induce long-range order in driven, non-equilibrium systems, contrasting this effect with the influence of long-range noise.

Main result. Sufficiently long-range quenched disorder can induce long-range order in 2D nonreciprocal XY systems, and in 3D, disorder combined with nonreciprocity creates a novel transition between strong-coupling and noninteracting regimes.

Method. The analysis employs a hydrodynamic model and utilizes perturbative Dynamic Renormalization Group (RG) techniques to study the scaling behavior of the system parameters.

Model / system. The primary model is the nonreciprocal XY (NRB XY) system, described by a hydrodynamic equation. The system is driven by quenched disorder (correlated velocities) and potentially long-range noise.

Key observables. Long-range order (LRO) vs. Short-range order (SRO), dynamic relaxation exponents (super/sub-diffusive), and scaling exponents (z, $\chi$).

Important parameters / regimes. The disorder correlation exponent ($y$), the noise correlation exponent ($ar{y}$), and the disorder strength parameter ($\mu$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on a hydrodynamic description and uses perturbative RG methods, assuming non-local correlation functions for disorder and noise.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate phase diagrams in 2D and 3D space (e.g., $y-ar{y}$ or $y-\mu$ planes), mapping out boundaries between LRO and SRO phases based on disorder and noise parameters.

Paper structure. The paper proceeds by analyzing the 2D case to show LRO induction, then extends the analysis to 3D using RG flow equations to map out phase transitions between different coupling regimes.

Abstract

Can quenched disorder generate ordering in driven systems? Using a recently proposed hydrodynamic model, we show that sufficiently long-ranged quenched disorder can induce long-range order in two-dimensional (2D) nonreciprocal XY systems, even when the clean system exhibits only short-range order at finite noise. Active surfaces tangentially advected by quenched velocities, governed by the same hydrodynamic equation, become statistically flat with super- or subdiffusive relaxation. In three dimensions (3D), quenched disorder combined with nonreciprocity produces a novel transition between strong-coupling and asymptotically noninteracting regimes, supporting either long- or short-range order. In both 2D and 3D, the exponents are nonuniversal, which vary continuously with the degree of transversality of the quenched disorder. The degree of transversality of the quenched disorder can be tuned to induce transitions in the model for fixed disorder and noise variances.

Summary. This theoretical study demonstrates that a uniform magnetic field can be used to switch the charge polarization between two quantum dots, even if the dots are otherwise symmetric. This control arises because the coupling to external reservoirs and the presence of Coulomb interactions allow the uniform spin splitting to manifest as a directional charge bias. The findings establish a robust mechanism for magnetic control of charge qubits.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical mechanism for controlling charge polarization using magnetic fields in strongly correlated nanoscale systems, which is highly relevant for quantum computing architectures based on charge qubits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine if a uniform Zeeman field can induce a controllable left-right charge polarization in an asymmetric double quantum dot (DQD), contrary to initial expectations.

Main result. The authors show that in an open, interacting DQD, the uniform Zeeman field can strongly reconfigure and even reverse the single-electron charge polarization through mechanisms involving unequal gate levels or unequal onsite interactions.

Method. The study employs a symmetry-preserving Green's-function Equation-of-Motion (EOM) approach, solving for density correlators up to three-body terms to reconstruct the full probability distribution over the system's Fock states.

Model / system. The system is an open, interacting double quantum dot (DQD) coupled to electronic reservoirs. The Hamiltonian includes intra-dot and inter-dot Coulomb repulsions, and the magnetic field enters via Zeeman splitting, $M_z$.

Key observables. Left-localized probability ($P_L$), right-localized probability ($P_R$), and the resulting charge polarization $D = P_L - P_R$.

Important parameters / regimes. Zeeman field strength ($M_z$), gate detuning ($v_1 - v_2$), and onsite interaction differences ($U_1 - U_2$).

Assumptions / limitations. The EOM hierarchy is truncated, and the authors use a symmetry-preserving closure technique to ensure the calculated correlators are free from artificial route dependence.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the magnetic field dependence of charge reconfiguration ($\Delta P_{ ext{max}}$) for both unequal gate levels and unequal onsite interactions, showing the switching behavior of $P_L$ and $P_R$ versus $M_z$.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the theoretical framework using the EOM approach, then analyzes the case of level asymmetry ($v_1 eq v_2$), followed by the case of interaction asymmetry ($U_1 eq U_2$), concluding with a discussion of the underlying physics of open-system conversion.

Abstract

A magnetic field that acts identically on two quantum dots is not expected to move charge between them. Nevertheless, we show that a uniform Zeeman field can strongly reconfigure and even reverse the single-electron charge polarization of an asymmetric open double quantum dot. Using a symmetry-preserving Green's-function equation-of-motion approach, we identify regimes where the preferred dot occupation reverses while the system remains in the single-electron charge sector. Two distinct mechanisms produce this behavior. Unequal gate levels produce different occupation responses because the Zeeman-shifted resonances lie at different positions relative to the reservoir chemical potential, whereas unequal onsite interactions distinguish the dots through their many-body addition spectra. Coulomb blockade stabilizes the single-electron sector, causing the reservoir-mediated response to appear as spatial charge redistribution rather than a change in total occupation. Our results establish a mechanism for magnetic control of charge polarization at fixed electrostatic detuning.

Abstract

Abstract

Markovian open-system dynamics have widespread applications throughout quantum information science, including algorithmic state preparation. Their convergence is commonly quantified using the worst case global trace distance between the evolving and stationary states. However, this criterion can be unnecessarily stringent when only physically relevant observables are of interest. Here we introduce and study observable-specific mixing times. We prove that, for quasi-local, rapidly mixing Lindbladians, sums of geometrically local observables equilibrate in a time independent of system size, in contrast to the logarithmic dependence of global state mixing. This separation reduces the runtime of dissipative quantum algorithms, including quantum Gibbs samplers, for estimating quantities such as the Gibbs state energy and local order parameters, yielding an overall scaling that is linear in system size. Complementing this quantum result, we develop a quantum-inspired classical algorithm for estimating the same quantities. Its runtime is likewise linear in system size, but scaling exponentially in $\mathcal{O}\big(\log(1/ε)^D\big)$, where $D$ denotes the spatial dimension of the lattice. We further analyse non-interacting Lindbladians over qudits, fermions, and bosons, demonstrating that locality of observables is not always necessary for a qualitatively faster mixing. Small-scale simulations of quantum Gibbs samplers reveal no large hidden constants in our asymptotic analysis and show that the theoretical predictions closely capture the finite-size dynamics.

Abstract

Abstract

We study the quantum transport of Dirac electrons in graphene subjected to a polyadic Cantor-structured electrostatic potential. Using the superperiodic potential formalism, we obtain a closed-form expression for the transmission probability. As the Cantor stage increases, the transmission spectrum evolves from a sparse set of superlattice resonances to a near-transparent regime, with the polyadic order setting the rate of this evolution. The angular response depends on the doping configuration, showing distinct behavior in the $n$--$n$--$n$, $n$--$p$--$n$, and Dirac-point cases. In the near-transparent regime, the transmission follows double-logarithmic scaling laws with respect to four independent control parameters: the Cantor stage, the potential height, the initiator length, and the angle of incidence. By combining these individual scaling relations, we establish a unified scaling law governing quantum transport. These results show that the hierarchical self-similarity of the potential governs the transport properties of such systems.
