Shape Evolution and Dynamics of Deformable Ring

Authors: Arun Kumar, Partha Sarathi Mondal, Pritha Dolai, Shradha Mishra
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27952v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 3/5 · Frenkel-Kontorova 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper numerically investigates the collective dynamics of active particles confined within a deformable ring structure. By controlling parameters like particle density and activity, the researchers show that the internal activity drives the ring towards a more stable, circular shape. The study successfully models the emergent center-of-mass motion, demonstrating that the complex internal activity can be accurately described by an effective single-particle model.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete example of how non-equilibrium active matter can generate collective, macroscopic behavior (like directed motion or shape stabilization) from local particle interactions, which is a key area of modern statistical physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how the internal dynamics of active particles confined within a deformable ring influence the ring's overall shape evolution and macroscopic center-of-mass motion.

Main result. The ring's shape is stabilized and driven towards a circular configuration by increasing the packing fraction, rotational diffusion coefficient, or the number of boundary beads. Furthermore, the collective activity generates emergent dynamics analogous to a single effective active particle.

Method. The research employs numerical simulations using Langevin dynamics for both the passive boundary beads and the active Brownian particles, supplemented by analytical derivations for Mean-Squared Displacement (MSD) based on effective particle models.

Model / system. The system consists of a deformable closed ring formed by passive beads connected by springs, filled with Active Brownian Particles (ABPs). The dynamics are governed by coupled Langevin equations incorporating harmonic, repulsive, and active self-propulsion forces.

Key observables. Asphericity parameter ($\Delta(t)$), Mean-Squared Displacement ($ ext{MSD}$) of the center of mass, and effective diffusion coefficients ($D_{eff}, u_r$).

Important parameters / regimes. Rotational diffusion coefficient ($D_r$), packing fraction ($\phi$), number of beads ($N_{ ext{beads}}$), and self-propulsion speed ($v_0$).

Assumptions / limitations. The system is modeled using overdamped Langevin dynamics, and the collective motion is successfully mapped onto an effective Self-Propelled Particle (SPP) model for analysis.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate shape transformations under varying parameters ($N_{ ext{beads}}$, $\phi$, $D_r$), show the probability distribution $P(\Delta)$ for shape fluctuations, and compare the MSD of the full ring simulation against the effective SPP model.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the dynamics by first defining the full coupled particle model, then quantifying shape fluctuations using the asphericity parameter, and finally deriving the effective large-scale dynamics (MSD) by treating the ring as a single effective active particle.

Abstract

We numerically investigate the dynamics of a deformable closed ring filled with active particles. The ring is modeled as a flexible boundary made up of passive beads interacting with a harmonic spring force. The interior of the ring is filled with active Brownian particles (ABPs), and their activity is controlled through the rotational diffusion coefficient. We explore how, by systematically varying the activity of ABPs, packing fraction, and size of the ring, we can control the shape deformation and dynamics of the ring. At low packing fractions, low rotational diffusion coefficients, and smaller ring sizes, the ring exhibits highly irregular and strongly deformed shapes due to the uneven spatial arrangement of active particles along the boundary. Increasing the packing fraction, rotational diffusion coefficient, or ring size promotes a more even distribution of active particles within the ring, thereby suppressing shape deformations and fluctuations, driving the ring toward a more circular shape. We further analyze the mean-squared displacement (MSD) of the ring's center of mass and observe a crossover from ballistic to diffusive dynamics, which can be tuned by varying the system parameters. Our results demonstrate that, despite its internal complexity and deformability, the ring exhibits emergent behavior analogous to that of a single effective active particle. This study provides insight into the collective effects of confined active matter and the resulting macroscopic dynamics of deformable systems.

Three-dimensional optical vortices in nonlinear Bessel waveguides

Summary. This paper theoretically analyzes how light bullets carrying orbital angular momentum propagate in nonlinear waveguides structured by Bessel potentials. By solving the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, the authors show that these structures stabilize the light against collapse, revealing complex multi-stability windows in both guiding and antiwaveguiding regimes. The findings advance the understanding of nonlinear light localization in multidimensional photonic systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to quantum optics and open quantum systems as it deals with nonlinear wave localization, structured light, and stability analysis in complex media, which has analogs in quantum field theories.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the formation, stability, and propagation dynamics of three-dimensional optical vortices (light bullets) within nonlinear Bessel-structured waveguides.

Main result. The paper demonstrates that structured potentials, particularly the squared-Bessel profile, can stabilize these vortices, preventing collapse that occurs in uniform media, and reveals complex multi-stability regimes.

Method. The analysis involves solving a nonlinear eigenvalue problem derived from the Nonlinear Schrödinger Equation (NLSE) using numerical techniques like the finite-differences scheme.

Model / system. The system models light propagation in a nonlinear medium characterized by Kerr nonlinearity and a graded-index potential, which can be Gaussian or a squared-Bessel profile. The governing equation is a modified NLSE incorporating transverse and temporal dispersion/nonlinearity.

Key observables. The 3D norm (N) of the stationary light bullet, the propagation constant (beta), and the stability determined by the Vakhitov-Kolokolov (VK) criterion.

Important parameters / regimes. Vortex topological charge (l=1), waveguide strength parameter (gamma), and the sign of dispersion/nonlinearity (sigma, alpha).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on a specific ansatz for the solution and assumes the validity of the transformation used to simplify the radial derivative structure.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate numerical solutions of the governing equations for Bessel lattices, show profiles of vortex bullets in both Bessel and Gaussian waveguides, and plot N vs. beta or N vs. gamma to map out stable/unstable regions.

Paper structure. The paper develops the governing nonlinear eigenvalue problem, analyzes the stability using the VK criterion, and then presents detailed numerical results comparing the behavior of light bullets in positive (waveguiding) and negative (antiwaveguiding) potential regimes.

Abstract

We explore the formation, stability, and propagation dynamics of light bullets carrying orbital angular momentum (vorticity) in Bessel-structured nonlinear graded-index waveguides. While these waves are prone to the collapse in uniform three-dimensional media, we demonstrate that their stabilization is provided by a squared-Bessel index profile in the plane orthogonal to the propagation direction, which may play the role of the waveguiding or antiwaveguiding potential. Different solution families are obtained, revealing a variety of power-vs.-propagation-constant diagrams, characterizing the coexistence of multiple families. We analyze the propagation dynamics in a wide experimentally accessible parameter range. Our results contribute to the understanding of nonlinear light localization in multidimensional settings and suggest new possibilities for using structured light in advanced photonic applications.

Criticality and universality in network dismantling

Authors: Lorenzo Cirigliano, Claudio Castellano, Minsuk Kim, Filippo Radicchi, Hanlin Sun
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27613v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: non-equilibrium universality 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper explores the physics of network dismantling by modeling edge removal using adaptive biased percolation. The key finding is that the structural collapse of the network exhibits a universal phase transition, meaning the critical behavior is independent of the underlying network's specific topology. This suggests a general, topology-agnostic theory for understanding network robustness.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical network theory, the study of universal critical exponents and phase transitions in complex systems shares deep mathematical and conceptual links with critical phenomena studied in quantum many-body physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the physics of network dismantling, specifically how the structural connectivity of a network changes when nodes or edges are removed, focusing on the thermodynamic limit.

Main result. The network dismantling process exhibits a universal phase transition where both the giant connected component and the largest 2-core disappear abruptly, suggesting a topology-agnostic theory can explain this critical behavior.

Method. The authors employ biased and adaptive percolation processes, analyzing critical behavior using finite-size scaling (FSS) analysis on synthetic and real network models.

Model / system. The system is an unweighted, undirected graph undergoing edge removal. The removal process is governed by an adaptive biased percolation rule, such as the max-NBC model, which prioritizes removing edges based on local topological centrality.

Key observables. Giant Connected Component (GC) size, largest 2-core (2C) size, and corresponding critical exponents ($eta, ar{ u}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The control parameter is the fraction of removed edges ($p=t/E$); the analysis compares different degree distributions (e.g., Poisson vs. power-law).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis often relies on approximations, such as using a greedy quasi-optimal algorithm for the intractable optimal removal sequence, and assumes averaging over many network realizations.

Figures summary. The notes reference schematic figures illustrating the stages of biased adaptive percolation and tables summarizing calculated critical exponents for various network types and biases.

Paper structure. The paper develops from defining the general framework of biased percolation, moves to specific models like max-NBC, derives analytical tools using spectral decomposition, and concludes by presenting quantitative results on universal critical exponents.

Abstract

Identifying the smallest set of elements whose removal dismantle a complex network, known as the network dismantling problem, is a fundamental task with many practical applications. Whereas network dismantling has been extensively studied over the past decade, most work has focused on developing efficient algorithms for large but finite networks. By contrast, the physics of the network dismantling process, namely how the network structural connectivity is affected by the removal of nodes or edges, remains largely unexplored in the thermodynamic limit. Here, we shed light on this understudied aspect of network dismantling by introducing an adaptive biased percolation process able to optimally dismantle a network. Through a systematic analysis of synthetic network models, we find that the proposed percolation process displays a universal phase transition, characterized by the abrupt and simultaneous disappearance of both the giant connected component and the largest 2-core, across networks with markedly different degree distributions. Simulations on real networks further support this universality, indicating that the physics of network dismantling is insensitive to a broad range of topological properties. Together, these results suggest that a topology-agnostic theory could be developed to explain the critical behavior of network dismantling.

Lattice Green's function of the hyperkagome lattice: modular uniformization at level 30 from an orthogonal differential Galois group

Authors: Bryan Nasr, Jean-Marie Maillard
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28141v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper solves the long-standing problem of finding the exact lattice Green's function for the hyperkagome lattice. By applying advanced techniques from differential Galois theory and modular forms, the authors prove the LGF is modular at level 30. This provides a closed-form solution for a key observable in frustrated quantum magnets.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between lattice Green's functions in frustrated magnetic materials and deep mathematical structures like modular forms provides a powerful, non-trivial link between condensed matter physics and number theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal was to find a closed-form, exact expression for the lattice Green's function (LGF) of the hyperkagome lattice.

Main result. The LGF is proven to be modular at level 30, governed by the uniformizing equation of the genus-zero modular curve X(Gamma_0(30)^+), providing the closed form sought by previous researchers.

Method. The authors employed differential Galois theory, analyzing the Picard-Fuchs operator governing the LGF, and linking its structure to modular forms and uniformization theory.

Model / system. The study focuses on the hyperkagome lattice, which models the Iridium sublattice of the spin-liquid candidate Na4Ir3O8. The LGF is derived from the resolvent of the nearest-neighbor tight-binding Bloch Hamiltonian.

Key observables. The Lattice Green's Function (LGF), the Local Density of States (LDOS), and the weight-two period of the LGF.

Important parameters / regimes. The modular level is 30, related to the structure of the differential Galois group and the associated modular curve.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the LGF is holonomic (D-finite) and relies on establishing the equivalence between the physical operator and the modular uniformizing equation.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the Density of States (DOS) of the hyperkagome model, showing four flat bands and eight dispersive bands symmetric about E=1.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the LGF's governing differential operator from spectral moments, then analyzing this operator using differential Galois theory to prove its irreducibility and modular structure, culminating in the identification of the modular parametrization.

Abstract

The lattice Green's functions of the cubic lattices are classical: each is a symmetric square of a second-order operator, with a closed form in complete elliptic integrals. The hyperkagome lattice, realized by the iridium sublattice of the spin-liquid candidate Na4Ir3O8, has resisted such a treatment: Varma and Monien reduced its density of states to a threefold integral they found no way to solve exactly. From exact lattice moments we obtain an irreducible third-order linear differential (Picard-Fuchs) operator annihilating the Green's function. It is not a literal symmetric square, yet its symmetric square carries a rational solution: the differential Galois group is orthogonal, and an order-two differential intertwiner makes the operator projectively a symmetric square. Our main result is that the underlying second-order operator is the uniformizing equation of the genus-zero modular curve of Gamma_0(30)+, the level-thirty group generated by Gamma_0(30) and all its Atkin-Lehner involutions, an explicit eta quotient parametrizing the natural variable. That uniformizing equation is itself not new - it is a row of the Lian-Yau table of genus-zero groups - so what is new here is the identification of a lattice Green's function with it, together with a proof: the Schwarzian identity is established by an a priori pole-degree bound rather than checked numerically, which also proves the tabulated entry. The hyperkagome Green's function is therefore modular at level thirty, supplying the closed form Varma and Monien sought. Its weight-two period is obtained explicitly: a depth-one quasimodular form which we prove is not a modular form times an algebraic function at any weight.

Physics-Constrained Conditional Generative Learning for Quantum State and Process Tomography

Authors: Daming Li
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28035v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5

Summary. This paper introduces a physics-constrained Conditional GAN to overcome the computational scaling issues in Quantum State and Process Tomography. By embedding a differentiable Cholesky layer, the model learns a direct generative mapping conditioned on Pauli measurements, ensuring the reconstructed states are physically valid. The results demonstrate high fidelity across various complex systems, while also addressing the exponential scaling bottleneck via tensor-network integration.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a machine learning approach to tackle the fundamental resource scaling problem in quantum characterization, offering a potentially scalable, data-driven alternative to traditional, computationally intensive inversion methods.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Quantum State and Process Tomography (QST/QPT) suffer from prohibitive computational scaling as the number of qubits increases.

Main result. The proposed physics-constrained generative model achieves high-fidelity reconstruction for complex states (like GHZ) and processes (like CNOT) by learning a data-driven mapping.

Method. It employs a physics-constrained Conditional Generative Adversarial Network (CGAN) that uses a differentiable Cholesky layer to enforce physical constraints (Hermiticity, positive semi-definiteness, unit trace) directly during generation.

Model / system. The framework is applied to reconstruct quantum states ($ ho$) and quantum processes (channels) conditioned on measured Pauli expectation values. It discusses extending the method to handle the exponential growth bottleneck using tensor-network structures for $n \ge 6$ qubits.

Key observables. Fidelity ($F$), Pauli expectation values ($ ext{Tr}( ho A_k)$), and the reconstructed density matrix ($\hat{ ho}_{ heta}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The strength of the $L^1$ penalty ($\lambda$) is critical for convergence; over-complete measurement bases are needed for high-temperature thermal states.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary bottleneck remains the exponential growth of the full density matrix representation, necessitating tensor-network integration for scalability.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate fidelity convergence trajectories for GHZ states based on the L1 penalty weight ($\lambda$), reconstruction of thermal Gibbs states using varying measurement bases, and ground-state reconstruction for the Ising model across different magnetic fields ($h$).

Paper structure. The paper introduces the CGAN framework, details the Cholesky constraint for physical enforcement, demonstrates its application to QST (e.g., GHZ states, thermal states), extends it to QPT (using the Choi matrix), and concludes with scalability discussions involving tensor networks.

Abstract

Quantum state and process tomography constitute essential diagnostic tools in quantum information science, yet their standard formulations suffer from prohibitive computational scaling as the number of qubits grows. In this work, we introduce a physics-constrained conditional generative adversarial network that bypasses iterative constrained inversion by directly learning a forward generative mapping conditioned on Pauli expectation values. The generator embeds a differentiable Cholesky layer at its output, which enforces Hermiticity, positive semidefiniteness, and unit trace by construction. Our experiments reveal that the strength of the $L^1$ penalty critically governs the emergence of GHZ coherence during training: an excessively large penalty postpones the coherence onset and yields a prolonged low-fidelity plateau, whereas an intermediate value enables the fastest stable convergence. Moreover, for high-temperature thermal states, an over-complete measurement basis proves necessary to prevent sustained late-stage fluctuations. By extending the same Cholesky constraint to the Choi-matrix representation, the framework naturally accommodates quantum process tomography. For systems with $n \ge 6$ qubits, the exponential growth of the underlying $2^n \times 2^n$ density matrix remains the fundamental bottleneck; we discuss how integrating tensor-network structures can contain the per-iteration cost while preserving reconstruction fidelity. Altogether, these results suggest that physically constrained generative learning offers a scalable and amortizable pathway toward data-driven tomography for noisy intermediate-scale quantum devices.

Summary. This work presents a novel, computationally efficient workflow to generate charge stability diagrams for semiconductor quantum dots. By combining advanced device simulations (MDMM/Schrödinger-Poisson) with Full Configuration Interaction calculations, the authors parameterize an effective Hubbard model. This model allows for rapid prediction of device behavior, showing promising agreement with experimental results and paving the way for predictive quantum device modeling.

Why it may be interesting. The development of a predictive 'digital twin' model for quantum dot charge states, moving from expensive first-principles simulations to rapid, effective Hamiltonian descriptions, is highly relevant for designing and controlling quantum computing hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Characterizing charge stability diagrams for semiconductor quantum dot devices is computationally expensive using full gate-voltage sweeps via self-consistent Schrödinger-Poisson calculations.

Main result. The derived Hubbard model, parameterized using a combination of MDMM simulations and FCI calculations, successfully reproduces charge stability diagrams with qualitative agreement to experimental data.

Method. The authors combine Multi-Domain Multi-Model (MDMM) Schrödinger-Poisson simulations with an automated tuning routine to identify gate voltages, which are then used alongside Full Configuration Interaction (FCI) calculations to parameterize an effective Hubbard model.

Model / system. The system is a layered semiconductor quantum dot device (e.g., Intel Tunnel Falls Si/SiGe). The physics is modeled using the extended Hubbard Hamiltonian, which captures intra-site and inter-site Coulomb interactions.

Key observables. Charge stability diagrams, charging energies, lever arms, and interdot Coulomb interactions.

Important parameters / regimes. Gate voltages ($V_1, V_2$), Hubbard parameters ($U_1, U_2, U_{12}$), and dielectric thicknesses ($ ext{HfO}_2$ variation).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on approximating the complex device physics by parameterizing a simplified Hubbard model using a small set of self-consistent simulations and assuming the validity of a constant lever arm matrix.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the device geometry, the application of MDMM subdomains, and the resulting charge stability diagrams comparing theory to experiment.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the computational bottleneck of full simulations, then details the MDMM/autotuning approach to extract parameters, parameterizes the Hubbard model, and finally validates the model by comparing predictions against experimental charge stability diagrams and testing its applicability to different architectures.

Abstract

Self-consistent Schrödinger-Poisson calculations are a powerful tool for predicting the behavior of layered semiconductor quantum dot devices. However, characterization of charge stability diagrams through fully simulated gate-voltage sweeps is computationally expensive. Combining a Multi-Domain Multi-Model (MDMM) approach with an automated tuning routine, we identify gate voltages associated with selected charge configurations. This small set of self-consistent simulations can be augmented with Full Configuration Interaction (FCI) energy calculations to extract charging energies, lever arms, and interdot Coulomb interactions to directly parameterize a Hubbard model for rapid charge stability diagram generation. For an Intel Tunnel Falls Si/SiGe device, we demonstrate the Hubbard model's ability to reproduce charge stability diagrams at a fraction of the computational cost in comparison to voltage bias sweeps. We further compare the simulated diagrams to experimental data and demonstrate qualitative agreement. Our result represents a step towards predictive digital twin models for semiconductor quantum dot devices. Finally, we apply this workflow towards lever arm engineering in a second device, demonstrating that the method extends to multiple architectures.

Sound propagation in one-dimensional quantum droplets

Authors: Zizhou Yuan, Jiarui Xiao, Xiao-Long Chen
Type: theory · Category: quantum gases · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28028v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5

Summary. This paper theoretically investigates sound propagation in 1D quantum droplets, showing that their acoustic behavior deviates significantly from standard BECs due to self-binding and quantum fluctuations. By comparing real-time wave dynamics with excitation spectrum analysis, the authors demonstrate that sound velocity is highly sensitive to droplet size and external trapping, providing a sensitive probe for quantum fluid physics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical framework for understanding how quantum fluctuations and finite-size effects modify fundamental properties like sound propagation in ultracold atomic systems, offering testable predictions for current experimental setups.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the sound propagation and acoustic response in one-dimensional quantum droplets, contrasting this behavior with that observed in conventional Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs).

Main result. The sound velocity is strongly dependent on the droplet's finite size and quantum-pressure contribution, decreasing as the droplet profile flattens, while external confinement enhances this velocity.

Method. The study employs a combination of real-time simulations by introducing localized density perturbations and analyzing the low-energy excitation spectrum via linearization of the extended Gross-Pitaevskii equation.

Model / system. The system models 1D quantum droplets formed by a symmetric Bose-Bose mixture, governed by an effective energy density functional incorporating a repulsive Lee-Huang-Yang (LHY) correction. The dynamics are described by the time-dependent extended Gross-Pitaevskii equation.

Key observables. Sound velocity ($c$), collective excitation frequencies ($\omega_j$), chemical potential ($\mu$), and droplet density profile evolution.

Important parameters / regimes. Particle number ($N$), external harmonic confinement strength ($\kappa$), and the crossover between self-bound quantum droplets and conventional BECs.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the linear-response regime following the removal of an excitation barrier, and the comparison between dynamical measurements and spectrum analysis is a key diagnostic tool.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the evolution of droplet morphology (e.g., $\mu$, $n_{ ext{peak}}$, $\sqrt{\langle x^2 angle}$) as a function of particle number and confinement, and compare calculated excitation frequencies under different conditions.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the acoustic response by first establishing the theoretical framework (extended GPE), then using real-time dynamics to measure sound velocity, and finally comparing these results with predictions derived from the low-energy excitation spectrum and sum-rule analysis.

Abstract

Sound propagation in quantum droplets differs from that in conventional Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs) because of their self-bound nature and the role of quantum fluctuations. We investigate sound propagation in one-dimensional quantum droplets formed by a symmetric Bose-Bose mixture, focusing on finite-size and confinement effects. Using the extended Gross-Pitaevskii equation, we extract the sound velocity from the real-time propagation of localized density perturbations and compare it with the low-energy excitation spectrum. We find that, unlike in a conventional BEC, the sound velocity of a finite droplet is strongly affected by its density profile and quantum-pressure contribution. It decreases with increasing particle number as the droplet evolves from a Gaussian-like to a flat-top profile, approaching the bulk quantum-droplet value. In contrast, external harmonic confinement compresses the droplet and enhances the sound velocity, driving the system toward the acoustic behavior of a trapped BEC. Our results establish sound propagation as a sensitive probe of finite-size effects and the crossover between self-bound quantum droplets and conventional Bose gases, and suggest a feasible route for experimental observation in ultracold $^{39}$K droplets.

Casimir-electrostatic pull-in in nanoelectromechanical actuators: Differentiable design sensitivities and the damping-dependent collapse boundary

Authors: N. S. Akintsov, A. P. Nevecheria, S. N. Andreev, Qing-Hua Qin
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28494v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: methods for driven-dissipative 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work tackles the complex physics of pull-in instability in NEMS actuators driven by Casimir and electrostatic forces. The authors employ Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) trained in a specialized rapidity coordinate to overcome numerical difficulties near collapse singularities. This advanced computational approach allows for highly accurate determination of design sensitivities and mapping of the full, damping-dependent collapse boundary.

Why it may be interesting. The application of PINNs to solve complex, non-linear, time-dependent physical systems, especially those with singularities, represents a powerful extension of ML techniques into computational physics, particularly relevant for modeling open quantum systems dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the pull-in instability in NEMS actuators caused by competing electrostatic and Casimir forces, focusing on accurately determining design sensitivities and the dynamic collapse boundary.

Main result. A Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) trained in a rapidity coordinate successfully reproduces the known static pull-in fold with high accuracy and extends the analysis to the complex, damping-dependent collapse boundary where no closed-form solution exists.

Method. The core methodology involves using PINNs trained on a rapidity coordinate transformation of the governing non-linear oscillator equation to solve the dynamics near singularities, coupled with automatic differentiation for sensitivity analysis.

Model / system. The system is a single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) lumped oscillator modeling NEMS actuators, governed by forces derived from electrostatic ($\propto (d-x)^{-2}$) and Casimir ($\propto (d-x)^{-4}$) interactions.

Key observables. Pull-in voltage ($V_{PI}$), design sensitivities ($\partial V_{PI}/\partial p$), and the gap closure dynamics near the collapse threshold.

Important parameters / regimes. Damping ratio ($\zeta$), Casimir strength ($eta$), Electrostatic strength ($\alpha$), and the gap size ($d$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis uses the ideal-conductor model for the Casimir force, which is noted to be an upper bound, and the PINN approach requires careful handling of the movable pole singularity.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate displacement trajectories in different regimes (stable, monotonic, collapse) comparing PINN results to reference solutions, and show the evolution of the pull-in phase diagram across varying damping ratios.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the physical model, develops the PINN framework using a rapidity coordinate to handle singularities, validates the PINN against known analytic solutions (the fold), and then applies the differentiable framework to calculate sensitivities and map the full dynamic collapse boundary.

Abstract

Nanoelectromechanical actuators operating at sub-100-nm gaps collapse through a pull-in instability set by competing electrostatic and Casimir forces. The quasi-static fold that bounds their safe operating range has been known in closed form for three decades, together with the Casimir ceiling above which no static equilibrium survives, which fixes the smallest gap a given stiffness and area can hold open against the quantum vacuum. That fold does not give the threshold reached from rest, its dependence on damping, or the design sensitivities of either. We train a physics-informed neural network in a rapidity coordinate that maps the movable pull-in pole to infinity, which keeps the residual bounded across the collapse threshold where fixed-step Runge-Kutta integration steps into unphysical states. Differentiating the trained surrogate returns pull-in-voltage sensitivities that match the closed-form fold to a relative error of $3\times10^{-6}$ and inverts a device specification to a gap of 97.036 nm at a target actuation voltage. Applied to the from-rest boundary, which carries no closed form once the damping is finite, it supplies the same sensitivities where no analytic root exists. We prove that this boundary is bracketed by two closed-form curves, that it is nondecreasing in the damping ratio, that it merges with the fold once the damping ratio exceeds $2^{-1/4}$, and that the gap closes as $(τ_*-τ)^{2/5}$. Numerically the merger already occurs at $0.396$, and the growth of the collapse time changes there from logarithmic to inverse square root. The classical-limit bound on the thermal Lifshitz derating is at the percent level, and the physical shift at these gaps lies orders of magnitude below it.

Network steering with arbitrarily low detection efficiency of any entangled measurement

Summary. This paper develops a protocol, termed 'swap-steering,' to demonstrate quantum steering in network settings. It overcomes the major experimental hurdle of low detector efficiency by showing that the required violation can be detected even with imperfect detectors, provided the sources have sufficient visibility. This significantly advances the feasibility of loophole-free quantum nonlocality tests.

Abstract

Abstract

Electronic-structure calculations require a finite representation of both the electronic wave functions and their Coulomb interaction. Conventional compact basis sets lead to a four-index interaction. Gausslet basis sets are one of a very small number of approaches that allow a much smaller two-index interaction, similar to a real-space grid, but with far fewer points. The continuing challenge in their development is reducing further the number of functions needed while maintaining high accuracy. Here we introduce projected q-shells (PQS), an improvement to the nested Cartesian gausslets of White and Lindsey. Their standard nesting construction divides a joining shell into disjoint faces, edges, and corners, reducing its polynomial completeness from order q to order q-2. PQS approximately restores the two lost orders. In terms of completeness, we prove that for an ideal, undistorted parent, the span of the PQS basis is equivalent to an overcomplete basis of overlapping patches. Coordinate distortions, which are always used in practice, harm this equivalence only slightly. The moment properties that allow two-index interactions also become inexact, even without distortion, but we show that the improvement in order more than makes up for this loss. For similar basis sizes, PQS gives smaller one-electron and interaction errors on small molecules, such as H2+, H2, and C2, than standard nesting. The improvement is largest for more compact bases with smaller q.

Abstract

Abstract

Quantum federated learning enables collaborative model training across quantum devices without sharing raw data, and it faces the data and hardware heterogeneity inherent to noisy quantum devices. Utilizing the quantum geometric tensor is a natural remedy, yet pure-state approaches and diagonal approximations discard the correlations that encode parameter incompatibility. To address this, we extend the parameter-space geometry to the mixed states that noisy clients actually prepare. The real part of the resulting mixed-state geometric tensor is the Bures metric, which measures how fast the physical state changes under parameter variation, and the imaginary part is the mean Uhlmann curvature, which quantifies the incompatibility of estimating multiple parameters simultaneously. Accordingly, we employ the Bures metric as a local preconditioner and use the mean Uhlmann curvature to develop an achievable-precision aggregation rule that dynamically down-weights unreliable clients. Furthermore, we establish theoretical guarantees by proving a convergence theorem and a variance-dominance proposition. Empirical evaluations on a trapped-ion quantum emulator demonstrate that the proposed method maintains high accuracy across diverse device-heterogeneity conditions and outperforms standard federated averaging, whose accuracy degrades under strong noise.
