Bipartite Bound Information Exists

Authors: Jef Pauwels, Nicolas Gisin, Renato Renner
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.25838v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. The paper proves the existence of 'bound information,' which is the classical counterpart to bound entanglement. It demonstrates that certain shared classical correlations cost resources to create but yield no extractable secret key. This finding establishes a resource analogy, showing that such phenomena are not exclusive to quantum mechanics.

Why it may be interesting. This work extends the concept of resource theory beyond pure quantum entanglement into classical correlations, providing a rigorous mathematical framework for understanding non-quantum forms of 'hidden' or costly information.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To determine if a classical analogue of bound entanglement exists: a correlation that costs secret bits to create but from which no secret key can be extracted.

Main result. The authors prove the existence of such 'bound information' using an explicit source distribution, demonstrating that positive intrinsic information can exist even when the distillable key rate is zero.

Method. The analysis involves comparing three quantities—secret-key rate ($S$), intrinsic information ($I_{\downarrow}$), and formation cost ($I_{ ext{form}}$)—using techniques from information theory applied to tripartite source distributions.

Model / system. The problem is modeled using a tripartite source distribution over finite alphabets, specifically involving two bits and one trit. The analysis draws analogies between quantum bound entanglement (qutrits) and classical correlations.

Key observables. Secret-key rate $S(X; Y \parallel Z)$, Intrinsic Information $I(X; Y \downarrow Z)$, Formation Cost $I_{ ext{form}}(X; Y | Z)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis uses parameters $\alpha$ governing a family of qutrit states, and the source is defined over finite alphabets (e.g., two bits and one trit).

Assumptions / limitations. The analogy established holds at the level of resources, not necessarily for individual quantum states or their measurement outcomes.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the resource analogy ($ ext{EC} > 0, ext{ED} = 0$ vs. $I_{ ext{form}} > 0, S = 0$). Figure 2 shows a family of qutrit states $\sigma_\alpha$ illustrating different entanglement regimes.

Paper structure. The paper develops the analogy by first establishing the classical bound information using an explicit source (Table I). It then analyzes this phenomenon in relation to quantum systems, examining specific families of qutrit states ($\sigma_\alpha$) and proving that measurements on these states can yield positive intrinsic information despite zero distillable key.

Abstract

Bound entanglement is an extreme irreversibility of quantum theory: certain states cost entanglement to create, yet no singlet can be distilled from them. Twenty-five years ago, Gisin and Wolf asked whether classical cryptography admits the same phenomenon. Are there correlations, shared by two parties and an eavesdropper, that cost secret bits to create although none can be extracted? We show that such bound information exists and give an explicit example, a distribution of two bits and a trit. The proof exploits a gap between two ways of comparing eavesdroppers: one can be better informed than another in every mutual-information comparison and nevertheless unable to simulate the other's data. We further prove that the distributions which motivated the conjecture, standard-basis measurements of bound-entangled qutrit states, are not themselves examples: a secret key is extractable from them whenever their creation costs any secrecy. Other measurements of their purifications, in contrast, do yield bound information, even for the separable states among them. The analogy is thus one of resources, not of individual states and their measurement outcomes.

Field-Induced Dissociation Reveals Excitonic Long-Range Photocarrier Transport in Bulk-Insulating Bi2Se3 Nanoribbons

Summary. The paper investigates the mechanism behind long-range photocarrier transport in topological insulator nanoribbons by comparing measurements under an external electric field against free-carrier theory. By observing that the required field to suppress photocurrent far exceeds theoretical limits for free charges, and by showing diffusivity violations in time-domain measurements, the authors conclude that excitons—a charge-neutral correlated state—are responsible for the observed transport.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a clear experimental distinction between single-particle transport and collective, correlated excitations (excitons) using field-dependent optical probes, which is highly relevant for understanding strongly interacting quantum matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to determine the physical mechanism responsible for anomalously long-range photocarrier transport observed in topological insulators at cryogenic temperatures.

Main result. The evidence strongly indicates that this long-range photoresponse is not carried by free charge carriers but rather by a charge-neutral correlated state, such as an exciton condensate.

Method. The researchers combined Scanning Photocurrent Microscopy (SPCM) and ultrafast Transient Photovoltage (TPV) measurements, using the application of a transverse electric field as a discriminating probe.

Model / system. The physical system is bulk-insulating Sb-doped Bi2Se3 nanoribbons. The analysis compares experimental results against theoretical models for both free charge carrier diffusion/drift and exciton dissociation dynamics (Poole-Frenkel type).

Key observables. Photocurrent suppression threshold field ($E_{th}$), photocarrier diffusivity, and the ratio $(D/\mu)/(k_B T/q)$ derived from TPV measurements.

Important parameters / regimes. Cryogenic temperatures (e.g., 12 K), transverse electric fields ($\sim 33 ext{ mV}/\mu ext{m}$ vs $\sim 0.7 ext{ mV}/\mu ext{m}$ benchmark), and excitation fluence.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the transverse field acts as a discriminator between charged (deflected) and neutral (undeformed) carriers, and that the exciton dissociation follows a Poole-Frenkel type relationship.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate SPCM maps showing field dependence of photocurrent suppression, comparisons to free carrier simulations, and TPV analysis plots detailing photovoltage decay and diffusivity ratios.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the anomalous transport using SPCM (field dependence), then providing independent confirmation via TPV measurements (diffusivity violation), and finally synthesizing these results across temperature and fluence dependencies to rule out free carriers in favor of excitons.

Abstract

Photoexcited charge carriers in topological insulators display anomalously long-range transport at cryogenic temperatures, but the underlying mechanism remains under debate. Here we use a transverse electric field as a discriminating probe of photocarrier dynamics in bulk-insulating Sb-doped Bi2Se3 nanoribbons, combining scanning photocurrent microscopy (SPCM) with ultrafast transient photovoltage (TPV) measurements. SPCM shows that suppression of the photocurrent requires a transverse electric field nearly 50 times larger than that needed to deflect free carriers, yet comparable to the expected exciton dissociation field. TPV measurements at 12 K and low excitation fluence reveal that the photocarrier diffusivity exceeds the value implied by the Einstein relation and the measured drift mobility by more than an order of magnitude, a bound that holds even at the lower end of the fitting uncertainty. Together, these observations indicate that the long-range photoresponse is not carried by free charge carriers but by a charge-neutral correlated state.

Summary. This theoretical work analyzes the Aharonov-Casher phase experienced by electrons in twisted bilayer graphene under an external electric field. The authors find that this quantum phase exhibits sharp peaks precisely at the material's magic angles. This provides a novel, measurable signature to probe the topological properties and electronic structure of correlated 2D materials.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between topological phases (Berry curvature/AC phase) and electronic structure features like flat bands at magic angles provides a powerful diagnostic tool for characterizing correlated electron systems in 2D materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the Aharonov-Casher (AC) phase acquired by an electron in twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) when subjected to a perpendicular electric field.

Main result. The AC phase is predicted to be linear with the applied electric field and exhibits sharp peaks precisely at the magic angles, where the renormalized Fermi velocity vanishes or changes significantly.

Method. The analysis involves deriving an effective low-energy Hamiltonian using perturbation theory in the moiré potential and calculating the resulting gauge-dependent AC phase factor via a gauge transformation.

Model / system. The system is Twisted Bilayer Graphene (TBG) lying in the x-y plane, modeled by incorporating kinetic energy, a Moiré potential, and Rashba spin-orbit coupling. The low-energy physics is described by an effective Hamiltonian involving Dirac fermions.

Key observables. Aharonov-Casher phase ($\phi_{AC}$), which is measured via the probability $P( heta)$ of tunneling into a polarized tip.

Important parameters / regimes. The perpendicular electric field ($E$), the twist angle ($ heta$), and the renormalized Fermi velocity $ ilde{v}_F( heta)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes small twist angles ($ heta \ll 1$ rad) and relies on approximations derived from perturbation theory in the moiré potential.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dependence of the renormalized Fermi velocity $v_F( heta)$ on $ heta$, showing vanishing points at magic angles, and plot the AC phase $\phi_{AC}( heta)$ versus $ heta$ and $E$.

Paper structure. The paper builds from defining the physical system (TBG with perpendicular E-field) to deriving the low-energy effective Hamiltonian incorporating spin-orbit coupling. It then calculates the resulting gauge potential, leading to the prediction of the AC phase dependence on $ heta$ and $E$.

Abstract

The Aharonov-Casher (AC) effect is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which the wave function of a particle with a magnetic moment moving in a region subject to an electric field develops a phase shift due to spin-orbit interaction, even if no classical force acts on it. This phase also depends on the medium through which the particle moves. Here we focus on the AC phase of an electron moving in twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) lying in the $x$-$y$ plane, subject to a uniform electric field perpendicular to the plane of the graphene, ${\bf E}=E{\hat{\bf z}}$. The AC phase is determined by an $SU(2)$ vector potential ${\bf A}$ from which a phase factor is generated, and used to perform a gauge transformation of the Hamiltonian. We find that the AC phase is linear with $E$ and exhibits sharp peaks at the magic angles.

Summary. This paper analyzes strongly-connected percolation on directed lattices, determining that its critical behavior belongs to a unique universality class different from standard undirected percolation. Using advanced simulation techniques and graph algorithms, the authors compare various bond arrangements and establish formal equivalences between different lattice models. The work is significant for understanding connectivity thresholds in anisotropic or directional physical systems.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical percolation, the rigorous mathematical analysis of connectivity thresholds and universality classes provides foundational tools applicable to understanding phase transitions in complex quantum systems or network reliability studies.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates percolation on lattices with directed bonds, specifically focusing on the properties of strongly-connected clusters.

Main result. Strongly-connected percolation exhibits critical behavior in a universality class distinct from traditional undirected percolation, although all tested bond arrangements fall into the same universality class.

Method. The authors employed extensive numerical simulations and developed advanced graph algorithms (like Tarjan's) to calculate high-precision values for critical exponents and thresholds.

Model / system. The model is bond percolation on two-dimensional square lattices with directed bonds, including specific arrangements like the Manhattan lattice, L-lattice, random diode, and square ice models.

Key observables. Percolation thresholds ($p_c$), critical exponents, fractal dimensions, crossing probabilities, and the hull dimension ($d_h$).

Important parameters / regimes. Bond occupation probability ($p$) and lattice size ($L$).

Assumptions / limitations. The study focuses on cases where bonds point in a single direction, excluding bidirectional (resistor-diode) configurations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate strongly-connected clusters for various models; one figure maps bond percolation on the L-lattice to site percolation on a Manhattan lattice at 45 degrees.

Paper structure. The paper analyzes the definition of strong connectivity, details algorithmic approaches (Tarjan's algorithm) for cluster detection, presents results comparing critical exponents across different directed lattices, and discusses formal mappings between models.

Abstract

We study percolation on lattices with directed bonds, focusing on the behavior of strongly-connected percolation clusters -- clusters in which every site is reachable from every other along a directed path. We consider the two-dimensional square lattice and various globally isotropic arrangements of the directions of the bonds. Performing simulations using a range of algorithmic approaches, we calculate high-precision values for critical exponents, fractal dimensions, crossing probabilities, and percolation thresholds for bond percolation with each bond arrangement. We find that the critical behavior is in a distinctly different universality class from that of traditional undirected percolation, but that all bond arrangements appear to fall in the same universality class.

QCOEM: Quantum Cloud Orchestration with Evolutionary Multi-Objective Optimization

Summary. This paper introduces QCOEM, a framework that uses multi-objective evolutionary optimization to schedule quantum tasks across noisy, heterogeneous cloud hardware. By jointly optimizing mean completion time, error rate, and load balance, it significantly outperforms simple heuristics. This work provides a crucial tool for achieving stable, high-fidelity execution in practical quantum cloud computing settings.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on cloud orchestration rather than fundamental physics, the core challenge involves optimizing complex resource allocation under realistic noise constraints, which has implications for designing robust quantum control protocols or understanding system-level decoherence effects in large arrays of qubits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Dynamically orchestrating quantum workloads across heterogeneous cloud backends while managing time-varying noise profiles, qubit topologies, and queue congestion.

Main result. The QCOEM framework achieves about 30% higher mean fidelity than noise-agnostic heuristics and eliminates task rescheduling in a simulated environment.

Method. It formulates the problem as a Multi-Objective Optimization Problem (MOOP) solved using NSGA-II/III, followed by preference selection via an Augmented Achievement Scalarization Function (AASF).

Model / system. The system models a heterogeneous quantum cloud environment composed of multiple Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era Quantum Processing Units (QPUs), managed via a centralized scheduler.

Key observables. Mean Task Completion Time, Mean Error Rate (Fidelity), Load Imbalance (standard deviation of completion times), and Scheduling Overhead.

Important parameters / regimes. Calibrated hardware snapshots (error rates, coupling maps), user priority weights ($w$), and batch sizes ($M$).

Assumptions / limitations. The problem is NP-hard, necessitating heuristic approximation via MOEAs; it assumes stable hardware snapshots and FIFO queueing on QNodes.

Figures summary. Figures compare performance across different optimization objectives (Load-Focus vs. Quality-Focus) against baseline heuristics, illustrating trade-offs between time efficiency, fidelity, and task distribution.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the QCOEM framework architecture, details the MOOP formulation with three objectives, presents the use of NSGA-II/III for Pareto front approximation, and concludes with extensive performance evaluation comparing its results against classical scheduling methods.

Abstract

Quantum cloud platforms need to dynamically orchestrate workloads across heterogeneous quantum computation backends whose noise profiles, qubit topologies, and queues vary over time. Existing orchestrators use noise-agnostic heuristics that ignore backend-specific errors, causing reduced execution fidelity, load imbalance, and frequent rescheduling. To address these challenges, we propose QCOEM - a Quantum Cloud Orchestration framework that leverages Evolutionary algorithms for Multi-objective optimization of quantum task scheduling. We compare NSGA-II and NSGA-III for jointly minimizing mean completion time, execution error rate, and load imbalance. To select schedules from a non-convex Pareto front, we apply an Augmented Achievement Scalarization Function (AASF) as a preference-based decision rule that maps the Pareto set to a single dispatchable schedule aligned with user priorities. Our extensive performance evaluation in a heterogeneous quantum cloud environment shows zero task rescheduling and about 30% higher mean fidelity than noise-agnostic heuristics, while maintaining bounded scheduling overhead. The experiment results indicate that our QCOEM framework can deliver stable, high-fidelity execution and lightweight resource management for quantum cloud computing.

Fault-tolerant distributed quantum computing with a single nucleus per node

Authors: Yotam Vaknin, Shoham Jaocby, Roi Nevo, Aleksander Kubica, Alex Retzker
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24907v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 2/5 · quantum measurements 1/5 · spintronics-quantum-optics interface 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. The paper presents a novel approach to fault-tolerant distributed quantum computing that bypasses resource-intensive state distillation. By exploiting an engineered error bias in photonic links—where phase errors are common but bit-flips are rare—the required ancillary qubits per node are drastically reduced. This makes the performance limited by the high-quality local memory rather than the noisy communication channel.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it provides concrete, resource-efficient protocols for scaling quantum computation across physically separated modules, directly impacting the feasibility of large-scale quantum networks.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving fault-tolerant distributed quantum computation while minimizing the required number of ancillary qubits per node.

Main result. By engineering a communication error bias (phase errors dominant), the need for dedicated state distillation can be avoided, significantly reducing resource overhead and relaxing link fidelity requirements.

Method. The authors design syndrome-extraction circuits that exploit this phase noise bias, allowing simple measurement repetition to suppress errors using the error-correcting code itself rather than complex purification protocols.

Model / system. The system models distributed quantum nodes interconnected via optical links. Each node utilizes high-fidelity nuclear spins for data qubits and low-coherence electron spins for communication qubits.

Key observables. Logical error rate, error correction thresholds (e.g., Honeycomb code threshold up to pz ~ 11%), and the required number of ancillary qubits per node.

Important parameters / regimes. Phase error probability ($p_z$), residual noise strength ($p_{ ext{res}}$), and the ratio between these two error types, which defines the exploitable bias.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the dominant noise contribution is phase noise affecting generated Bell pairs, allowing it to be treated as a measurement error rather than propagating directly to data qubits.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate implementing Floquet QEC protocols using minimal ancillary qubits and show how repeating measurements significantly raises the threshold for phase errors in both Honeycomb and Hyperbolic codes compared to single measurements.

Paper structure. The paper develops theory by first identifying the resource bottleneck (distillation) and proposing a solution based on error bias. It then analyzes performance thresholds using specific codes (Floquet/stabilizer) and discusses stability improvements through measurement repetition, concluding with detailed circuit implementations for state preparation.

Abstract

Distributed quantum computing interconnects small, high-quality nodes through optical links, but this architecture carries a pronounced asymmetry: in-node gates and measurements are cheap and high-fidelity, whereas inter-node communication relies on a low-coherence communication qubit and faulty photonics. Previous approaches overcame the noisy link by placing several high-quality data qubits in each node and consuming them for Bell pair and GHZ state distillation. Here we show that distillation can be avoided altogether. The key observation is that we can engineer a communication error bias, where photonic Bell pairs suffer frequent phase errors but only rare bit-flip errors. We design the syndrome-extraction circuits so that this phase noise appears solely as a measurement error that does not propagate to the data qubits, and is therefore suppressed by simply repeating the measurement; letting the error-correcting code itself, rather than a dedicated distillation subroutine, to purify the link. This dramatically reduces the need for ancillary nuclei: Floquet codes require only a single data qubit per node, while general stabilizer codes require just one additional ancilla. We demonstrate high error-correction thresholds throughout this regime, and we identify lattice surgery as inherently robust for this setting, enabling logical operations at a threshold close to that of quantum memory. As a result, the performance of the quantum computer is limited by the high-quality data qubits, while the requirements on photon indistinguishability and coherence of the communication qubit are substantially relaxed.

Summary. This paper introduces TempLat, a versatile C++ engine designed for high-performance lattice field theory simulations in arbitrary dimensions. Abstract

We present TempLat, a C++ framework for lattice field theory simulations in arbitrary dimensions. Its symbolic language, built on expression templates, translates mathematical expressions almost verbatim into C++ while compiling to highly optimized kernels, in contexts ranging from classical-statistical to Monte Carlo simulations. TempLat is performance-portable: building on Kokkos, it supports large CPU clusters, as well as NVIDIA and AMD GPUs. Its abstraction of hardware into a device concept makes TempLat extensible to future architectures. We demonstrate excellent strong and weak scaling on both CPUs and GPUs. We also present ParaFaFT, a standalone parallel discrete Fourier transform library supporting arbitrary dimensions on all of the above hardware, and benchmark its scaling. Both are open source and available on GitHub, and power a new release of CosmoLattice, a widely used early-universe simulation library now available on GPUs.

Summary. The paper introduces an advanced bosonic Ring Polymer Molecular Dynamics (RPMD) method to calculate real-time correlation functions for interacting bosons. This technique bypasses the severe dynamical sign problem inherent in direct quantum simulations. It successfully enables the first direct simulation of finite-temperature, real-time dynamics for large bosonic condensed phases.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, non-perturbative tool for simulating real-time quantum dynamics in bosonic many-body systems, overcoming major limitations associated with path integral methods.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is developing a method to accurately approximate real-time correlation functions and quantum transport coefficients for bosonic condensed phases.

Main result. The authors developed a bosonic Ring Polymer Molecular Dynamics (RPMD) method that enables the direct simulation of finite-temperature, real-time dynamics for large bosonic systems using RPMD for the first time.

Method. They extend standard RPMD by incorporating bosonic exchange symmetry into the classical ring polymer Hamiltonian and utilizing the Kubo-transformed correlation function estimator.

Model / system. The work focuses on bosonic condensed phases, benchmarked on harmonic and anharmonic model systems, and applied specifically to the Lieb-Liniger gas (1D bosons with contact interactions).

Key observables. Real-time correlation functions (e.g., density-density), quantum transport coefficients, and the Dynamic Structure Factor (DSF).

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($eta$), time ($t$), momentum transfer ($k/k_F$), and the number of ring polymer beads ($P$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method is exact in the harmonic, short-time, and high-temperature limits; it relies on the assumption that correlated observables are symmetric under particle exchange for bosons.

Figures summary. Figures compare bosonic vs. distinguishable particle dynamics (e.g., position autocorrelation), showing how the bosonic symmetry manifests dynamically, and illustrate the accuracy of the method across different anharmonic potentials.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the general problem (sign problem in real-time quantum simulation) before developing the specific bosonic RPMD formalism. It then benchmarks this method on simple models (harmonic/anharmonic traps) to validate its theoretical limits and finally applies it to complex systems like the Lieb-Liniger gas.

Abstract

We present a method for approximating real-time correlation functions and quantum transport coefficients of bosonic condensed phases. The direct evaluation of quantum real-time correlation functions in the path integral formulation is impossible due to a severe dynamical sign problem. Evaluating imaginary-time correlation functions and inverting them using analytic continuation is famously ill-posed. Ring polymer molecular dynamics (RPMD) provides an alternative approach resulting in accurate approximations for real-time correlation functions of condensed phases but neglects exchange effects. In this work, we develop a bosonic RPMD method, which is exact in the harmonic, short time, and high temperature limits. A critical enabling observation is that the Kubo-transformed correlation function for bosons is real only when the correlated observables are symmetric under exchange. We benchmark the method on harmonic and anharmonic model systems and then use it to directly obtain the Lieb-Liniger gas density-density real-time correlation functions at various temperatures and momentum transfers. This work enables the direct simulation of finite-temperature, real-time dynamics of large bosonic condensed phases using RPMD for the first time.

OmniQEC: discovering practical quantum error-correcting codes by an AI scientist

Summary. The paper presents OmniQEC, a novel AI framework designed to discover practical Quantum Error-Correcting codes by simulating their performance on real quantum hardware. It employs a synergistic slow-fast loop where LLMs coordinate code generation and circuit evaluation. The results demonstrate that this physically informed approach yields superior error suppression compared to traditional methods.

Model / system. The focus is on QEC codes, specifically evaluating qLDPC construction families, designed for implementation on modern quantum processors. The process models the full cycle from abstract code design to physical circuit execution and decoding.

Key observables. Logical Error Rate (LER) measured via $-\log_{10}( ext{pLER})$, alongside code-level metrics like logical qubits ($k$), distance ($d$), and $\Phi_{code}$.

It presents comparative results showing outperformance over benchmarks, validates the necessity of the full loop, and concludes with future directions for multi-agent system integration.

Abstract

Quantum error correction (QEC) is indispensable for scalable fault-tolerant quantum computing. However, discovering QEC codes that remain effective is challenging, as logical performance depends on the interplay between code structure, hardware, syndrome extraction, and decoding, which often impose competing requirements. Here we introduce OmniQEC, an efficient AI scientist for discovering QEC codes suited to deployment on modern quantum processors. OmniQEC formulates QEC design as an iterative discovery process in which an orchestrator, implemented by advanced large language models (LLMs), coordinates code generation, code-level screening, syndrome-extraction synthesis, and decoder-based circuit evaluation. At its core, OmniQEC combines a self-evolving reasoning mechanism with a slow--fast synergistic workflow: a fast loop explores candidates using inexpensive code-level proxies, whereas a slow loop performs physically grounded circuit-level evaluation and feeds the resulting evidence back into the search. We evaluate OmniQEC across four qLDPC construction families, three LLM backends, and $14$ total-physical-qubit budgets per backend. Abstract

In this work, we show that Fermi Sets---a provably universal neural network architecture for fermionic wavefunctions---can be systematically scaled up to find interacting ground states through energy minimization in a variational Monte Carlo framework. By further performing fixed-phase diffusion Monte Carlo (DMC) on the optimized neural network wavefunction, we demonstrate that as the network size increases, the variational energy systematically decreases while the energy improvement from DMC collapses monotonically to zero, indicating convergence to the ground state. We illustrate the scaling of Fermi Sets accompanied by the DMC assessment for interacting electrons in jellium and in a quantum dot under high magnetic fields.

Abstract

It is obtained the $κ$-Poincare Casimir from the de Sitter geometry of momentum space and employed as the dynamical constraint governing charged particles within the framework of Poisson gauge theory. The resulting formalism is applied to investigate both scalar and spin-1/2 particles in a constant magnetic field. Exact energy spectra are obtained, including all orders in the deformation parameter $1/κ$. The curvature of momentum space implies a maximal invariant momentum, which in turn leads to a finite Landau spectrum characterized by the existence of a Highest Landau Level (HLL). In the fermionic case, the truncation becomes spin dependent, resulting in a polarized HLL. Possible implications of this ultraviolet truncation and its relation to anomaly-related phenomena are briefly discussed.
