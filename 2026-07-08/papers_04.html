Summary. The researchers demonstrated that an antiferromagnet ($ ext{LiFePO}_4$) can have its magnetic domains deterministically switched simply by shining mid-infrared light from opposite directions. This directional switching, which persists over broad wavelengths, is attributed to the generation of nonreciprocal heat currents, establishing a general principle for light control in symmetry-broken materials.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful example of controlling symmetry-broken quantum phases (AFMs) using thermal gradients generated by light, suggesting that nonreciprocity via energy deposition is a general principle applicable across various condensed matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To demonstrate deterministic control over antiferromagnetic (AFM) domains using nonreciprocal heat currents generated by mid-infrared light.

Main result. Nonreciprocal, broadband domain switching was achieved in $ ext{LiFePO}_4$ by pumping from opposite sides, confirming that directional heat current is the key mechanism, rather than specific optical resonances.

Method. The study used pump-probe techniques with intense mid-infrared pulses ($\lambda=7-19 \mu ext{m}$) to induce domain switching in a single crystal of $ ext{LiFePO}_4$, while using visible light for imaging the resulting domain states.

Model / system. The system is the magnetoelectric antiferromagnet $ ext{LiFePO}_4$, which inherently breaks both space-inversion and time-reversal symmetries. The control mechanism relies on generating nonreciprocal heat currents ($\mathbf{J}_c$) that act as a directional symmetry-breaking field.

Key observables. Domain states ($ ext{L+}$ and $ ext{L-}$), change in transmitted light intensity, and the directionality of the induced heat current.

Important parameters / regimes. Mid-infrared wavelength range ($7–19 \mu ext{m}$); temperature dependence around the Néel temperature ($T_N = 50 ext{ K}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The switching is primarily mediated by nonreciprocal heat currents ($\mathbf{J}_c$), which reverses sign upon reversing the light propagation direction, overcoming limitations of resonance-based control.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate domain imaging showing $ ext{L+} o ext{L-}$ switching from one side and $ ext{L-} o ext{L+}$ from the opposite side. Time-resolved measurements track transient changes linked to heating, and simulations map the direction reversal of the heat current.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the theoretical basis (symmetry breaking in AFMs) before presenting experimental evidence: first, demonstrating nonreciprocal switching using mid-IR pulses; second, attributing this effect robustly to broadband heat currents rather than resonances; and finally, ruling out simple directional dichroism mechanisms.

Abstract

What distinguishes front from back? In physics, such directionality emerges only when an underlying symmetry is broken. Antiferromagnets that inherently break both space-inversion and time-reversal symmetries provide a striking example, exhibiting nonreciprocal optical responses that depend on the direction of light propagation. Beyond distinguishing antiferromagnetic domains, we show that this nonreciprocity can deterministically create them. Using mid-infrared light, we demonstrate deterministic switching of antiferromagnetic domains in the magnetoelectric antiferromagnet LiFePO4, where illumination from opposite sides selectively stabilizes opposite domain states. Remarkably, the switching persists over a broad wavelength range rather than being confined to a narrow transition-specific spectral region, overcoming the spectral and material constraints of resonance-based optical switching schemes. The broadband switching originates from the material's intrinsic nonreciprocity through optically generated heat currents. Our results establish nonreciprocity as a general principle for deterministically controlling symmetry-broken phases with light.

Summary. This paper analyzes quantum random walks where the coin operator is randomized using the Haar measure, leading to an averaged channel that mimics classical diffusion. Despite this decoherence in the coin subspace, the authors demonstrate that specific measurements on the vertex subsystem can still detect information about the initial state for certain graph types. It suggests a model useful for studying strongly perturbed quantum systems rather than search algorithms.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a theoretical framework for understanding how quantum coherence is lost due to environmental coupling (modeled by Haar averaging), yet retains measurable signatures of initial conditions through subsystem measurements. This touches upon concepts relevant to open quantum systems and measurement theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates quantum random walks on d-regular graphs when the coin operator is randomized according to the Haar measure, analyzing information retention despite channel averaging.

Main result. Although the averaged process depolarizes the coin subspace and resembles classical diffusion, measurements in the vertex subspace can still reveal initial state information under specific graph structures (e.g., Abelian Cayley graphs).

Method. The analysis involves calculating the expectation value of the quantum channel over Haar-random unitaries and analyzing the evolution of correlation functions and variances.

Model / system. The model is a discrete-time quantum random walk on a graph, defined by an evolution operator involving a controlled shift and a coin operator. The key modification is averaging the coin operation over the Haar measure.

Key observables. Correlations between vertices ($p_{vv'}$), variance of vertex position measurements ($\sigma^2_{\hat{B}}$), and the decay/retention of initial state information.

Important parameters / regimes. The degree $d$ of the graph, the number of steps $t$, and properties of the underlying group structure (e.g., even element orders for Abelian groups).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on averaging over the Haar measure; it assumes that information retention can be diagnosed by measuring correlations or variances.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the quantum circuit for the walk using a controlled shift operator. Figure 2 compares position probability distributions for cycle graphs under Hadamard vs. Haar-random coin walks, showing classical diffusion behavior for the latter.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the model with random coins to deriving the averaged channel, comparing its resulting dynamics (classical walk) to coherent walks, and finally developing diagnostic tools (correlation measurements, variance analysis) to prove information retention in specific regimes.

Abstract

With unitary coin operator that is a random matrix drawn from a uniform distribution with respect to the Haar measure, we construct a variant of a discrete quantum random walk using a d-regular undirected connected simple graph. With each step of the walk, the coin operator random matrix is drawn independently from the distribution. The expectation value over the distribution of random unitaries for the associated quantum channel gives a depolarization channel for the coin subspace and resembles the associated classical random walk where the direction a walker steps depends upon the outcome of a fair coin or balanced d-sided dice. Remarkably, despite the fact that the averaged channel depolarizes the coin subspace, measurements in the vertex subspace can be designed that would reveal information about the initial quantum state, even after many iterations of the channel. We illustrate with examples of quantum walks on Cayley graphs of Abelian groups, such as the cycle and hypercube graphs. These quantum walks are examples of bipartite strongly interacting systems, where one subsystem is strongly perturbed, yet information about the initial state in the other subsystem is potentially measurable forever. Due to its decoherence, a quantum random walk with a Haar-random coin would not be useful in search algorithms but could aid in understanding quantum systems with strongly perturbed subsystems.

Summary. This paper introduces Quantum Spectral Anomaly Detection (QSPADE), a novel technique to compute anomaly scores for quantum states by analyzing the spectrum of the normal data's average state. It achieves this by replacing hard spectral cutoffs with a smooth, temperature-controlled thresholding mechanism. QSPADE offers an efficient tool applicable both to encoded classical data and directly to monitoring complex quantum systems undergoing phase transitions.

Why it may be interesting. The ability to monitor complex quantum systems (like those near phase transitions) using only local spectral information, without needing explicit knowledge of order parameters or full state reconstruction, is highly valuable for experimental diagnostics in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core task is developing an efficient method for quantum anomaly detection, quantifying how much a test quantum state deviates from a normal dataset.

Main result. QSPADE provides a continuous, spectral-based framework that successfully detects changes across quantum phase transitions without requiring predefined order parameters.

Method. It replaces hard PCA rank selection with a smooth, temperature-controlled spectral threshold applied to the average state's spectrum, yielding both residual and Hotelling's T^2 type anomaly scores.

Model / system. The analysis is based on quantum data represented by density operators ($ ho_i$) whose average forms the normal support operator $C$. The method is shown to apply to monitoring quantum-native systems like the transverse-field Ising model.

Key observables. Anomaly score (e.g., residual statistic $Q$ or Hotelling's $T^2$), spectral eigenvalues ($\lambda_j$), and acceptance probabilities $\langle\psi|M f_{\mu\alpha,T} |\psi angle$.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature parameter ($T$), spectral threshold ($\mu$), target retained-mass fraction ($\alpha$), and measurement shot budget ($m$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumption is that the anomaly score can be derived solely from the spectrum of the average normal state, avoiding full Gram matrix construction.

Figure 7 shows QSPADE successfully tracking curved supports (ring) where linear PCA fails. Figure 8 demonstrates that smaller temperatures ($T$) improve robustness to shot noise in quantum-native experiments.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the classical PCA background, then introduces QSPADE as a soft spectral detector using a temperature parameter $T$. It develops theoretical proofs for this smooth detection mechanism and validates it by comparing performance against various classical kernel methods on synthetic data geometries and in quantum-native simulations.

Abstract

A core task in quantum anomaly detection is to compute an anomaly score that quantifies how strongly a test quantum state deviates from a given quantum dataset assumed to be normal. Classically, principal component analysis (PCA) for centered data computes the anomaly score by evaluating the test sample relative to the subspace spanned by the selected leading eigenvectors. However, for quantum data that lack a standard centering, explicitly recovering principal eigenvectors, constructing full Gram matrices, or loading quantum-random-access-memory-style data can be more costly than estimating the anomaly score itself. To avoid these costs, we propose Quantum Spectral Anomaly Detection (QSPADE), which computes PCA-like anomaly scores directly from the spectrum of the average state of the normal dataset. By replacing hard PCA rank selection with a smooth, temperature-controlled spectral threshold, QSPADE makes near-threshold spectral components contribute partially to the anomaly score. This makes the score vary continuously rather than jump when a borderline component is included or excluded, and makes it less sensitive to noise or arbitrary hard cutoffs near the threshold. In the zero-temperature limit, QSPADE recovers the hard-projector PCA score. The proposed measurement-based quantum detector can be calibrated with a sample complexity independent of the data dimension. Numerical simulations show that QSPADE behaves like kernel-PCA on encoded classical data and detects changes across a transverse-field Ising transition without predefined order parameters. Consequently, QSPADE gives an efficient framework for both quantum-kernel anomaly detection on encoded classical data and the monitoring of quantum-native systems where diagnostic observables are unknown.

Summary. This paper theoretically investigates the quantum tunneling Mpemba effect in an open double-well potential system. Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous theoretical framework for observing non-equilibrium phenomena like the Mpemba effect in quantum systems by connecting it to fundamental mathematical properties (Sturm-Liouville theorem) within open quantum dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the quantum tunneling Mpemba effect, a counterintuitive relaxation phenomenon where hotter systems cool faster than cooler ones, in an open quantum setting.

Main result. It proves that the non-monotonic behavior of key spectral coefficients is a universal topological property governed by Sturm-Liouville theory and is invariant with respect to system size L, contrasting classical boundary effects.

Method. The analysis uses non-Hermitian spectral decomposition on an effective Hamiltonian derived from the GKSL master equation, employing biorthogonal quantum mechanics.

Model / system. The model is a single particle in a continuous one-dimensional symmetric double-well potential open to environmental sinks at boundaries (modeled by a Continuous Absorbing Potential).

Key observables. Non-monotonic behavior of the first non-trivial even-parity spectral coefficient ($a_{2}(T_{i})$), total survival probability trace $S(t, T_i)$, and trace distance $\mathcal{D}(t, T_i)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Initial temperature ($T_i$), system size ($L$), and the ratio of decay rates ($\Gamma_2 \gg \Gamma_0$ and $\Gamma_2 \gg \Gamma_1$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes permanent absorption at boundaries (no recycling) and requires a strict separation of timescales for observation.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by setting up the open quantum system dynamics via the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian, analyzing relaxation using spectral decomposition, proving the topological nature of the Mpemba signature in coefficients, and finally demonstrating its size invariance compared to classical models.

Abstract

The quantum tunneling Mpemba effect is investigated within a continuous one-dimensional symmetric double-well potential open to external environmental sinks at the boundaries ($x=\pm L$). Using a non-Hermitian spectral decomposition of the effective Hamiltonian, we characterize the open-system relaxation dynamics without relying on abstract state-space quenches. We mathematically prove that the non-monotonic behavior of the first non-trivial even-parity spectral coefficient, $a_{2}(T_{i})$, with respect to the initial preparation temperature $T_{i}$ is a universal topological property born from quantum statistical mechanics. Crucially, we demonstrate that this intermediate thermal peak is governed by the Sturm-Liouville oscillation theorem and remains completely invariant with respect to the global system size $L$, contrasting sharply with the boundary-driven classical Mpemba effect. This universal peak arises from the geometric and nodal alignment between highly localized unperturbed states and extended non-Hermitian decay channels. Furthermore, we clarify that while this mechanism is robust, the actual observation of anomalous crossings in the total survival probability trace $S(t,T_{i})$ and the trace distance $\mathcal{D}(t,T_i)$ demand a strict separation of timescales, requiring the over-barrier escape rate to vastly exceed the decay rate of the deep-well tunneling doublet ($Γ_{2}\gg Γ_{0}$ and $Γ_2\gg Γ_1$). Our continuous formulation successfully bridges real-space classical boundary-driven dissipation with open quantum dynamics, providing novel insights for engineering non-equilibrium states via tailored boundary loss.

Abstract

We use fully resolved nematohydrodynamic simulations to study deformation statistics of a passive nematic droplet in two-dimensional extensile active-nematic turbulence. We find that the droplet aspect ratio serves as a scalar probe of the active bath. While the mean deformation increases with activity, normalized intermittency is strongest at lower activity. This suggests slower and more coherent bath forcing. When compared with translational and forcing-side fluctuations, it reveals a hierarchy of intermittency: shape is more weakly intermittent than translation and active-stress fluctuations, consistent with filtering by interfacial restoring forces. Power spectra show an extended near-$1/ω$ regime for the maximal normal interface velocity, distinct from the steeper, approximately $1/ω^{2}$ spectrum of the interfacial active stress. Soft inclusions thus reveal how interfacial restoring forces convert active forcing into bursty, scale-rich deformation dynamics.

Abstract

Fixed-point (FP) tensor networks provide a discrete spacetime representation of conformal field theories (CFTs), offering a new route toward understanding holographic duality, generalized symmetries, and even quantum gravity. In this work, we construct FP tensors for the 2D compactified boson theory at a generic compactification radius, an archetypal irrational CFT, using boundary (open-string) data with conformal boundary conditions. We show that the resulting tensors reproduce the closed-string spectrum with high accuracy and generate stable renormalization-group (RG) flows under the tensor complex renormalization algorithm. Moreover, we identify a controllable exactly marginal deformation at the level of a single tensor, enabling flows that move continuously along the $c=1$ moduli space. This framework establishes a concrete lattice-level route toward describing a broad class of 2D irrational CFTs.

Summary. This paper rigorously determines the fundamental boundaries on what states can be prepared in an open qubit system under coherent control. It characterizes this boundary as a specific surface of revolution within the Bloch ball using advanced geometric control theory. Why it may be interesting. This work provides a complete, rigorous geometric description of controllability for open quantum systems, which is foundational knowledge for designing robust quantum gates and state preparation routines in realistic noisy environments.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to analytically determine the ultimate geometric limits of state preparation (the reachable set) for an open qubit subjected to coherent control.

Main result. It proves that the boundary of the reachable set is a surface of revolution around the $r_x$-axis, which is analytic except for two conical singularities. Furthermore, it provides explicit control protocols to approach any state on this boundary asymptotically.

Method. The analysis uses geometric control theory applied to the Bloch sphere dynamics governed by a GKSL master equation, involving techniques like analyzing separatrices and constructing specific time-optimal control protocols.

Model / system. The system is an open two-level quantum system (qubit) whose evolution is described by a Lindblad/GKSL master equation incorporating free Hamiltonian ($\mathrm{H}_0$), coherent driving ($V$), and dissipation rates ($\gamma$).

Key observables. Bloch coordinates $(r_x, r_y, r_z)$ describing the state $ ho$, and the boundary surface $\partial R$.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio $\alpha = \gamma/\omega$ (dissipation rate to frequency) is a key parameter governing the geometry of the reachable set.

Assumptions / limitations. Boundary points are only asymptotically reachable in finite time, and the analysis relies on local hyperbolic dynamics near equilibrium points.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the dynamics in Bloch coordinates, then characterizing the boundary using geometric control theory concepts like separatrices, culminating in the derivation of explicit protocols to approach these fundamental limits.

Abstract

We analytically determine the ultimate limits of state preparation in two-level open quantum systems driven by coherent control. For a dissipative qubit governed by a GKSL master equation, we give an exact characterization of the reachable set in the Bloch ball. Dissipation excludes a region of states in the Bloch ball which cannot be approached even under arbitrarily strong coherent driving, and we prove that this region has a nontrivial geometry whose boundary is a surface of revolution around the $x$-axis which is analytic except for two conical singularities. We derive a closed-form control protocol for moving on this boundary, and construct an explicit protocol that steers the system arbitrarily close to any prescribed boundary state. These results provide a complete geometric constructive description of reachable qubit states in the standard dissipative environment, establishing fundamental bounds on controllability and state-preparation fidelity for open two-level quantum systems.

Summary. The paper details the use of advanced magnetic traps, generated by higher-order multipoles, to confine levitated superconducting microparticles. This confinement creates anharmonic potentials necessary for inducing nonclassical quantum motion in the particle's center-of-mass. The research paves the way for fundamental experiments testing the boundary between classical and quantum mechanics using massive mechanical oscillators.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges quantum mechanics (quantum interference, non-Gaussian states) with macroscopic mechanical systems and superconducting materials, making it highly relevant to open quantum systems and quantum sensing in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central goal is generating and observing macroscopic quantum interference effects in the center-of-mass motion of levitated superconducting microparticles.

Main result. The authors demonstrate that using higher-order multipole magnetic traps allows for the creation of anharmonic potentials capable of realizing non-Gaussian motional states, opening a path to study the quantum-to-classical transition with massive objects.

Method. The analysis involves solving the dynamics of the center-of-mass motion in phase space, utilizing Wigner function representations and analyzing specific nonlinear potential forms like Duffing or double-well potentials.

Model / system. The system is a superconducting microparticle levitated and trapped by static magnetic fields generated via higher-order multipole coils. The dynamics are governed by the particle's interaction with an anharmonic potential $U(r)$ derived from the applied field $\mathbf{B}_a$.

Key observables. Non-Gaussian motional states, measurement of particle position to distinguish classical vs. quantum behavior.

Important parameters / regimes. The nonlinearity scale is set below nanometers (hundred times the zero-point motion); trap potentials are defined by magnetic field coefficients ($b_1, b_2, b_3$).

Assumptions / limitations. It is assumed that the superconducting particle remains in a state where its dynamics can be effectively modeled by these anharmonic potentials, and that rapid switching of fields is possible.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates various magnetic traps (harmonic/anharmonic) generated by coil configurations. Figure 2 shows classical trajectory solutions for different nonlinear potential types (DP, DWP).

Abstract

We show how magnetostatic higher-order multipole traps can be used to generate macroscopic quantum interference of the motion of levitated superconducting microparticles. An appropriate combination of multipolar magnetic fields offers great versatility in constructing various trap potentials, including anharmonic trap potentials such as Duffing or double-well types. Crucially, the anharmonic trap potentials realize a nonlinearity on the order of hundred times the zero-point motion, i.e., on a length scale below nanometers. These anharmonic potentials allow for the generation of quantum features of the center-of-mass motion of a magnetically levitated superconducting microparticle. Importantly, they can be easily generated with a static arrangement of coils, requiring only that the current running through them is tunable. We propose protocols exploiting the versatility of the magnetic trap landscape to generate non-Gaussian motional states. We solve the dynamics of the center-of-mass motion of the particle in phase space and analyze its parameter dependence. Furthermore, we give a recipe to distinguish classical from quantum behavior in a statistically meaningful way through measurement of the position of the particle. Our results open a path to accessing the quantum regime of the center-of-mass motion of objects with masses larger than picogram, i.e., $10^{13}$ atomic mass units. This will enable fundamental physics experiments for studying the transition between quantum and classical behavior, exploring the intersection between quantum physics and gravity as well as probing of certain types of dark matter.
