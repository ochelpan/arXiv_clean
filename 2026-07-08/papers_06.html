Stellar Braid Monodromy of Finite-Rank Non-Gaussian Photonic States

Authors: Arnaud Coatanhay, Angelique Dremeau
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04007v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5 · correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · driven-dissipative phase transition 1/5

Summary. The paper introduces stellar braid monodromy as a powerful topological invariant for characterizing non-Gaussian bosonic photonic states. It shows that the space of these regular states has a fundamental group isomorphic to the Artin braid group. This invariant captures how the state's defining zeros move along a preparation path, offering a comprehensive tool complementary to existing quantum diagnostics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a deep geometric and topological tool (braid theory) to classify quantum states beyond simple measures like entanglement or negativity. For AMO/Quantum Optics, understanding how state properties change topologically under parameter variation is crucial for designing robust quantum protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a topological invariant, called stellar braid monodromy, that characterizes regular parameterized families of non-Gaussian bosonic states.

Main result. The regular stratum of the finite-rank state space is biholomorphic to the unordered configuration space $ ext{Conf}_r(\mathbb{C})/\mathfrak{S}_r$, meaning its fundamental group is the Artin braid group $B_r$. The resulting monodromy provides a robust, path-dependent invariant complementing scalar measures of non-Gaussianity.

Method. The authors map the state space onto the configuration space of the stellar zeros by analyzing the motion of these zeros under continuous deformations (parameter loops). This movement is captured by calculating the braid monodromy homomorphism into the Artin braid group.

Model / system. The system involves single-mode bosonic Fock states, whose non-Gaussian structure is encoded by a finite stellar divisor in the Bargmann representation. The analysis is restricted to the regular stratum of these finite-rank states within the truncated Fock subspace $\mathcal{H}_r$.

Key observables. Braid monodromy (the full braid invariant), Stellar Rank, Wigner negativity, and the coefficients $(b_0, \dots, b_{r-1})$ defining the state in the finite-Fock chart.

Important parameters / regimes. The rank $r$ of the non-Gaussian state; the parameters defining the preparation loop or parameterized family.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is strictly limited to the 'regular' stratum where stellar zeros do not collide and no zero vanishes at infinity. The method does not directly address mixed states or multimode dynamics.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the mathematical framework by relating the state space to configuration spaces, deriving that the fundamental group is $B_r$. It then extends this concept to define the braid monodromy invariant for parameterized families of states, comparing it to existing diagnostics.

Abstract

Finite-rank non-Gaussian bosonic states admit a holomorphic description in the Bargmann representation: after a zero-free Gaussian factor is separated off, their non-Gaussian structure is encoded by a finite stellar divisor. This article introduces a topological refinement of stellar rank for regular parameterized families of such states. Rather than only counting the zeros of the stellar divisor, we follow their motion under deformations of the state and record the associated braid monodromy. In the finite-Fock chart, a regular degree-r stellar state is represented by a monic polynomial with r simple zeros. The regular stratum is biholomorphic to the unordered configuration space of r points in the complex plane, and its fundamental group is the Artin braid group on r strands. Thus braid monodromy is an intrinsic invariant of loops in the regular finite-rank stellar state space. We then extend the construction to admissible finite stellar divisors of the form E_tau,mu(z) P(z); the zero-free Gaussian parameters form a contractible fiber over the same configuration-space base. Experimentally motivated finite-Fock families, especially the cubic subspace spanned by the first four Fock states, provide concrete laboratories, while trinomial slices yield explicit discriminants and local half-twists. The resulting invariant is post-tomographic and applies to preparation loops and parameterized families; it complements Wigner negativity, stellar rank, approximate stellar rank, and other scalar diagnostics of non-Gaussianity.

Summary. This paper develops a unified diagrammatic framework to calculate the time evolution of moment operators for Brownian motion on orthogonal and symplectic Lie groups. By extending techniques from unitary ensembles, it derives explicit formulas for these moments up to fourth order. This work is vital for analyzing quantum chaos, thermalization, and transport in systems with particle-hole symmetry.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides powerful mathematical tools derived from random matrix theory that describe quantum dynamics in systems possessing specific symmetries (orthogonal/symplectic), which is crucial for understanding transport and chaos.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a unified diagrammatic framework to characterize Brownian ensembles for orthogonal and symplectic random matrices.

Main result. Explicit, complex formulas are derived for the time evolution of moment operators ($U_k(t)$) and their corresponding trace moments ($ ext{Tr}(V^k)$) up to fourth order.

Method. The calculation uses diagrammatic techniques based on extending methods from unitary ensembles to orthogonal and symplectic cases, involving tensor algebra and matrix exponentiation.

Model / system. The study concerns Brownian motion on the orthogonal $O(q)$ and symplectic $ ext{Sp}(q)$ groups. The dynamics are governed by moment operators derived from random matrices constrained by an antiunitary involution Z.

Key observables. Moment operators ($U_k(t)$), trace moments ($ ext{Tr}(V^k)$), spectral form factor, and frame potential.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension parameter $q$, the time variable $t$, and the order of the moment $k$ (up to 4).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on treating the problem using an antiunitary involution Z to unify the orthogonal and symplectic cases, and involves advanced tensor algebra related to the Brauer algebra.

Figures summary. Figure 1 shows diagrammatic representations and multiplication rules for basis tensors in the orthogonal case; Figure 2 does the same for the symplectic case.

Paper structure. The paper builds systematically by calculating moment operators $U_k(t)$ and trace moments recursively, first establishing the general framework (k=1, 2) and then providing explicit, highly complex formulas for higher orders (k=3, k=4).

Abstract

Matrix Brownian motion provides a powerful framework for studying crossover ensembles in quantum chaos and quantum transport, as well as thermalization and information scrambling in many-body dynamics. Here, we develop a unified diagrammatic framework to characterize Brownian ensembles for orthogonal and symplectic random matrices, which describe systems with particle-hole symmetry. We compute polynomial averages up to fourth order and construct an orthogonally invariant interpolation for the disconnected $\mathrm{SO}^-(q)$ sector of the orthogonal group. We consider applications relating to the fields of quantum information, quantum chaos, and quantum transport.

Summary. This paper proposes integrating circuit design considerations, specifically using Hadamard Test constraints, into adaptive variational quantum algorithms. By doing so, it significantly reduces the measurement overhead required to build expressive ansätze for solving problems like the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. This systematic approach enhances the practical feasibility of running complex quantum simulations on current NISQ hardware.

Model / system. The study applies these techniques to find the ground state of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE), which is discretized using a finite-difference method.

Abstract

Resource-efficient computation is of central importance in the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era, where decoherence, gate errors, and restricted qubit connectivity severely limit the reliable execution of quantum algorithms. In this work, we demonstrate that incorporating circuit design considerations is crucial for developing resource-efficient variational quantum algorithms. By focusing on the Hadamard test circuit architecture, hardware-aware qubit connectivity, and problem-specific adaptive framework, we analyze how circuit design constraints can systematically reduce the measurement overhead associated with repeated evaluations of the candidate gate pool in adaptive algorithms. Specifically, we demonstrate reductions in the required measurement resources ranging from at least 25% to as high as 50% - 55%. To assess the effectiveness of our approach, we investigate the ground state problem of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation. Overall, our work contributes to resource-friendly strategies for quantum computation and underscores that algorithmic frameworks should systematically integrate circuit design constraints with hardware-aware and problem-specific structures to enhance the practical feasibility of quantum devices in the NISQ era.

Summary. This theoretical paper develops an effective field theory for noncritical M-theory by applying bosonization techniques to its microscopic description as a free fermion system. The authors validate this derived bosonic action by calculating correlation functions and showing they match predictions from the exact fermionic model in appropriate limits, advancing the understanding of spacetime gravity connections.

Main result. The authors derive an effective field theory using bosonization that successfully reproduces established results in lower dimensions (like the Das-Jevicki theory) and matches correlation functions calculated from the exact Fermi liquid description in the semiclassical limit.

Method. They extend the coadjoint orbit approach to bosonization, systematically comparing the resulting bosonic effective action with predictions derived from the underlying integrable structure of noncritical M-theory.

Model / system. The model is Noncritical M-theory, which is treated as a double-scaled $(2+1)$-dimensional nonrelativistic Fermi liquid. This system serves as a lower-dimensional analog for understanding string theory vacua and quantum gravity.

Key observables. Fermion density correlation functions (one-, two-, and three-point functions) are used to test the consistency between the effective field theory and the exact fermionic description.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis focuses on different dimensions ($1+1$d and $2+1$d), the semiclassical limit, and the underlying parameters defining the Fermi surface/potential.

Assumptions / limitations. Key steps involve applying approximations like the stationary phase approximation to calculate resolvents and assuming suitability of gauge choices for dimensional reduction.

Paper structure. The paper systematically applies the coadjoint-orbit method to derive an effective bosonic action for bosonization of the Fermi surface. Abstract

We extend the coadjoint orbit approach to bosonization systematized by Delacretaz-Du-Mehta-Son to double-scaled non-relativistic Fermi liquids with central inverted harmonic oscillator potential. In 1+1d, this reproduces the Das-Jevicki collective field theory describing quasiparticle excitations of the $c=1$ matrix model. In 2+1d, this yields an effective field theory for noncritical M-theory, namely the 2+1d Fermi liquid proposed by Hořava and Keeler as a unified framework for characterizing noncritical string vacua. With a suitable gauge choice this theory reduces to a continuous family of 1+1d chiral bosons resembling Das-Jevicki collective fields coupled by additional interactions. Utilizing the underlying integrability of noncritical M-theory, we compute select fermion density correlation functions order-by-order in perturbation theory, and provide evidence that they agree with their Fermi liquid counterparts in a suitable semiclassical limit. This represents a step toward an effective spacetime gravity description of noncritical M-theory, which we expect to shed light on both the landscape of 2d noncritical strings and 3d quantum gravity in general.

Universal fluctuations of first discoveries in competitive exploration

Authors: Arthur Plaud, P. L. Krapivsky, S. Redner, Olivier Bénichou
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04252v1

Summary. This work develops a general fluctuation theory for competitive exploration processes by analyzing the fraction of first discoveries secured by one searcher. The key finding links the persistence of early advantages to the spectral dimension ($d_s$) of the underlying random walk, establishing a critical threshold at $d_s=2$ that dictates whether fluctuations are persistent or self-averaging.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of 'spectral dimension' controlling macroscopic fluctuation behavior is highly relevant to understanding anomalous transport in complex media, which has parallels in quantum many-body systems exhibiting fractal or disordered dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining whether early competitive advantages, measured by the 'discovery share' ($X_n$), persist or are erased over long exploration histories in a setting where multiple searchers compete for resources.

Main result. Self-averaging (convergence to $1/2$) occurs if and only if the spectral dimension $d_s$ of the underlying random walk is greater than or equal to 2, with specific power laws governing the variance decay in different regimes defined by $d_s$.

Method. The analysis involves deriving asymptotic fluctuation laws for the discovery share using tools from probability theory, specifically analyzing two-point correlations and scaling functions.

Model / system. The model is competitive exploration involving multiple searchers discovering sites on a lattice or space whose dynamics are characterized by the spectral dimension $d_s$. This framework is generalizable to various random walk types (e.g., Brownian motion, Lévy walks) and geometries.

Key observables. Discovery share ($X_n$), asymptotic variance ($ ext{Var}(X_n)$), two-point label correlations $ ext{Cov}(I_k, I_{k+\ell})$.

Important parameters / regimes. Spectral dimension ($d_s$): controls the fluctuation regime; $d_s < 2$ (persistent randomness) vs. $d_s \ge 2$ (self-averaging).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes a general symmetric jump law for the searchers, allowing the results to be generalized across different geometries and memory effects.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the phase diagram based on $d_s$, showing variance decay tests confirming derived power laws, and demonstrate covariance collapse onto universal scaling functions for various random walk types.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the diagnostic observable ($X_n$), links its fluctuation behavior to the spectral dimension ($d_s$) of the underlying diffusion process, derives asymptotic variance formulas across different $d_s$ regimes, and validates these results by testing multiple physical models (e.g., Lévy walks, unequal mobilities).

Abstract

Random exploration is usually quantified by how fast new space is found, from the range of a single walker to the territory collectively covered by many walkers. In competitive exploration, first arrival secures an exclusive resource, as when foragers compete for food items or agents capture distributed targets. It is then no longer enough to know which sites have been discovered: one must determine, for each discovered site, which searcher reached it first. We introduce the discovery share $X_n$, the fraction of the first $n$ collective discoveries secured by a tagged searcher. For two identical competitors, exchange symmetry fixes $\langle X_n\rangle=1/2$, but the central question is whether this equal split emerges in each long exploration history or only on average, \emph{i.e.} whether early competitive advantages are erased or persist. Here we show that the answer is controlled by the spectral dimension $d_s$, defined by the large-time decay of the probability that a single searcher is at its starting point after $t$ steps, $p_0(t)\sim t^{-d_s/2}$. Across ordinary diffusion, long-range superdiffusion and subdiffusion induced by crowding or memory, $d_s$ separates persistent randomness in recurrent exploration $(d_s<2)$, anomalously slow non-Gaussian concentration for $2\le d_s<3$, and Gaussian concentration, logarithmically corrected at $d_s=3$, for $d_s\ge3$. For $d_s\ge2$, we derive exact asymptotic variances, including prefactors, and the discovery scale on which competitive imbalances are erased. Two-point correlations of first-discovery labels identify the memory mechanism behind these regimes. The same phase structure persists under changes in geometry, competitor heterogeneity, number of competitors and memory, revealing a general fluctuation theory of first-arrival inequalities.

Abstract

Quantum Architecture Search (QAS) automates the design of parameterized quantum circuits for variational quantum algorithms, yet existing benchmarks organize instances by molecular identity or qubit count -- criteria agnostic to Hamiltonian structure -- and rely solely on energy accuracy, which cannot detect structural failures such as over-parameterization on near-product ground states. We introduce HamQASBench, a Hamiltonian-informed diagnostic benchmark organizing 11 molecules into five structural tiers via fingerprints derived from the Pauli operator basis, computational basis representation, and ground-state entanglement. A post-hoc critical-structure extraction procedure identifies minimal circuits consistent with each tier's requirements, complementing energy-based evaluation with per-qubit entanglement analysis and pairwise state fidelity. Benchmarking five QAS methods across four paradigms reveals failure modes invisible to conventional metrics: over-parameterization in the minimalism regime, eigenstate commitment under degeneracy, a representation bottleneck in strongly correlated systems, topology-induced routing failure, and circuit search space growth as a scalability bottleneck.

Polynomial Initial-State Jumps and Christoffel Transforms in Krylov Complexity

Summary. This theoretical paper develops an exact mathematical framework to study how changing the initial state of a quantum system affects its Krylov complexity. By employing polynomial spectral filters and Christoffel transforms, it derives finite formulas that bypass computationally intensive full Hilbert space calculations. This provides a powerful 'relative calculus' for analyzing complex dynamics in various physical models.

Why it may be interesting. It provides powerful algebraic tools to analyze spectral properties and complexity measures by decoupling the dependence on the initial state from the underlying Hamiltonian structure, which is crucial for understanding open quantum systems or time-dependent perturbations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the 'relative initial-state problem' for Krylov complexity, which quantifies how changing the initial state of a quantum system affects its spectral properties.

Main result. It derives an exact relative calculus that allows generating polynomially related initial-state dynamics without needing to recompute the full Lanczos expansion in the original Hilbert space.

Method. The core method involves using polynomial spectral filters, $Q(H)$, which transforms the spectral measure via a Christoffel transform into shifted Krylov amplitudes and projected Christoffel–Darboux kernels.

Model / system. The analysis is applied to general quantum evolution governed by a Hamiltonian $H$ (or Liouvillian $\mathcal{L}$) acting on an initial state $|K_0 angle$. It tests the formalism on solvable Jacobi chains, including Heisenberg–Weyl/Charlier and SU(2)/Krawtchouk models.

Key observables. Krylov complexity, spread complexity ($\Phi[Q]$), filtered spectral measure $ ext{d} u_Q(E)$, and projected Christoffel–Darboux kernels.

Important parameters / regimes. The polynomial filter $Q$, the initial state $|K_0 angle$, and temperature/time parameters in generalized extensions.

Assumptions / limitations. The method is exact for polynomial seed jumps, though some interpretations are restricted to fixed low-degree spectral preparation regimes. Generalizations require careful handling of inner products (e.g., thermal vs. zero-temperature).

Paper structure. The paper develops the theory by first establishing the relative initial-state problem using polynomial filters, deriving exact finite sums for amplitudes and kernels via Christoffel transforms. It then generalizes this to operator dynamics (Liouvillians) and discusses extensions to thermal equilibrium.

Abstract

State Krylov, or spread, complexity is a property of a pair $(H,\ket{K_0})$ rather than of the Hamiltonian alone. Thus, changing the initial state at fixed $H$ generally changes the Lanczos coefficients and the ordered Krylov basis. We solve this relative initial-state problem for normalized polynomial filters, $\ket{ψ_Q}=Q(H)\ket{K_0}/\sqrt{N_Q}$ with $N_Q=\langle K_0|Q(H)^\dagger Q(H)|K_0\rangle$. The filtered spectral measure is the positive polynomial modification $|Q(E)|^2\mathrm dμ(E)/N_Q$, and orthogonality turns this measure change into a finite-band transfer from reference Fourier-orthogonal-polynomial moments to shifted Krylov amplitudes. We derive exact finite sums for individual amplitudes and projected Christoffel-Darboux kernels for cumulative probabilities and spread complexity. The formulae cover confluent roots, complex seed coefficients, support loss, and terminal quotients in finite dimensions. We evaluate the construction in three canonical Jacobi families, the Heisenberg--Weyl/Charlier oscillator, the compact $SU(2)$/Krawtchouk spin, and the constant-coefficient tight-binding/Chebyshev chain, with a Hermite central-limit scaling of Charlier as a continuous-spectrum check of this Christoffel jump machinery. Finite seed families are organized by a matrix-valued parent measure whose scalar compressions recover the individual shifted problems. The fixed-inner-product construction carries over to operator Krylov complexity after the replacement \(H\mapsto\mathcal L\) and \(\ket{K_0}\mapsto O\); polynomial seeds then become nested-commutator descendants \(Q(\mathcal L)O\). The result is an exact relative calculus in which a solved cyclic problem generates a family of polynomially related initial-state dynamics without repeating Lanczos in the original Hilbert space.

Authors: Mohammad Arif Rasyidi, Syahirul Faiz
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04072v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: QC/QI experiment 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper benchmarks 'API drift' in LLMs generating quantum code using Qiskit across multiple SDK versions (v0.43 to v2.0). The authors developed a comprehensive benchmark measuring version fidelity, cross-compatibility, and repair success rates. They conclude that while LLMs can generate plausible code, ensuring the generated code adheres precisely to the required SDK version remains a significant, structured challenge.

Why it may be interesting. While not focused on fundamental physics, this work is highly relevant to quantum information science infrastructure. It quantifies the reliability gap between theoretical LLM capability and practical, version-locked implementation required for building real quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is 'API drift,' which describes the failure of Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate quantum code that reliably targets a specific, requested Software Development Kit (SDK) version.

Main result. Version alignment is shown to be a distinct and significant challenge in LLM-generated quantum code; while stronger models exhibit better performance, API drift remains only partly recoverable even with documentation guidance.

Method. The authors introduce the 'quantum-api-drift' benchmark, evaluating 17 different LLMs across 50 tasks using three successive versions of Qiskit (v0.43, v1.3, and v2.0).

Model / system. The system under study is quantum computing code generation, specifically utilizing the Qiskit SDK. The evaluation compares performance across multiple historical and modern versions of this software platform.

Key observables. Diagonal Pass@1 rates (version fidelity), cross-version compatibility matrices, failure mode taxonomies (e.g., broken imports vs. deprecation warnings), and documentation-guided repair success rates.

Important parameters / regimes. The three key SDK versions tested are v0.43, v1.3, and v2.0; the evaluation is constrained by a 1024-token output cap in the REST API setting.

Assumptions / limitations. The benchmark relies on test harnesses rewritten to be SDK-version-agnostic, meaning semantic correctness cannot always be guaranteed solely from execution success. The comparison between API and CLI deployments is noted as non-directly comparable.

Figures summary. Figures include drift heatmaps showing Pass@1 values across the $3 imes 3$ version matrix, and tables summarizing diagonal fidelity (version alignment) and repair effectiveness for various models.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the benchmark, details the methodology using Qiskit versions, presents results comparing model performance across different drift metrics (fidelity, compatibility, repair), and concludes by establishing version alignment as a necessary evaluation axis.

Abstract

Large language models can generate plausible quantum code, but it is unclear whether they can reliably target the specific software development kit (SDK) version requested by the user. We study this problem as API drift and introduce quantum-api-drift, a benchmark for measuring version fidelity, defined here as execution success on the requested SDK version, cross-version compatibility, failure modes, and documentation-guided repair in LLM-generated quantum SDK code. We instantiate the benchmark with Qiskit, a representative quantum SDK that underwent substantial interface changes across v0.43, v1.3, and v2.0. We evaluate 17 models on 50 tasks with 3 samples per prompt, yielding 450 generated samples and 1,350 executions per model. Sixteen models are tested in a matched REST API setting with a 1024-token output cap, while GPT-5.4 (Codex CLI) is reported separately as a reference configuration. Across the 16 matched REST models, diagonal Pass@1 ranges from 0.02 to 0.85. Claude Opus 4.7 is strongest on v0.43 and v2.0, while Grok 4.20 is strongest on v1.3 at 0.513. Error profiles differ systematically by model strength: weaker models fail mainly with broken imports, while stronger models more often reach deprecation-level failures. Documentation-guided repair succeeds for 0.19 to 0.59 of repair attempts overall and is consistently much more effective for migration to v2.0 than to v1.3. The benchmark artifacts are publicly available at https://github.com/arasyi/quantum-api-drift. These results show that version alignment is a distinct evaluation axis for quantum code generation and that API drift remains only partly recoverable even with migration guidance.

Breaking the One-Dimensional Expressibility-Trainability Tradeoff

Authors: Kyoungho Cho, Yu-Seong Jeon, Jinhyoung Lee, Jeongho Bang
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04598v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 1/5

Summary. This work refutes the notion that expressibility and trainability are linked by a single tradeoff curve. By introducing Entangling Power (EP) and its deviation (EPD), the authors propose a two-dial design rule for PQCs. This framework guides researchers to select circuit depths that maximize quantum state coverage without prematurely activating barren plateau conditions, thus optimizing QML utility.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous, multi-faceted diagnostic framework for designing quantum circuits, moving beyond simple metrics like circuit depth or parameter count to guide practical implementation in variational quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper challenges the assumption that expressibility (coverage) and trainability in Parameterized Quantum Circuits (PQCs) form a single, one-dimensional tradeoff.

Main result. It establishes a two-dial design rule using Entangling Power (EP) and Entangling-Power Deviation (EPD), showing that high coverage can be achieved while retaining sufficient input-dependent variability for effective training.

Method. The authors analyze the system using a moment hierarchy, defining global descriptors like EP and EPD alongside local diagnostics like gradient variance to separate these physical quantities.

Model / system. The model involves Parameterized Quantum Circuits ($\hat{U}( heta)$) acting on $n$ qubits, typically probed by product-input states ($\mu_{ ext{prod}}$), used in the context of Quantum Machine Learning (QML).

Key observables. Expressibility/Coverage, Entangling Power (EP), Entangling-Power Deviation (EPD), and local Gradient Variance.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit depth ($L$), number of qubits ($n$), and the specific choice of cost observable ($\hat{O}$) defining the gradient light cone.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on characterizing fixed-circuit quantities by averaging over product-input states, and it assumes that local gradient variance remains the ultimate criterion for trainability.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate ansatz trajectories in an EP-EPD two-dial plane, distinguishing between underexpressive, 'sweet spot' (high coverage/open variability), and near-plateau regimes. Tables compare task performance metrics across these identified regions.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the moment hierarchy relating global descriptors ($ ext{EP} ightarrow ext{EPD}$) and then develops a two-dial design protocol. This is validated by performing route scans and concluding with task-level benchmarking on a specific QML problem to confirm the utility of the 'sweet spot' region.

Abstract

Expressive parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) are often designed under a dilemma: the growth of expressibility and entangling power (EP) that improves Hilbert-space coverage is also expected to randomize an ansatz and activate barren-plateau (BP) conditions. We show that this dilemma is not a one-dimensional tradeoff. The usual picture collapses three inequivalent objects -- parameter-ensemble coverage, fixed-circuit entangling response, and local gradient moments -- into one scalar narrative. For a fixed circuit probed by Haar-product inputs, EP is a global two-copy mean of the output-entanglement distribution, whereas entangling-power deviation (EPD) is a global four-copy fluctuation descriptor. Gradient variance, however, is a local two-copy contraction selected by a parameter light cone and a cost observable. This moment hierarchy yields an analytic separation: equal EP need not imply equal trainability, as witnessed by equal-EP circuits with different EPDs and different gradient variances. These separations turn EP and EPD into a two-dial design rule for PQC ansatzes: EP measures how far the circuit has moved along the coverage dial, while EPD monitors whether input-dependent variability remains. We find that ansatz routes can reach high, Haar-like coverage before EPD and gradient variance collapse, showing that coverage and BP activation are distinct crossover events. The EP/EPD framework thus breaks the apparent one-dimensional expressibility-trainability tradeoff into a practical design rule: search for highly expressive PQCs in the window where coverage is high but BP-like homogenization has not yet erased trainable structure.
