Summary. This work derives the hydrodynamic action for anomalous fermionic fluids by calculating the low-energy effective action from the Dirac fermion's path integral. It successfully incorporates chiral anomalies using transgression forms derived from higher-dimensional bulk terms. The resulting framework provides a microscopic, quantum field theory justification for the necessary ingredients in anomalous hydrodynamics.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on condensed matter theory (anomalous hydrodynamics), the rigorous use of path integrals, effective field theories, and symmetry constraints mirrors advanced techniques used in describing quantum many-body systems far from equilibrium.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To provide a microscopic explanation for incorporating chiral anomalies into the hydrodynamic action formulation derived from fermionic path integrals.

Main result. The effective low-energy action is shown to correspond to previously proposed anomalous hydrodynamic actions, yielding specific forms for single-fluid and two-fluid systems involving transgression forms.

Method. The analysis involves integrating out Dirac fermions via the path integral method, utilizing anomaly inflow concepts (5D Chern-Simons terms), and deriving the low-energy effective action through restricted variational principles.

Model / system. The system models a perfect fluid described by an anomalous Dirac fermion coupled to background vector and axial gauge fields. The theory is analyzed in both single-fluid and two-fluid (vector/axial) regimes.

Key observables. Hydrodynamic action ($ ext{Seff}$), transgression forms, conserved currents ($J, ilde{J}$), and the resulting equations of motion derived from restricted variations.

Important parameters / regimes. Zero temperature limit, residual irrelevant current-current interactions, and coupling constants related to anomaly coefficients ($\alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation assumes an infrared fluid phase where a semiclassical low-energy effective action can be obtained, requiring the use of restricted variations (gauge/diffeomorphism) to define local 4D equations.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from analyzing the fermionic path integral and obtaining the initial effective action via anomaly inflow. It then details the derivation of the single-fluid and two-fluid actions, culminating in applying restricted variational principles to derive the final local 4D hydrodynamic equations of motion.

Abstract

The path integral of the Dirac fermion with vector and axial gauge backgrounds is analyzed near the infrared limit in the presence of residual irrelevant current-current interaction. After integrating out fermions, a semiclassical low-energy effective action is obtained, written in terms of currents. Its expression is found to correspond to the hydrodynamic action previously proposed for perfect barotropic fluids with anomalies at zero temperature. This approach also leads to two further hydrodynamic actions to be associated, respectively, with the Weyl fermion, and the Dirac fermion having independent vector and axial currents. These actions feature four- and five-dimensional bulk-boundary terms, owing to anomaly inflow, which are identified as being the so-called transgression forms. These are generalizations of Chern--Simons forms that involve two gauge fields: the dynamical field and the background field. The path-integral argument provides a ``microscopic'' explanation for several ingredients of the action formulation of hydrodynamics that are necessary to incorporate anomalies. It also clarifies the infrared reduction required to pass from the effective field theory to a local hydrodynamic description. This reduction is implemented by considering restricted variations of the action, familiar from hydrodynamics, which at the same time lead to four-dimensional equations of motion from the five-dimensional transgression terms.

Summary. Main problem. The research investigates how various quantum phenomena, such as Born rule frequencies, double-slit interference, and classical motion, emerge from the stochastic unitary evolution governed by random matrices in projective Hilbert space.

Method. The work relies heavily on numerical simulations to test theoretical predictions, analyzing projected increments, path reversibility, and the behavior of state vectors under measurement constraints.

Model / system. The core model is projective quantum mechanics ($\mathbb{CP}^{N-1}$), where state evolution is driven by random Hamiltonians drawn from GUE. The analysis extends to multi-particle systems and incorporates detector-defined equivalence classes.

Abstract

We present numerical simulations supporting the random-matrix state-reduction framework developed in the companion theoretical paper. The simulations test the main derived features of the model: isotropic diffusion generated by Gaussian Unitary Ensemble Hamiltonians in projective state space, the restriction of this diffusion to Brownian motion on the classical submanifold, Born-rule frequencies for detector-defined outcome classes, and stroboscopic Newtonian motion for macroscopic systems under repeated environmental monitoring. We also compare GUE and GOE random Hamiltonians and show that GOE fails to produce the required isotropic complex projective diffusion. Further simulations examine finite-resolution detector records in the double-slit experiment, Zeno stability of recorded equivalence classes, effective irreversibility from high-dimensional state-space dynamics and loss of path information, and tensor-product particle-device dynamics in the device limit. The results show that microscopic state reduction, stable measurement records, effective irreversibility, and macroscopic classicality can be described as different coarse-grained manifestations of the same stochastic unitary mechanism.

Summary. The paper introduces and analyzes the Topological Spectral Form Factor (TopSFF) as a probe for global quantum properties in chaotic many-body systems. Main problem. The study investigates how topological defects inserted into quantum partition functions can probe global properties, specifically mapping the Topological Spectral Form Factor (TopSFF) to an emergent non-Hermitian single-particle problem.

Main result. The TopSFF reveals a clear signature of $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry breaking in the effective dynamics: monotonic exponential scaling below a critical interaction strength ($\epsilon_{ ext{EP}}$), and oscillations above it, with linear enhancement at $\epsilon_{ ext{EP}}$.

Model / system. The analysis is applied to generic 1D many-body chaotic systems modeled by random quantum circuits (e.g., Random Phase Model). The key diagnostic tool is the TopSFF, which probes mismatched spacetime world-sheet topologies.

Key observables. Topological Spectral Form Factor ($ ext{TopSFF}$), effective discriminant ($b_\Delta$), and scaling functions for topological free energy differences ($\Delta F_{ ext{top}}$).

Abstract

In equilibrium physics, topological defect insertions in quantum and classical partition functions provide non-perturbative probes of phase transitions beyond local observables. In non-equilibrium physics, the spectral form factor provides a minimal probe of universal quantum dynamics, and admits a representation as a product of two partition functions at imaginary inverse temperature. We define the topological spectral form factor (TopSFF) by inserting topological defects acting non-trivially on the doubled partition functions, producing mismatched spacetime world-sheet topologies. For the minimal $\mathbb{Z}_2$ spatially extended defect, implemented by the global swap operator, we derive an exact mapping of the TopSFF of a generic 1D many-body chaotic system to an emergent $(3+1)$D non-Hermitian single-particle problem describing a temporal domain wall (tDW). We show analytically that the effective tDW dynamics undergoes a $\mathcal{PT}$ symmetry breaking transition at a finite interaction strength $ε_{\mathrm{EP}}$: below $ε_{\mathrm{EP}}$, the leading modes are polarized into Gaussian or non-Gaussian tDW sectors and the TopSFF varies monotonically and exponentially with system size; above $ε_{\mathrm{EP}}$, the tDW sectors hybridize and the TopSFF oscillates with system size; at the exceptional point $ε_{\mathrm{EP}}$, Jordan non-diagonality produces a linear-in-system-size enhancement. For temporally extended topological defects, we derive exact universal scaling forms for the TopSFF free energy in systems with time reversal or time translation symmetry, and verify them numerically in independent models.

Main result. Operational classicality can emerge even when substantial coherence remains in the equilibrium state, provided that this coherence becomes operationally inaccessible to measurements.

Method. The authors derive sufficient conditions for operational classicality by analyzing time-averaged expectation values and bounding fluctuations using concepts derived from equilibration theory.

Model / system. The study considers isolated quantum systems evolving unitarily under a Hamiltonian H, focusing on the transition from pure state evolution to behavior indistinguishable from a classical mixture.

Abstract

The emergence of classical behavior and the origin of thermal equilibrium are two central problems in the foundations of physics. In the standard accounts, both phenomena are typically explained through interactions with an external environment: decoherence suppresses quantum interference, while coupling to a thermal bath drives relaxation toward equilibrium. Over the last decades, however, it has become clear that equilibration and thermalization can arise even in fully isolated quantum systems, in the operational sense that the expectation values of relevant observables remain close to equilibrium values for most of the time. Here, we ask whether the same intrinsic equilibration mechanism can also account for the emergence of classical behavior. Using rigorous bounds on equilibration in closed systems, we derive sufficient conditions under which a time-evolved pure state becomes, for most times, operationally indistinguishable from a classical mixture associated with a chosen physical property. We identify two complementary routes to such operational classicality: either the chosen property almost commutes with the system Hamiltonian or the observables used to probe the system lose access to the remaining coherence after equilibration. Our results show that classical behavior need not be confined to the energy basis and may emerge even when substantial coherence remains present in the equilibrium state. This establishes a direct connection between two foundational questions: the origin of thermalization in isolated quantum systems and the quantum-to-classical transition

Summary. This paper develops a hydrodynamic model for exciton transport coupled to an electron-hole plasma in 2D semiconductors. It shows that while simple collision models predict positive diffusion, including the inertia of the plasma leads to hybridization with acoustic plasmon modes. This coupling generates a dynamical instability, manifesting as a theoretically predicted negative effective exciton diffusivity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates exciton transport dynamics, specifically focusing on the theoretical conditions under which an effective negative diffusion coefficient can arise in two-dimensional semiconductors.

Main result. Negative exciton diffusivity is predicted to occur when the system's collective plasma oscillations (plasmons) couple strongly with the slow excitonic motion, leading to a dynamical instability rather than simple nonlinear effects.

Method. The authors developed and utilized a minimal hydrodynamic framework by treating excitons, electrons, and holes as coupled fluids, analyzing the resulting dispersion relations in Fourier space.

Model / system. The physical system is an exciton-electron-hole plasma within 2D semiconductors (like TMDs). The model treats the three species using coupled fluid dynamics equations, moving from simple collision models to full self-consistent analysis incorporating plasmons.

Key observables. Effective exciton diffusivity ($D_{eff}^X$), dispersion relation ($\omega$), and the sign of the effective diffusion coefficient (positive for damping, negative for instability).

Important parameters / regimes. Plasma inertia, collective charge oscillation frequency ($\omega_p$), collision rates ($\gamma_X, \gamma_p$), and the degree of coupling between excitons and plasma modes.

Assumptions / limitations. Initial analyses assume quasi-neutrality or symmetric effective masses; the key result requires incorporating plasmons beyond simple diffusive plasma dynamics.

Abstract

We develop a minimal hydrodynamic framework to describe exciton transport in the presence of an electron hole plasma in 2D semiconductors. Treating excitons, electrons, and holes as coupled fluids, we show that exciton diffusion is strongly renormalized by momentum exchange with the plasma. In the collisional regime, mutual diffusion leads to a nontrivial redistribution of transport coefficients but preserves the positivity of the exciton diffusivity. In contrast, when plasma inertia and collective charge oscillations are accounted for, the exciton diffusive mode hybridizes with acoustic plasma modes, giving rise to a dynamical instability manifested as an effective negative diffusion coefficient. We demonstrate that this instability originates from the nonequilibrium coupling between slow excitons and fast plasma degrees of freedom, rather than from nonlinear diffusion or thermodynamic effects. Our results provide a unified physical mechanism for negative exciton diffusivity reported in recent experiments and establish collective plasma dynamics as a key control parameter of exciton transport in 2D materials.

Summary. This paper introduces Blind Symmetry Matching, an advanced technique to estimate unknown statistical properties in quantum computations by first discovering the underlying symmetry group from measurement data. By selecting and exploiting this discovered symmetry, the method significantly reduces the required number of measurements (shots) compared to structure-blind estimation techniques.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a powerful, general-purpose primitive for quantum algorithm design that moves beyond assuming prior knowledge of system symmetries, which is crucial for applying quantum computation to complex physical systems where symmetries are emergent or unknown.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a method, called Blind Symmetry Matching, that reduces the required number of measurement shots for estimating unknown statistical quantities in quantum computations when the underlying symmetry is not known beforehand.

Main result. The proposed pipeline successfully discovers an unknown symmetry group and exploits it to achieve significant shot count reductions, with the strong confinement mode showing superior scaling ($\propto L^2$) compared to structure-blind methods.

Method. The method involves a three-step process: (1) Discovery of candidate symmetries via specialized tests (weak/strong twirls), (2) Selection of the largest passing group ($G^\star$), and (3) Exploitation by measuring the target observable in the symmetry-adapted basis.

Model / system. The framework is general over finite groups, demonstrated using quantum computations on systems like an L-site ring or two interacting systems. The target quantity to be estimated is a statistical expectation value of an operator $R$.

Key observables. Matching Score ($s_T(R)$), Shot Count Reduction Factor, Weak/Strong Twirl measurements.

Important parameters / regimes. System size ($L$), group order ($|G|$), and the ratio between discovery cost and exploitation savings.

Assumptions / limitations. The target quantity must be statistical (an expectation value or distribution) rather than a single deterministic outcome. The discovery overhead must be less than the shot savings achieved by exploiting the symmetry.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the overall blind-matching pipeline (Discovery $ o$ Selection $ o$ Exploitation), detailing circuits for weak vs. strong twirls, and comparing scaling costs ($L^4$ vs $L^3$ vs $L^2$) across different modes.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem of unknown symmetry estimation, details the mathematical framework involving weak (commutation) and strong (confinement) symmetry tests, proves the selection rule is unbiased, and concludes with seeded demonstrations showing exponential resource savings.

Abstract

Measuring a quantum computation in a basis adapted to a symmetry it carries reduces the repeated measurements, commonly referred to as ``shots'', needed to read a statistical answer. Detecting the symmetry a quantum state carries has many uses: certifying a claimed symmetry, identifying a conserved-charge sector, flagging symmetry-breaking as an error signature, and selecting a compression or readout basis; shot-count reduction is developed here as one exemplary case. Existing methods assume the symmetry is known in advance; we remove that assumption. When it is unknown, the carried symmetry is discovered from the data by a symmetry test that scores candidate groups, and the largest passing group is exploited as the measurement basis. We state the pipeline precisely, prove the selection rule is unbiased, and charge discovery in full. Two conditions are treated, both detected by the same score with a different projection: a weak condition, commutation with the representation, and a strong condition, confinement to a single charge sector, the distinction drawn in the quantum-reference-frame literature. A single circuit, a controlled twirl followed by a SWAP test, discovers both: discarding the group register tests the weak condition, post-selecting it the strong one. The framework is general over finite groups, with cyclic (Fourier), dihedral, and symmetric-group (Schur-Weyl) examples; strong confinement to the symmetric, or Dicke, subspace is an exponential reduction. Seeded demonstrations show the loop wins net of discovery: weak matching on momentum readout reduces shots by a factor widening from ten to several thousand, and strong matching on a two-system target by a further factor of the subsystem size. Blind symmetry matching is a practical primitive for the common case where the matched basis cannot be written down in advance.
