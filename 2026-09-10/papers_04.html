Quantifying cell shape and density fluctuations in epithelial tissue in vivo

Summary. This work develops a theoretical framework to analyze cell shape and density fluctuations in living epithelial tissues. By coarse-graining microscopic cell interactions into continuum stochastic equations, the authors quantify how active remodeling and passive stiffness govern tissue mechanics. The results demonstrate that these parameters can be extracted from measured correlation functions, providing quantitative insights into biological processes like wound healing.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on biology, the mathematical machinery—deriving continuum stochastic equations and correlation functions from microscopic particle interactions—is highly relevant to non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, active matter, and many-body dynamics in soft matter systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study quantifies cell shape and density fluctuations in epithelial tissue in vivo to gain insights into the underlying mechanical properties, such as effective tissue stiffness.

Main result. The theoretical framework successfully distinguishes between active cell shape remodeling and passive tissue stiffness, showing that active remodeling is crucial for rapid wound healing.

Method. The authors developed a theoretical framework by coarse-graining microscopic equations of motion into continuum stochastic differential equations, allowing for the derivation of spatial-temporal correlation functions.

Model / system. The system is epithelial tissue, modeled by interacting soft ellipsoids. The dynamics are described by continuum fields for local cell density ($\hat{ ho}$) and cell shape ($\hat{\mathbf{Q}}$), derived from microscopic interactions.

Key observables. Cell density correlation function ($C_{ ho ho}$), cell shape correlation function ($C_{qq}$), and derived parameters like active remodeling strength ($A$) and effective stiffness ($L$).

Important parameters / regimes. Active shape change parameter ($A$), effective tissue stiffness ($L$), cell density ($ ho$), and mean $q$-tensor ($ar{Q}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The approach relies on linearization around mean fields and using leading-order terms in the continuum limit; it also assumes accurate measurement of cell shapes via deep learning.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate cell shape fluctuations in the Drosophila wing, show time evolution of mean density and shape parameters, and compare correlation decay rates in wild-type versus mutant tissues near wounds.

Paper structure. The paper moves from defining the physical problem and microscopic model to deriving continuum stochastic equations via coarse-graining. It then analyzes the resulting correlation functions to fit and extract key mechanical parameters ($A$ and $L$) by comparing predictions against experimental data from wounded and perturbed tissues.

Abstract

Controlling changes in cell shape are crucial for many biological processes, such as tissue development and wound healing. Tissues typically are heterogeneous with a variety of cell shapes and sizes. Of particular interest are local deviations from an average cell shape and size. These fluctuations may extend and transmit across tissues, potentially offering valuable insights into tissue characteristics such as variations in effective "stiffness" or rigidity. In this study, we present a theoretical framework that captures the dynamics of epithelial cell shapes within tissue, incorporating both their average behaviour and fluctuation patterns. We model cells as interacting soft ellipsoids of varying size and aspect ratio. Coarse-graining our model, we obtain a set of continuum stochastic differential equations from which we derive spatial-temporal correlation functions. These correlation functions fit with our experimental data from the developmental process of the \textit{Drosophila} pupal wing. From the correlation functions, critical parameters representing active cell shape changes and effective tissue "stiffness" can be determined.

Dynamic Observable Subspaces: Fast Simulation of Observable Quantum Dynamics

Authors: Hannes Leipold
Type: theory · Category: numerical methods · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09806v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5 · quantum measurements 2/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This paper introduces the Dynamic Observable Subspace (DOS) framework to achieve fast, classical simulations of quantum dynamics. By restricting the evolution to the subspace spanned by relevant observables, it allows for polynomial-time calculation of expectation values and gradients for VQAs. This significantly enhances the feasibility of simulating large, complex quantum systems by avoiding the computational bottlenecks of full Hilbert space evolution.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, mathematically rigorous tool for simulating quantum dynamics that goes beyond standard tensor network or circuit simulation methods by exploiting the algebraic structure of the observable evolution, making it highly relevant for analyzing complex many-body dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of simulating complex quantum dynamics and calculating gradients for variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) efficiently.

Main result. It introduces the Dynamic Observable Subspace (DOS) framework, which allows for polynomial-bounded classical simulation of quantum dynamics by projecting the evolution onto a relevant, smaller subspace.

Method. The core method involves constructing the DOS, which is the smallest subspace invariant under commutation with system generators, and then using projection operators and adjoint methods to simulate time evolution and compute gradients within this restricted space.

Model / system. The framework is applied to general quantum systems whose dynamics are governed by unitary evolution, exemplified by Pauli string generators and the TFXY model. The dynamics are analyzed in the Heisenberg picture via expectation values.

Key observables. The primary observables are the time-evolved observable $ ext{iO}(t)$ and the projected density operator $ ext{i}\varrho(t)$, whose expectation values are calculated within the DOS.

Important parameters / regimes. The complexity is bounded by the polynomial size of the Dynamic Lie Algebra (DLA) associated with the generators.

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes that the relevant dynamics can be captured within a polynomially sized DOS, allowing for classical tractability.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the concept of the Observable Subspace and how nested commutation defines the DOS, while other figures compare energy landscapes for TFXY circuits using different parameter sharing schemes.

Paper structure. The paper builds conceptually from defining the DOS via Lie algebra decomposition, establishing theorems proving the invariance of the observable and density operator within this subspace, and finally detailing algorithms for simulating time evolution and computing gradients using the adjoint method.

Abstract

By decomposing quantum dynamics across the Lie orbits of a list of observables, we find polynomial bounded classical simulations for the dynamics of the quantum system given a polynomial sized dynamic Lie algebra (DLA) for the generators of the quantum system. To do so, we describe how to construct the Dynamic Observable Subspace (DOS) that captures all the relevant dynamics for calculating expectation values for a specific observable for Pauli strings, diffusor mixers, and general local generators. Efficient sparse matrix representation, decoupling nonlinearity from such representation and basis construction with permissible pruning allows us to simulate the closed dynamics of such systems with hundreds of qubits. Moreover, we find that while restricted DOS circuits may not express the space of an associated Hamiltonian, they can dramatically outperform a fully expressive circuit due to the absence of the Barren Plateau. While classically simulatable, such circuits can still exhibit a form of quantum advantage through inference, act as warm starting for more expressive circuits, and find high quality trial states for Quantum Amplitude Amplification or Quantum Phase Estimation. In the simulation of quantum dynamics, we find that our restricted circuit can be simulated in polynomial time while producing high quality guiding state for downstream tasks like Quantum Phase Estimation with significantly lower energy than circuits with full expressivity.

Summary. This paper provides a pedagogical guide to using BRST quantization to restore broken symmetries in quantum many-body systems. By treating symmetry breaking as a gauge redundancy, the authors develop both Hamiltonian and path integral methods. This framework offers a systematic alternative to traditional projection techniques for ensuring physical consistency in complex quantum calculations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the theoretical challenge of restoring symmetries (like translational invariance) in many-body quantum systems when using approximate reference state methods that inherently break these symmetries.

Main result. It demonstrates that BRST quantization provides a systematic, gauge-theoretic framework to handle symmetry restoration, offering a potential alternative to conventional, complex projection methods.

Method. The authors employ both Hamiltonian and path integral formulations, treating the broken symmetry as a gauge symmetry and utilizing the BRST formalism involving ghost fields.

Model / system. The primary demonstration system is a simple one-dimensional two-body interacting system, but the formalism is designed to be generalizable to higher dimensions and more complex many-body contexts.

Key observables. Gauge invariant observables, the intrinsic spectrum of the system, and the cancellation of symmetry-breaking errors in calculated expectation values.

Important parameters / regimes. The analysis involves the collective coordinate $R$, the gauge-fixing parameter $\mu$, and the structure of the BRST charge $Q$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the BRST approach correctly captures the physics, and it compares results derived from different gauge-fixing prescriptions (e.g., Lagrange multipliers vs. width parameters).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the existence of gauge orbits in the extended phase space and compare variational energy calculations using different ansatze (VAP, Hint, recentered gauge-slice) for the $N=2$ system.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from a pedagogical introduction using a simple two-body model to developing the Hamiltonian and path integral formalisms. It then compares different gauge-fixing techniques, culminating in variational energy comparisons.

Abstract

We present a pedagogical showcase of BRST quantization for restoring symmetries in nuclear many-body systems as a reformulation and potential alternative to conventional projection methods. The formalism is illustrated using translational invariance for a simple system of two interacting masses in one dimension, but with an eye toward generalizing to more particles, higher dimensions, and other symmetries. We explain the considerations underlying particular choices within the BRST construction, and develop both Hamiltonian and path integral formulations to provide guidance for the variety of many-body and effective field theory contexts where gauge fixing for symmetry restoration might be useful. For the demonstration system we show how to diagonalize within the extended BRST phase space, how variation after projection is recovered for product reference states, how the corresponding gauge-fixed functional integral is constructed, and how collective zero modes are isolated and controlled. Throughout, we keep in mind extensions of BRST symmetry to various approximation schemes as a guide for consistent symmetry restoration.

Summary. This paper details a novel protocol for fusing multiple, independently generated photonic quantum states into a single, highly entangled resource using active quantum storage loops. By mathematically modeling the state accumulation via polynomial factorization, the authors overcome the challenge of asynchronous generation times. This technique is demonstrated for creating NOON states, crucial for enhancing sensitivity in quantum metrology applications like photonic clock interferometry.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a detailed theoretical blueprint for building large-scale quantum resources (like high-photon-number entangled states) from weak, probabilistic sources, which is central to quantum networking and quantum simulation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core challenge is fusing photonic quantum states generated probabilistically in asynchronous trials into a single, highly entangled state required for multiphoton protocols.

Main result. The authors propose a fusion protocol using active storage loops that successfully constructs programmable multi-photon entangled states, such as NOON states, by removing generation-time labels.

Method. The method involves sequentially loading independently heralded photonic states into storage loops, modeled by polynomial factorization, and optimizing the loading coupling strength for efficiency.

Model / system. The system is based on photonic state fusion utilizing active quantum memory loops (U and L modes) and heralded sources (SPDC). The state evolution is mathematically described by successive loading operations leading to a product of linear factors.

Key observables. The detected rate ($R_{ ext{out},N}$) of the final entangled state, the survival probability ($S_{N,n_b}$), and the resulting NOON-type state coefficients.

Important parameters / regimes. Loading step index ($j$), storage loop coupling strength, source waiting time, and round-trip loss ($\lambda$).

Assumptions / limitations. The two alternatives ($X$ and $Y$) undergo the same loading transformation, and the analysis relies on specific optimal scheduling conditions for loading probability.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the bosonic fusion scheme, showing the process flow through tunable couplers, and depict mode-occupation amplitudes demonstrating the formation of the NOON state.

Paper structure. The paper develops the fusion protocol by first addressing the temporal mismatch, deriving the mathematical framework using polynomial factorization, and finally applying this to calculate the achievable rate for photonic clock interferometry.

Abstract

Probabilistic photonic sources generate elementary states in different trials, whereas multiphoton protocols require them to interfere in common temporal modes. We propose a fusion protocol that overcomes this mismatch by successively loading independently heralded photonic states into active storage loops. Conditioning on vacuum in monitored dump modes selects events in which each newly generated state is transferred into the same circulating modes as the photons already stored, thereby removing its generation time label. Provided the two alternatives of each elementary state undergo the same loading transformation, the accumulated state is described by a product of programmable linear factors, allowing a target superposition to be constructed by polynomial factorization. Adjusting the storage loop coupling as the state grows substantially improves the loading efficiency, changing the faster-than-exponential penalty of fixed balanced couplers to exponential scaling. We apply the protocol to two-photon path--frequency states for photonic clock interferometry and estimate the detected rate including source waiting time and round-trip loss.

Summary. This paper introduces pyeCE, a Python library implementing the embedded cluster expansion (eCE) to overcome the computational limitations of modeling high-entropy alloys. By using machine learning to reduce the complexity of chemical species, it accurately predicts thermodynamic properties like short-range order and hydrogen solubility. This provides a powerful, modular tool for studying complex, disordered materials.

Main result. The pyeCE library successfully models short-range order and hydrogen solubility in complex multi-component alloys by employing an embedded cluster expansion formalism.

Model / system. The model is applied to multi-component alloys (e.g., 9-component refractory alloys, Mo-Nb-W systems) to calculate thermodynamic properties. The eCE uses neural networks trained on DFT data to predict site properties based on symmetry-adapted descriptors.

Key observables. Short-range order, hydrogen uptake ($\langle H/M angle$), local coordination preferences, and total energy.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature (finite-temperature simulations), chemical potentials ($\mu_H$), and the dimensionality reduction factor ($k$).

Abstract

The cluster expansion is a widely used approach for predicting the finite-temperature thermodynamics of alloys from zero-kelvin first-principles calculations, but its conventional formulation becomes intractable for materials with more than three or four chemical species. High-entropy alloys have therefore remained largely out of reach. We present pyeCE, an open-source Python library that implements the embedded cluster expansion (eCE), in which machine learning maps many chemical species onto a smaller set of effective species and thereby limits the growth in the number of cluster functions. pyeCE provides the complete modeling workflow, including the construction of symmetry-adapted descriptors with a learnable per-sublattice chemical embedding, a neural-network energy model, ladder-based training, uncertainty quantification, and finite-temperature simulations through Monte Carlo sampling. Built on the PyTorch and pymatgen libraries, it supports systems with multiple species on multiple sublattices and runs on graphics processing units. We demonstrate the package on two material systems. In the first, a single model spanning the full composition space of a 9-component refractory alloy resolves short-range order and order--disorder behavior. This model enables rapid screening for compositions with strong Cr clustering, a feature linked to the formation of a continuous, corrosion-resistant oxide scale. In the second, a model of hydrogen dissolution in a Mo--Nb--W alloy reproduces the composition dependence of hydrogen uptake and resolves the interstitial environments that hydrogen occupies. The modular design of pyeCE allows it to be extended to problems beyond alloy thermodynamics, including kinetics, defect energetics, and the coupling of chemical order to magnetic and vibrational degrees of freedom.

The Role of Concentration in Determining NaCl Nucleation Mechanism: A Story of Pathways Coexistence

Authors: Porhouy Minh, Sapna Sarupria

Summary. This work investigates the nucleation mechanism of NaCl in solution by analyzing reactive pathways across varying supersaturations. Using advanced path sampling techniques, the authors demonstrate that the mechanism is not strictly one-step or two-step, but rather an ensemble of competing pathways whose relative probabilities change continuously with concentration. This provides a unified view that resolves previous mechanistic disagreements in chemical physics.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical nucleation, the methodology—using advanced path sampling and analyzing the continuous evolution of competing reactive pathways—provides a powerful framework for understanding phase transitions in complex, non-equilibrium systems relevant to AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to resolve the long-standing debate regarding the microscopic mechanism of crystal nucleation in aqueous sodium chloride (NaCl), specifically whether the process is a simple one-step aggregation or a more complex, two-step process involving amorphous intermediates.

Main result. The nucleation mechanism is not fixed but exists as an ensemble of competing reactive pathways whose relative probabilities vary continuously with the supersaturation level, reconciling previous contradictory reports.

Method. The authors employed unbiased path sampling techniques ($\infty$RETIS) combined with advanced analysis tools like reaction coordinate analysis, joint/conditional probability distributions, and free energy landscape mapping to characterize the reactive trajectories.

Model / system. The system studied is the nucleation of crystalline sodium chloride (NaCl) from an aqueous solution, investigated across three distinct supersaturation regimes (12, 13, and 14 mol/kg).

Key observables. Nucleation rate ($J$), crystalline nucleus size ($ntf$), crystalline ordering ($ heta_{ntf}$), amorphous aggregate size ($nAO$), and the committor probability ($p_B$).

Important parameters / regimes. Supersaturation ($S$) levels (3.2 to 3.8), which control the relative probability of different reactive pathways.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the system can be described by an ensemble of competing reactive pathways rather than requiring distinct, isolated metastable free energy basins.

Figures summary. Figures compare calculated nucleation rates against prior studies, and joint/conditional probability distributions show how the coupling between amorphous and crystalline growth changes with concentration, moving from coupled (one-step) to decoupled (two-step-like) behavior.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first establishing the general framework using path sampling, then using order parameters and probability distributions to diagnose the coupling between amorphous and crystalline growth, and finally synthesizing these findings to propose a continuous ensemble view of the nucleation process.

Abstract

Deciphering the microscopic details of crystal nucleation remains a key open problem in chemical physics. For aqueous sodium chloride (NaCl), the extent to which nucleation involves amorphous ion aggregation, and the supersaturation regime in which two-step-like behavior emerges, remains actively debated. In this work, we examine NaCl nucleation at 12, 13, and 14 mol/kg ($S = 3.2, 3.5, 3.8$) using unbiased path sampling with $\infty$RETIS. Through a combination of reaction coordinate analysis, joint and conditional probability distributions, diffusion tensor analysis, and conditional free energy landscapes, we identify nucleation pathways without a priori mechanistic assumptions. While all concentrations are governed by the same reaction coordinate, indicating that nucleus growth and structural ordering are central to crossing the barrier, the nature of reactive trajectories changes with supersaturation. At lower concentrations, nucleation mainly occurs via tightly coupled increases in cluster size and ordering, compatible with a one-step-like mechanism. As concentration increases, amorphous aggregation becomes increasingly decoupled from crystallization, and pathways involving substantial amorphous growth prior to crystallization become progressively more probable. Importantly, these pathways coexist within a single broad reaction channel rather than proceeding through distinct metastable intermediate states. Together, these results reconcile earlier, contradictory reports on NaCl nucleation mechanisms and support a view of nucleation as an ensemble of competing reactive pathways whose relative probabilities vary continuously with supersaturation. More broadly, our findings illustrate that substantial mechanistic diversity can emerge within a classical nucleation framework, without requiring distinct non-classical descriptions.

Summary. This paper introduces TorFormer, a novel neural quantum state designed to incorporate exact symmetries, specifically total momentum, into the wavefunction ansatz. By enforcing translation equivariance, it achieves superior training speed and significantly lower variational energies for the 2D electron gas compared to prior methods. This work demonstrates that symmetry-aware neural networks can provide an accurate and efficient route to solving large-scale, strongly correlated quantum many-body problems.

Main result. The proposed TorFormer architecture significantly improves both training speed and final variational energy compared to previous state-of-the-art NQS methods.

Method. The authors introduce TorFormer, a neural network wavefunction designed to be an exact eigenstate of the total momentum, enforcing translation equivariance by construction.

Abstract

We demonstrate that designing a neural quantum state to be an exact eigenstate of the Hamiltonian's symmetries significantly improves both training speed and final variational energy. For the 2D electron gas, we design TorFormer, a neural network wavefunction which is an exact eigenstate of the total momentum. TorFormer describes both the Fermi liquid and Wigner crystal with no supervision and significantly outperforms Psiformer-based references up to large system sizes. For $r_s = 30.0$ and $40.0$ at $N=91$, we compare TorFormer trained for $8\mathrm{K}$ steps against the previous best NQS, which required $100\mathrm{K}$ training steps. Our improvement to the total energy at $r_s = 40.0$, excluding the trivial Madelung part, is $0.12\%$---enormous compared to the tiny differences separating phases. Relative to Slater-Jastrow-backflow diffusion Monte Carlo, TorFormer's energy decrease is roughly $9.8$ times that of the previous best NQS. Our work demonstrates that neural quantum states can both accurately and efficiently solve large-scale problems.

Thermal Quantum Sensing: Fisher Information and Work Beyond Gaussian Signals

Authors: Yash Chitgopekar, Nikolaos Koukoulekidis, Iman Marvian
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.09583v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · analog quantum simulation 1/5

Summary. This paper develops a general theoretical framework connecting quantum sensing sensitivity (measured by various QFI metrics) to the work done by a signal in thermal quantum states. It derives that at high temperatures, these metrics converge to a universal quantity related to the signal's fluctuation variance. This provides crucial, quantitative bounds for improving quantum measurements in the presence of thermal noise.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides deep theoretical connections between quantum metrology (QFI) and quantum thermodynamics (work), offering quantitative bounds on sensing precision under realistic thermal conditions, which is crucial for experimental quantum sensing platforms like trapped ions.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses how thermal fluctuations modify quantum-enhanced sensing and establishes general relationships between the work done by a signal and the information acquired by a sensor in quantum thermodynamics.

Main result. In the high-temperature limit, various Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) metrics coincide to a universal quantity related to the variance of the signal generator. For quadratic Gaussian signals, the infinite-temperature QFI is at least twice the zero-temperature SLD QFI.

Method. The authors establish general theoretical relations between different QFI metrics (SLD, BKM, RLD, $I_A$) and the work done, analyzing these relationships across different temperature regimes.

Model / system. The analysis uses the Gibbs state $ ho_eta = e^{-eta H}/Z_eta$ for the probe, which is probed by a unitary signal $U(s) = e^{-iAs}$. Specific models include harmonic chains of bosonic modes relevant to trapped-ion platforms and continuous-variable systems.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information metrics (SLD, BKM, RLD, $I_A$), induced work $W(s)$, and the variance of the signal generator $ ext{Var}{ hoeta}(\dot{A})$.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature ($T$ or $eta$), signal generator $A$, and the intrinsic Hamiltonian $H$. The high-temperature limit ($eta o 0^+$) is a key focus.

Assumptions / limitations. The probe is assumed to be prepared in the Gibbs state $ ho_eta$. The analysis relies on relating QFI metrics to the second derivative of the work done.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the sensing protocol and plot various QFI metrics (SLD, BKM, RLD, $I_A$) as functions of temperature, demonstrating their hierarchy and high-temperature convergence.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the general framework relating QFI metrics to work in thermal quantum sensing. It then analyzes the high-temperature limit, deriving the universal quantity $I_A( ho_eta)$, and applies these findings to specific bosonic and harmonic chain models.

Abstract

Understanding how thermal fluctuations modify or suppress quantum-enhanced sensing is a central problem in quantum metrology. Closely related questions arise in quantum thermodynamics, particularly regarding the relation between the work induced by a signal and the information acquired by a sensor. Here, we establish general relations among the sensitivity of thermal quantum states, as quantified by different quantum Fisher information metrics, their temperature dependence, and the work induced by a unitary signal. We further show that, at high temperature, all monotone QFI metrics coincide to leading order with a universal quantity that can be expressed as the variance of a simple observable and itself defines a lesser-known QFI metric. This emergent uniqueness is reminiscent of the uniqueness of classical Fisher information in information geometry. In continuous-variable systems, the infinite-temperature limit is finite and generically non-zero for quadratic Gaussian signals, while it diverges for higher-degree signals. Remarkably, for any purely quadratic signal generator, the infinite-temperature QFI is at least twice the zero-temperature SLD QFI or, equivalently, at least eight times the ground-state variance of the generator. Consequently, the Cramér--Rao lower bound on the estimator variance is reduced by at least a factor of two. Abstract

We propose a quantum version of the Barankin bound as an alternative to the quantum Cramér-Rao bound for quantum parameter estimation. The quantum Barankin bound provides a lower bound on the mean squared error of estimators satisfying arbitrarily chosen bias constraints at arbitrarily chosen parameter points. In particular, unbiasedness over the entire parameter space can be imposed, yielding precision limits for globally unbiased quantum parameter estimation. In contrast to the recently derived quantum Barankin bound, which is based on a symmetric division superoperator, our bound is based on a nonsymmetric right-division superoperator. This formulation enables us to find analytic expressions for the Barankin matrix for Gaussian states, which allows for an efficient calculation of the bound. We demonstrate the usefulness of our results by applying them to various examples that exhibit the threshold effect in the few-shot regime, which is invisible to the standard quantum Cramér-Rao bound approach.

Abstract

Can propagating gravitational waves serve as operational probes of the quantum nature of gravity? We address this question by developing a unified theoretical framework that combines spacetime geometry, quantum information, and gravitational-wave physics. Starting from the geodesic deviation equation in linearized General Relativity, we derive the effective detector Hamiltonian directly from spacetime geometry and construct the complete quantum dynamics for detector subsystems interacting with both classical and quantized propagating gravitational-wave fields. This unified formulation enables a direct comparison between classical and quantum descriptions of gravitational radiation within the same physical framework. We demonstrate that classical gravitational-wave backgrounds can induce mixedness in the detector state but cannot generate genuine quantum correlations between the detector subsystems. In contrast, quantized gravitational waves coherently mediate gravity-induced entanglement, quantum coherence, quantum memory, and nonclassical correlations, providing clear operational signatures of the quantum nature of propagating gravitational radiation. We further discuss how mesoscopic quantum mechanical oscillators offer a promising route towards experimentally probing these effects. Our results establish a geometric and quantum-information-based framework for exploring quantum gravity through propagating gravitational waves.
