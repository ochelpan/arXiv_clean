Gate Control Improves Routing Efficiency for Periodic Traffic

Authors: Hyunwook Song, Hoyun Choi, B. Kahng
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.01534v1

Summary. This paper models traffic flow on complex networks using a two-queue system and compares two control protocols: Free Entry (FE) and Restricted Entry (RE). It demonstrates that the RE protocol acts as a superior self-regulating buffer, mitigating the severity of congestion and changing the jamming transition from abrupt to gradual, especially when external demand fluctuates periodically.

Why it may be interesting. The study of non-equilibrium dynamics, phase transitions (jamming), and the effect of external periodic forcing on collective behavior is directly relevant to non-equilibrium statistical mechanics and open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how regulating entry into a main flow queue, using a two-queue gate control mechanism, improves routing efficiency in complex networks subjected to periodic traffic fluctuations.

Main result. The restricted entry (RE) protocol significantly outperforms the free-entry (FE) protocol, particularly under large periodic fluctuations, by replacing sudden jamming transitions with gradual, continuous ones.

Method. The analysis uses macroscopic and microscopic modeling of traffic flow, comparing the dynamics of FE and RE protocols using order parameters and time-averaged system occupancy.

Model / system. The model simulates traffic movement on complex networks (like ER or BA graphs) using a two-queue system (waiting and flowing queues) at each node, driven by a time-varying external demand.

Key observables. Total system vehicles A(t), order parameter $\rho$ (congestion rate), and the critical transition point $p_c$.

Important parameters / regimes. Periodic fluctuation amplitude $\eta$, driving period $T$, and the traffic-awareness parameter $h$.

Assumptions / limitations. The approach relies on a simple local rule using only local queue information, making it highly implementable.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the temporal evolution of system occupancy $A(t)$ for both protocols under varying $\eta$, and phase diagrams showing the transition point $p_c$ dependence on $\eta$ for both FE and RE.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the two-queue model and protocols (FE vs. RE) to performing macroscopic analysis of congestion transitions ($\rho$ vs. $p_0$), and finally analyzing the impact of periodic forcing ($\eta > 0$) on the stability and transition characteristics.

Abstract

Traffic movement on highways and data packet transmission across the Internet serve as prototypical examples of collective dynamical behavior in complex systems. Although enhancing traffic flow in complex networks has been extensively investigated, most existing studies focus on single-queue models. In this paper, we introduce a simple yet effective two-queue scheme with gate control that regulates vehicle entry and integrates it with the routing protocol. When vehicles arrive at a on-ramp gate, they first join a waiting queue and are permitted to enter the main flowing queue once it has emptied. This two-queue strategy is especially advantageous when highway inflow varies periodically with large fluctuations, as is commonly observed in real traffic. Moreover, it modifies the system's failure characteristics, replacing sudden, discontinuous jamming transitions with gradual, continuous ones. The approach is based on a simple rule using only local queue information, making it easy to implement and widely applicable. Beyond the specific setup studied here, it can be applied to other transportation networks under different conditions or extended to other queuing systems, such as Internet data packet routing.

Summary. This paper proves that quantum Gibbs samplers rapidly mix to the true thermal Gibbs state for 1D quantum spin chains at any temperature. The mixing time scales logarithmically with system size, $O(\log(n/\epsilon))$, and the required quantum circuit depth for state preparation is also polylogarithmic. This provides a powerful theoretical tool for simulating thermalization in quantum many-body physics.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous, resource-efficient theoretical guarantees for simulating thermalization dynamics in quantum many-body systems, which is crucial for understanding quantum thermalization and equilibrium states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to prove the rapid mixing time of quantum Gibbs samplers for 1D quantum spin chains at any finite temperature.

Main result. Rapid mixing is proven for the quantum block heat-bath dynamics in $O(\log(n/\epsilon))$ time, and the Gibbs state preparation can be done with $O( ext{polylog}(n/\epsilon))$ quantum circuit depth.

Method. The analysis uses a path-coupling argument bounding the evolution in the quantum Wasserstein distance of order 1, combined with techniques for approximating quantum channels with efficient quantum circuits.

Model / system. The system is a 1D quantum spin chain on a periodic lattice ($\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$) governed by a nearest-neighbor Hamiltonian. The dynamics are simulated using a quantum block heat-bath process defined by a Lindbladian.

Key observables. Mixing Time ($t_{ ext{mix}}$), Quantum Wasserstein distance ($W_1$), and Quantum Circuit Depth.

Important parameters / regimes. Finite temperature ($eta$), system size ($n$), error tolerance ($\epsilon$), and constant block length ($\ell$).

Assumptions / limitations. The Hamiltonian is assumed to have bounded local interactions, and the proof relies on establishing specific boundary estimates and Lipschitz control conditions.

Figures summary. Figure 1 outlines the proof structure linking static properties to dynamical mixing. Table 1 compares the derived mixing time bound against other known results for spin systems.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the rapid mixing result using the quantum Wasserstein distance, followed by providing an explicit quantum circuit depth bound for state preparation. Later sections confirm the optimality of the logarithmic scaling for both periodic and open boundary conditions.

Abstract

We prove that a quasi-local quantum Gibbs sampler rapidly mixes to the Gibbs state of any 1D finite-range Hamiltonian at all finite temperatures in $\mathcal{O}\left(\log (n/\varepsilon)\right)$ time. Each update traces out a contiguous block of constant length and reconstructs it using the Petz recovery map, following early proposals for quantum heat-bath Gibbs samplers by Kastoryano and Brandão~\cite{kastoryano2016quantum}. Our proof directly bounds the distance between the updated state and the target Gibbs state using quantum Wasserstein distance of order $1$. We further provide $\mathcal{O}(\text{polylog}(n/\varepsilon))$ depth of quantum circuits to prepare these Gibbs states. We hope these techniques will facilitate the analysis and design of other Gibbs samplers.

Summary. This paper analyzes pulsar spin noise using a two-component model for a neutron star's rotation, distinguishing between models where total angular momentum is conserved (stationary) versus those where it diffuses (nonstationary). By developing Gaussian process methods, the authors show that joint observations of both the crust and superfluid components are necessary to robustly test the fundamental question of angular momentum conservation in these extreme astrophysical objects.

Why it may be interesting. The rigorous application of Gaussian process methods to model complex, multi-component stochastic dynamics in a physical system like a neutron star provides a template for analyzing open quantum systems where multiple coupled degrees of freedom are involved.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central problem is testing the stationarity of pulsar spin noise, which is equivalent to determining whether the total angular momentum of a neutron star is conserved.

Main result. The authors show that the two-component model can be analyzed for both nonstationary (diffusive) and stationary (conserved) dynamics, and these models are fully testable using joint observations.

Method. The analysis develops scalable Gaussian process methods to construct and analyze the likelihood function for observing both the crust and superfluid rotational states simultaneously.

Model / system. The system is a two-component dynamical model of a neutron star's rotation, involving a crust component and a superfluid component, whose dynamics are governed by stochastic torques.

Key observables. Pulsar timing data (measuring the crust's rotational state) and continuous gravitational wave observations (inferred superfluid interior dynamics).

Important parameters / regimes. Relaxation time scale ($ au$), ratio ($r$), and various steady-state averages ($\langle\langle\alpha_c angle angle, \langle\langle\Delta\Omega angle angle, Q_c, Q_s$).

Assumptions / limitations. Robust inference is difficult if only one component (e.g., crust) is observationally accessible, and the analysis relies on the assumption of Gaussian noise processes.

Figures summary. Figure C3 shows parameter posteriors for singular Brownian motion, comparing fixed noise vs. joint state+noise estimation, and includes noise diagnostics for residuals.

Paper structure. The paper develops the two-component model, derives the necessary Gaussian process methods, and then applies these methods to distinguish between the nonstationary (diffusive) and stationary (conserved) limits of the total angular momentum.

Abstract

We present an analysis of pulsar spin noise based on physically-motivated two-component models of spin wandering. We focus on two models, distinguished by their total angular momentum dynamics: a singular, nonstationary model with a diffusive total angular momentum, and a minimal, stationary model anchored on a conserved total angular momentum. We develop scalable Gaussian process methods and, using mock data, show that the two models are fully testable and distinguishable with full-state observations, i.e., simultaneous independent data on the crust and the superfluid rotational states. This paves a path to testing stationarity in pulsar spin noise, potentially achievable with joint continuous gravitational wave observations and radio timing of pulsars. However, robust inferences and predictions are harder to achieve, and depend on the priors and data quality, when only one component is observationally accessible.

Summary. This work investigates a quantum Ising chain with complex anisotropic interactions to map out its phase diagram and dynamic critical points. By analyzing vector chiral order, entanglement entropy, and the Loschmidt amplitude, the authors demonstrate that the competition between different interactions leads to rich quantum phases and distinct dynamical critical behaviors. It highlights how off-diagonal exchange terms significantly enrich both the static and time-dependent physics of the quantum magnet.

Why it may be interesting. The paper provides a detailed study of how geometric frustration (dimerization) and anisotropic interactions enrich both the equilibrium phase diagram (chiral order) and the non-equilibrium dynamics (DQPTs), offering rich material for understanding quantum criticality beyond simple Ising models.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the emergence of vector chiral order and the nature of dynamical quantum phase transitions (DQPTs) in a quantum Ising chain subjected to dimerized anisotropic Gamma interactions.

Main result. The competition between Gamma anisotropy and the transverse field dictates the phase diagram, revealing distinct critical behaviors, including a gapless critical line in the absence of Ising interactions and periodic/aperiodic sequences for critical times.

Method. The analysis employs theoretical techniques including Jordan-Wigner transformation, mapping the system to a fermionic Hamiltonian, and utilizing the Loschmidt amplitude to probe non-equilibrium dynamics.

Model / system. The system is a transverse-field Ising chain incorporating dimerized anisotropic Gamma interactions. The Hamiltonian is analyzed using a Bogoliubov quasiparticle formulation, which is then used to calculate correlation functions and the Loschmidt amplitude.

Key observables. Vector spin chirality, vector chiral order parameter, spin-spin correlation functions ($C_{xx}^r, C_{xy}^r$), subsystem entanglement entropy ($S_L$), and the Loschmidt amplitude/rate function ($G(t), g(t)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Transverse field ($h$), Ising coupling ($J$), and the anisotropic Gamma interaction ($\Gamma$), along with the dimerization parameter ($\alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the system starts in the ground state for DQPT calculations, and the fermionic representation is used to diagonalize the Hamiltonian.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the phase diagram based on chiral order, the closing of the energy gap ($\Delta=0$) defining critical lines, and the rate function $g(t)$ showing nonanalytic cusps marking DQPT critical times.

Paper structure. The paper systematically explores the equilibrium phase diagram using chiral order parameters, analyzes correlation functions and entanglement entropy to characterize critical points, and finally uses the Loschmidt amplitude to independently probe DQPTs, comparing the results from both equilibrium and non-equilibrium diagnostics.

Abstract

We investigate a transverse-field Ising chain with dimerized anisotropic Gamma interactions and demonstrate that, both in the absence and presence of Ising interactions, the competition between Gamma anisotropy and the transverse field gives rise to vector chiral order and dynamical quantum phase transitions. The phase diagram comprises positive and negative vector chiral phases and a paramagnetic phase, characterized by the vector spin chirality and the vector chiral order parameter. In the absence of Ising interactions, we identify a gapless critical line that exhibits no finite-size drift, along which the spin-spin correlations and subsystem entanglement entropy display behavior distinct from that of Ising criticality. Furthermore, we find that the critical times of dynamical quantum phase transitions form periodic or aperiodic sequences, depending on the quench path. Our findings highlight how dimerized off-diagonal exchange enriches both equilibrium ordering and dynamical critical behavior in a quantum Ising chain.

Summary. This theoretical paper investigates the renormalization group flow of stochastic turbulence to determine if perturbative results can be reliably extrapolated to physical conditions. By calculating the exponent $\omega$ to high loop orders, the authors find evidence suggesting that the fixed point governing the system loses its real stability before reaching the physical forcing strength, implying a fundamental breakdown of the simple perturbative RG description.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical fluid dynamics, the methodology—using advanced RG techniques to probe the breakdown of perturbative expansions—is highly relevant to understanding non-equilibrium critical phenomena in quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates whether the perturbative Renormalization Group (RG) description of stochastic turbulence can be smoothly extended from small forcing parameters ($\epsilon \approx 0$) to the physically relevant large-scale forcing ($\epsilon_{ ext{phys}} = 2$).

Main result. The analysis suggests that the RG fixed point loses its reality or stability in an intermediate range of $\epsilon$, indicating a possible obstruction to a simple, controlled perturbative extrapolation to the physical regime.

Method. The authors employed advanced RG techniques, calculating the correction-to-scaling exponent $\omega$ up to five-loop order in the large-$d$ limit, and using pattern recognition (OEIS) to conjecture a closed-form expression for $\omega(\epsilon)$.

Model / system. The system modeled is homogeneous isotropic stochastic turbulence, governed by the Navier-Stokes equation driven by a Gaussian random force. The analysis is performed in the large spatial dimension limit ($d o \infty$).

Key observables. The correction-to-scaling exponent $\omega(\epsilon)$, the stability and reality domains of the RG fixed point, and the renormalized parameters ($ u_Z, g_0, Z_g, D_0$).

Important parameters / regimes. The forcing parameter $\epsilon$, which varies from the perturbative limit ($\epsilon o 0$) to the physical limit ($\epsilon_{ ext{phys}} = 2$). The large dimension limit ($d o \infty$) is crucial for the calculations.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary assumptions include performing calculations in the large-$d$ limit and treating the derived closed-form expression for $\omega(\epsilon)$ as a conjecture based on observed coefficient patterns.

Figures summary. Figure 1 is noted to show the conjectured closed-form expression for $\omega(\epsilon)$ and plots the stability and reality domains of the RG fixed point, highlighting the physical value $\epsilon_{ ext{phys}} = 2$.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by setting up the stochastic turbulence model via field theory, performing multi-loop calculations of the RG flow equations (determining $\omega$), conjecturing a closed form for $\omega(\epsilon)$, and finally analyzing the stability and reality of the resulting fixed point solution across the parameter space $\epsilon \in [0, 2]$.

Abstract

We examine whether the perturbative renormalization group (RG) regime of stochastic turbulence can be continued from small $\varepsilon$ to the physical value $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{phys}}=2$, corresponding to large-scale forcing. Exploiting the simplifications of the large-$d$ limit, we calculate the correction exponent $ω$ to five-loop order and find a simple structure suggesting a closed analytic form. This form implies that the fixed point stable near $\varepsilon=0$ ceases to be simultaneously real and infrared stable before reaching $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{phys}}=2$, pointing to a possible change of infrared regime rather than a controlled perturbative RG extrapolation.

Summary. This theoretical paper analyzes the information content gained from measuring a weak absorber using single photons in an interferometer. It demonstrates that while negative quasiprobabilities are theoretically significant, they do not improve the information per absorbed photon in a single pass. True advantages are found either through phase accumulation over multiple passes or when the measurement setup is designed to exploit specific interference effects.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous theoretical bounds on quantum metrology, specifically quantifying when and how negative quasiprobabilities can enhance measurement sensitivity beyond classical limits in the context of photon loss.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the fundamental limits on the information gain per absorbed photon when performing interaction-free measurements on a weak sample.

Main result. For single-pass measurements, negativity does not improve the total information per absorbed photon; advantage only accumulates over multiple, uncounted passes or when the phase response is significant.

Method. The analysis uses the Fisher information derived from the linear response of output probabilities to weak absorption or phase shifts, utilizing negative Kirkwood-Dirac quasiprobabilities.

Model / system. The system models a single photon passing through a $d$-path interferometer containing a weak absorber with transmittance $t$. The analysis extends to multi-pass scenarios using ring resonators.

Key observables. Fisher information ($F_s(t)$), evidence per absorbed photon ($\eta$), and the complex Kirkwood-Dirac quasiprobabilities ($\varrho_C$).

Important parameters / regimes. Transmittance $t$, number of passes $\ell$, and the weak value $\langle\hat{\Pi}(a) angle_w$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis often relies on approximations, such as the Gaussian regime or small probabilities of absorption ($P(a)$).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the interferometer setup, showing how the single-shot gain ($R_M^{-1}$) decays for different measurement limits and how the gain is enhanced by negative weak values near null ports.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the single-pass bound using Fisher information (Theorem 1) to analyzing the role of negativity, and finally extends the analysis to multi-pass systems and the evidence gain ($\eta$) using advanced entropy measures.

Abstract

Negative Kirkwood--Dirac quasiprobabilities are credited with the quantum advantage of interaction-free measurement and postselected metrology. We test that claim on the task interaction-free measurement was designed for: learning about a sample letting it absorb as little light as possible. The Kirkwood--Dirac distribution of path and output gives the linear response to a weak sample: its real part responds to absorption, its imaginary part to a phase. For a pure single photon passing once through any interferometer, the Fisher information about transmittance per absorbed photon is the direct-transmission value minus a sum of squared imaginary parts: no interferometer or readout beats direct transmission. Negativity plays no role beyond concentrating information in weakly lit outputs. The imaginary parts of the Kirkwood-Dirac distribution depend on the output basis here, not on the sample, so a basis exists in which every term is real, which is optimal. A single photon gains information per absorbed photon only through a phase response, which for an optical line needs a thick or inhomogeneously broadened sample, or through many passes when absorptions go uncounted, as shown by a critically coupled resonator. For detecting a weak absorber, negativity gives a single-shot advantage that does not accumulate over multiple trials; advantage instead accumulates when there are outputs which are dark without the object, a case which needs no negativity.

Summary. This work develops a transport theory for a Majorana interferometer incorporating edge vortex tunneling. It finds that while DC conductance is insensitive to topological effects, the low-frequency response reveals an effective capacitance sensitive to the non-Abelian statistics of the edge vortices. This provides a measurable diagnostic for topological physics in superconducting quantum devices.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on topological signatures (non-Abelian statistics) measurable via capacitance in a superconducting platform makes it highly relevant for understanding topological quantum computation pathways.

Detailed structure

Main problem. To develop a transport theory for a generic two-arm co-propagating Majorana interferometer that incorporates both Majorana fermion tunneling and the effects of edge vortex (EV) tunneling.

Main result. While the DC conductance does not show signatures of edge-vortex interference, the interference manifests as an effective capacitance at low frequencies, which is sensitive to the non-Abelian anyon statistics of the edge vortices.

Method. The theory employs a replicated bosonization field theory framework to analyze the linear-response conductance tensor, generalizing previous treatments to asymmetric configurations.

Model / system. The system is a Majorana interferometer realized on a Topological Insulator proximitized by a Superconductor, featuring two co-propagating chiral Majorana edge modes and coupling points for both Majorana fermions and EVs.

Key observables. Effective capacitance ($C_{ ext{eff}}$) at low frequencies, and the frequency-dependent conductance tensor $G(\omega)$.

Abstract

Co-propagating chiral Majorana edge modes are predicted to exist in proximity-coupled quantum anomalous Hall systems. They combine the exciting prospects of edge vortices as flying non-Abelian Ising anyons, with their fully electrical detection through anyon fusion processes. In this setup, electrical transport arises from the interference of a pair of Majorana edge states. By bosonizing the model with an additional replica sector, we generalize previous treatments to fully asymmetric configurations. We analyze a Mach-Zehnder interferometer and show that while the DC conductance does not exhibit signatures of edge-vortex interference, at low frequencies, this interference manifests as an effective capacitance. This quantity is sensitive to the non-Abelian anyon statistics of edge vortices and, in particular, carries signatures of their topological spin and nontrivial conformal dimension. We demonstrate that the thermal length $l_T = \hbar v/(πk_B T)$ is the relevant scale at low temperatures $T$, analogous to quasiparticle transport in conventional quantum Hall edge states with velocity $v$. As long as the interferometer arm lengths match within this length scale, transport signatures of the non-Abelian statistics of edge vortices remain visible.

Summary. This paper demonstrates that adding local dissipation to the Bose-Hubbard model restores a robust, state-independent Lieb-Robinson bound, overcoming a major hurdle in analyzing bosonic quantum dynamics. By regularizing the state via photon loss, the authors prove that local approximations are valid uniformly in the initial state. This has significant implications for digital quantum simulation and quantum error correction codes.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial theoretical tool—state-independent locality bounds—for bosonic systems, which is highly valuable for analyzing quantum simulation protocols and understanding information flow in complex quantum hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of establishing operator-norm Lieb-Robinson bounds for bosonic lattice systems, which is difficult because information propagation velocity can depend on the local boson occupancy.

Main result. The authors prove that introducing local dissipation (on-site $\ell$-photon loss with $\ell>2$) restores an almost-ballistic Lieb-Robinson bound, allowing for uniform state-independent approximations.

Method. The core method involves using local dissipation to regularize the dynamics, allowing the use of Sobolev regularization techniques to bound the difference between the full and truncated evolution.

Model / system. The system is the Bose-Hubbard model on a lattice, described by a Lindbladian master equation incorporating on-site $\ell$-photon loss. Extensions cover cat-code dissipation and quartic bosonic models.

Key observables. Operator-norm local approximation estimates, Lieb-Robinson bounds, and the decay rate of the difference between full and restricted evolution.

Important parameters / regimes. The dissipation order $\ell > 2$, the hopping coefficients $J_{ij}$, and the rates $\gamma_j$ are crucial parameters determining the bound's validity.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the existence of Sobolev-preserving and regularizing quantum Markov semigroups and the assumption of sufficiently strong local dissipation ($\ell>2$).

Paper structure. The paper builds from establishing the general difficulty of locality bounds in bosonic systems to introducing dissipation as a regularizing mechanism. It then proves the main Lieb-Robinson bound for the Bose-Hubbard model, extends this to cat-code dissipation, and concludes with applications like local adiabatic approximations.

Abstract

Closed bosonic lattice systems do not generally admit operator norm Lieb--Robinson bounds uniformly in the initial state. The reason is that the information propagation velocity can grow with the local boson occupancy, which can be macroscopically large. Here, we show that local dissipation restores an operator-norm Lieb--Robinson bound. We consider the dissipative Bose--Hubbard model described by a Lindbladian operator with on-site $\ell$-photon loss, $\ell>2$ and, for our main result, we establish an almost-ballistic Lieb-Robinson bound. Dissipation rapidly depletes highly occupied sites, thus regularizing the state on the Sobolev-type scale of local particle moments. The resulting moment bounds diverge as time approaches zero but remain integrable near zero at sufficiently low orders. We extend these ideas to treat cat-code dissipation and, for initial states in the code space, we prove a local adiabatic approximation uniform in the total volume. Further consequences include local channel approximation, a thermodynamic limit, and efficient digital quantum simulation. The point is that these applications are now available \textit{uniformly in the input state}, as for quantum spin systems, but in contrast to closed Bose--Hubbard systems.

We show that: (1) the ground energy of $H$ can be approximated to additive error $\varepsilon$ by a classical algorithm with runtime $\textsf{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)$, improving on the quasi-polynomial runtime of the best previously known algorithm; (2) at inverse temperature $β$, the Helmholtz free energy and a classical description of the thermofield double state can be computed to precision $\varepsilon$ in time $\textsf{poly}(n,β,1/\varepsilon)$; (3) simulating the time evolution $e^{-iHt}$ is $\textsf{BQP}$-complete, for $H$ that is time-independent and has a fixed, constant impurity size. Our algorithms exploit exponential suppression of multi-particle bath excitations in a basis organized by energy scale and Krylov depth. Our universality construction realizes a stationary quantum processor whose program arrives in a stream of freely propagating fermions.

Abstract

We study Hamiltonian learning in a novel setting, where we are promised a physical structure that is obfuscated by a global unitary. In particular, we consider the task of learning the weights $E_i$ of a Hamiltonian $H=U^{\dagger}(\sum_{i \in I} E_i n_i + H_O)U$ comprised of a set of non-interacting modes $I$ and an arbitrary ``spectator'' $H_O$ acting on a disjoint set of modes $O$. The non-interacting structure is hidden by an unknown global unitary $U$, and our goal is to recover the $E_i$ without reconstructing $U$. Such Hamiltonians are well-motivated in the setting of materials modeling, where they find application for classical methods such as mean-field theory, Green's functions, and density functional theory. We show that this problem is DQC1-hard, despite the apparent simplification given by the non-interacting structure. We then introduce SPICES (single-particle inference via contour estimators), which combines a Hadamard test with a novel classical postprocessing procedure to efficiently recover the spectral distribution in Wasserstein distance under certain natural assumptions. The method exploits the correspondence between single-particle energies of the individual quasiparticles and the Fourier frequencies governing zeros of the partition function at complex inverse temperature. As quasiparticle spectra feature ubiquitously in downstream materials calculations, and with SPICES being simple and practical to execute, we expect that SPICES is a promising route to quantum applications in materials simulation.
