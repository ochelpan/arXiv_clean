Landscape-Dependent Performance of Photonic Quantum Solvers in QUBO Feature Selection for Financial Risk Detection

Summary. This paper compares classical and photonic quantum solvers for feature selection in financial risk detection using two large datasets. It demonstrates that the choice of solver and method is critical, showing that quantum approaches can outperform classical methods when the underlying feature signal is highly concentrated. This highlights the potential role of quantum computation in solving complex, structured optimization problems in finance.

Why it may be interesting. The work directly tests the practical utility of quantum computation (photonic solvers) for solving complex, real-world optimization problems arising in data science, providing empirical benchmarks for quantum advantage in optimization.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses feature selection for imbalanced financial classification tasks by comparing classical optimization methods against photonic quantum solvers.

Main result. Photonic solvers (Dirac-3) showed superior performance over classical solvers (Gurobi) in certain scenarios, particularly when the predictive signal is highly concentrated in a small feature subset (ULB dataset).

Method. The authors benchmarked three computing paradigms—classical branch-and-bound, photonic entropic computing, and photonic boson sampling—across various QUBO and graph-theoretic feature selection methods.

Model / system. The model involves formulating feature selection as a combinatorial optimization problem, utilizing two financial datasets: ULB Credit Card Fraud and AmEx Consumer Default. The computational platforms include classical solvers and photonic quantum devices.

Key observables. F1 score (primary metric), Gini Coefficient, and Jaccard similarity, used to quantify classification performance and feature redundancy.

Important parameters / regimes. Feature budget ($k$), dataset size (30 vs. 159 features), and the specific mathematical structure of the objective function (QUBO, higher-order terms).

Assumptions / limitations. The comparison assumes that the mathematical structure of the feature selection method dictates the appropriate quantum or classical solver, and that the performance gap between solvers is measurable.

Figures summary. The notes reference tables and matrices showing F1 and Gini scores across various methods and feature budgets for both datasets, and similarity matrices illustrating feature overlap.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem (feature selection for imbalanced data), details the datasets and the three computational paradigms (Gurobi, Dirac-3, Piquasso), systematically compares performance across multiple methods (Tier 1, 2, 3), and analyzes the dependence of performance on dataset structure and feature budget.

Abstract

Feature selection for imbalanced classification tasks such as credit card fraud and consumer default detection requires balancing predictive relevance, inter-feature redundancy, and computational feasibility. We benchmark three computing paradigms, classical branch-and-bound optimization (Gurobi), photonic entropy computing (QCI Dirac-3), and simulated photonic boson sampling (Piquasso), across thirteen feature-selection methods on two datasets: ULB Credit Card Fraud (30 features) and AmEx consumer default (159 features). Each method is routed to the solver matched to its mathematical structure. On ULB, Dirac-3 MI-Spearman matches the all-features model using 13 of 30 features (mean F1 0.873 +/- 0.023 over five runs, best run 0.896), and Piquasso is the best method at k=5. On AmEx, performance rises steadily with the feature budget and every paradigm approaches F1 = 0.80 only near the full feature set. Most differences between Gurobi and Dirac-3 on identical methods fall within run-to-run variation; the large gaps occur where the certified optimum generalizes poorly, most sharply for distance correlation on AmEx at k=25 (Gurobi F1 = 0.422 vs. a Dirac-3 mean of 0.746). At matched budgets, F1 varies about ten times more across methods on ULB than on AmEx, which we trace to how concentrated the predictive signal is in each feature space.

Summary. This paper introduces a quantum algorithm to efficiently sample rare events in stochastic processes modeled by Quantum Hidden Markov Models (QHMMs). Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of rare-event sampling—studying outcomes of high consequence but low probability—which is computationally prohibitive using classical methods.

Model / system. The framework is built upon Quantum Hidden Markov Models (QHMMs), which generalize classical HMMs using completely-positive maps. The dynamics are modeled by sequential application of unitary operators over $L$ timesteps.

Abstract

In a world fraught with uncertainty, it behooves us to consider the most disastrous possible outcomes, in order that we can best mitigate against them. Often these are extreme risk events -- of high consequence, but low probability. Yet, the rarity of such events limits our ability to study them; there is a paucity of past occurrences to draw upon, and sampling such rare events from models is typically costly in time. Here, we introduce an approach for rare-event sampling based on quantum stochastic models. This integrates the memory advantages of quantum stochastic modelling -- and its associated efficient means of preparing quantum sample states -- with the `speed-ups' of quantum amplitude amplification, to yield simultaneous reductions in spatial and temporal computational complexity compared to classical Monte Carlo-based approaches.

Summary. This paper proves a super-exponential advantage of using squeezed vacuum states over coherent states for phase estimation when the measurement process involves discrete phase randomization. By analyzing the Quantum Fisher Information and required sample complexity, the authors show that squeezed light can achieve significantly higher precision with fewer measurements, even in the presence of noise. This highlights the profound resource advantage of non-classical quantum states in advanced quantum sensing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the ultimate precision limits in quantum phase estimation when the measurement channel involves discrete phase randomization (twirling). It aims to demonstrate a super-exponential advantage of using squeezed light over classical coherent states for this task.

Main result. A super-exponential separation in the Quantum Fisher Information (QFI) and required sample complexity is shown between squeezed vacuum states and coherent states, even when considering noise and loss.

Method. The analysis involves comparing the QFI bounds derived from squeezed vacuum states versus coherent states, utilizing both asymptotic scaling arguments and non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds.

Model / system. The system is a bosonic mode undergoing phase estimation under a channel that implements a phase shift by $2\pi k/M$ (discrete randomization) plus a small unknown phase shift $\delta$. The comparison is drawn between squeezed vacuum states and coherent states.

Key observables. Quantum Fisher Information (QFI), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and required number of samples ($ u$).

Important parameters / regimes. Mean photon number per channel use ($ar{n}$), the randomization parameter $M$, and the achievable accuracy $\epsilon$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is constrained by the mean photon number per channel use ($ar{n}$) and considers the effect of optical loss and thermal noise.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes detail comparisons of QFI scaling and sample complexity bounds for different noise regimes.

Paper structure. The paper builds from general QFI analysis to specific comparisons of scaling laws for squeezed vs. coherent states, culminating in non-asymptotic results considering noise and loss.

Abstract

A paradigmatic task in quantum metrology is that of phase estimation. For this task it is commonly believed, under reasonable assumptions about prior information, that a quadratic advantage in sensitivity is the maximum possible advantage offered by the use of quantum resources. In this work, we consider a channel that implements a phase shift by an angle $2πk/M$, where $k$ is a uniformly random unknown integer, in addition to a small unknown phase shift of interest. We show that, subject to a constraint on the mean photon number per channel use, there is a super-exponential separation between the quantum Fisher information (QFI) attainable using a squeezed vacuum state and that attainable using coherent states. This advantage holds over any state obtained from a coherent state via phase covariant channels. Specifically, when the mean photon number per channel use is 1, the ratio of their QFI grows as $Ω[(M/\sqrt{2}e)^M]$. For a mean photon number per channel use of $M/\log(M)$, the squeezed vacuum QFI is $Ω(M^{3/2}/\sqrt{\log(M)})$, whereas for a coherent state it is at most $\exp[-M\log\log(M)+O(M)]$. We complement this QFI analysis with non-asymptotic estimation results. For a mean photon number per channel use of $M/\log M$, an explicit squeezed-vacuum protocol estimates the phase to accuracy $O(1/M)$ using $O(\sqrt{M\log M})$ channel uses, whereas every coherent state protocol requires at least $\exp[M\log\log M-O(M)]$ channel uses. We consider the effects of noise and show that the ratio of their QFIs can still grow super-exponentially in $M$. Finally, under fixed optical loss and thermal noise, choosing $\bar n=M^2/(\log M)^{3/2}$ allows squeezed vacuum with heterodyne detection to achieve accuracy $O(1/M)$ using sub-polynomially many channel uses, whereas every protocol in the specified classical family requires super-polynomially many.

This capability is then applied to quantum teleportation, showing that the encoded protocol can maintain a fidelity above the classical limit, even when the unencoded channel fails to transmit entanglement.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete, mathematically rigorous demonstration of how structured noise environments and specific physical interactions (two-body gates) can be harnessed to protect and enhance quantum communication protocols beyond classical limits.

Main result. The system achieves semi-universality in logical gate control, and under specific noise conditions, the encoded teleportation fidelity exceeds the classical bound of 1/2 while the unencoded channel is entanglement breaking.

Method. The analysis uses Lie-algebra arguments and the structure of the walled Brauer algebra to prove the existence of finite pulse sequences implementing arbitrary logical gates.

Model / system. The system involves $r$ fundamental qutrits transforming under $U \in SU(3)$ and $s$ dual qutrits under $\overline{U}$. Information is encoded in noiseless subsystems derived from the global Hilbert space $H_{r,s}$.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of fundamental ($r$) and dual ($s$) qutrits, and the specific noise parameters defining the Weyl mixture.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the analysis compares resources and fidelity bounds for unencoded versus encoded protocols.

Abstract

Collective noise can leave degrees of freedom unaffected, allowing them to store quantum information. Processing this information also requires physically available logical gates. We consider $r$ fundamental qutrits transforming under $U\in SU(3)$ and $s$ dual qutrits transforming under its complex conjugate $\overline U$, with collective action $R_{r,s}(U)=U^{\otimes r}\otimes\overline U^{\otimes s}$. Using the image of the walled Brauer algebra and Lie-algebra arguments, we show that adjacent exchanges within each qutrit type and one singlet-projector interaction between the two types generate any independently chosen family of special unitary gates on the noiseless subsystems by a finite pulse sequence, for every $r,s\geq1$. This establishes semi-universality of this system. We illustrate the result with a numerically optimised sequence of 24 pulses for three fundamental qutrits and one dual qutrit. We then use the encoding and available logical corrections for qutrit teleportation under correlated Weyl noise. Under explicit assumptions on channel composition and which trials are retained, and allowing for baseline implementation errors, we identify noise ranges in which encoded teleportation exceeds the classical fidelity bound of $1/2$, while the unencoded teleportation channel is entanglement breaking. We analyse fidelity on accepted trials separately from the transmission costs of the additional physical qutrits.

What Must a Quantum-Memory Decoder Know About Temporally Correlated Noise?

Authors: Danesh Morales-Hashemi, Graeme Smith, Ningping Cao
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03545v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: correlated / nonlocal dissipation 1/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

Summary. This paper analyzes the theoretical requirements for quantum error-correcting decoders operating under temporally correlated noise. It compares two noise models—one with a fixed global sign and one with independently resampled signs—showing that while their syndrome statistics match, their optimal recovery strategies differ. The work establishes scaling laws for the required experimental resources (calibration) to maintain high fidelity.

Why it may be interesting. It provides rigorous theoretical bounds on the information required to mitigate complex, time-dependent noise in quantum memory, which is crucial for scaling up quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates what information about temporally correlated noise a quantum error-correction (QEC) decoder must possess to achieve optimal recovery, and quantifies the cost of learning this information.

Main result. The optimal cost for complete QEC cycles scales inversely with the rotation angle raised to the code's X distance, and the authors prove matching calibration bounds for two different noise models (fixed vs. resampled signs).

Method. The analysis uses a statistical decision theory framework, formulating calibration in terms of fidelity differences and employing process tensors to model the full quantum evolution.

Model / system. The study uses fixed stabilizer memory experiments with CSS codes (e.g., Shor, Steane, surface codes) subjected to weak coherent X rotations, comparing two noise models: one where all qubits share a fixed sign, and one where the sign is redrawn independently each interval.

Key observables. Entanglement fidelity ($F$), fidelity loss ($\Delta F$), syndrome record ($y$), and the logical rotation accumulated over time.

Important parameters / regimes. The noise correlation structure (fixed vs. resampled signs), the code's X distance ($d_X$), and the rotation angle ($ heta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the ability to characterize the noise process via syndrome measurements and uses the Spatiotemporal Pauli Process (SPP) as a complete description for randomized errors.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the memory experiment flow, detailing the sequence from encoding, noise intervals, syndrome measurement, correction, and final recovery, alongside calibration runs.

Paper structure. The paper develops a general framework using process tensors to describe the logical instrument, then focuses on comparing the performance and required calibration data for different noise models (F vs. R) to determine the minimum information needed for optimal decoding.

Abstract

What must a quantum error-correction decoder know about temporally correlated noise, and what does it cost to learn? We study this question for fixed stabilizer memory experiments with known system-environment interactions. We formulate calibration in terms of the fidelity differences that select a Pauli recovery and relate missing information to the resulting fidelity loss. Our main example uses weak coherent $X$ rotations in a class of CSS codes including Shor, Steane, and odd-distance rotated surface codes. All physical qubits share a sign either fixed throughout a run or independently redrawn each interval. The two models have identical syndrome statistics and the same spatiotemporal Pauli process (SPP), obtained by Pauli-twirling every noise interval. Yet at suitable storage times, they require different logical corrections. In the weak-noise limit, each model is almost perfectly correctable when known. Without model information, even the best final recovery has a worst-case fidelity loss approaching $1/2$ relative to the noise-aware optimum, despite access to the full syndrome record. For this pair of models, at fixed code and fixed positive target loss below $1/2$, we prove matching calibration bounds. With logical preparation and final readout, complete QEC cycles require a number of noise intervals scaling as the inverse rotation angle raised to the code's $X$ distance. Allowing two uninterrupted intervals before one extraction reduces the optimal cost to inverse-square scaling. Physical Pauli twirling of every noise interval changes both recovery performance and its information requirements. Discarding the record of applied random Pauli operations makes the SPP sufficient for optimal recovery. Keeping this record can improve the attainable fidelity, but reaching that optimum may require additional noise information.

A Complete Proof of $\mathsf{QMA}_1$-Hardness for Weighted Gapped Clique Homology

Summary. This paper provides a complete and rigorous proof that determining the homology of weighted, spectrally gapped clique complexes is $\mathsf{QMA}_1$-hard. It achieves this by meticulously re-analyzing the local topological structures and spectral properties involved in the reduction from a known hard problem. The result establishes a strong computational barrier for simulating quantum systems based on topological features.

Why it may be interesting. This work connects deep topological invariants (homology) directly to the computational hardness of quantum physics problems, providing a rigorous complexity measure for quantum simulation feasibility.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to establish the computational complexity of determining non-trivial homology in weighted, spectrally gapped clique complexes.

Main result. Weighted gapped clique homology is proven to be $\mathsf{QMA}_1(\mathcal G)$-hard, providing a complete proof that resolves a gap in previous hardness results.

Method. The proof uses exact finite certificates and relative homology to analyze the local topology of gadgets, culminating in a quantitative global estimate that links the source-Hamiltonian gap to the weighted Hodge Laplacian.

Model / system. The model involves clique complexes derived from graphs, where the existence of a topological hole (non-trivial homology) is mapped to the existence of a zero-energy state in the Hodge Laplacian ($\Delta_k$).

Key observables. The existence of a zero eigenvalue in the Hodge Laplacian ($\Delta_k$) corresponding to a topological hole, and the quantitative spectral gap ($\gamma(n)$) for 'NO' instances.

Important parameters / regimes. The hardness holds for fixed efficiently computable $k(n)$ and inverse-polynomial $\gamma(n)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The argument requires a new, detailed analysis of the local topology and filtered cochain complexes to correct an analytical gap in prior work.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes detail the structure of matrices, rank checks, and specific identities required for the local gadget constructions.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the problem (homology complexity) to detailing the reduction structure, using exact finite certificates for local data, proving a finite-dimensional filtered-Hodge statement, and finally deriving the global spectral estimate and the main $\mathsf{QMA}_1$-hardness theorem.

Abstract

The computational complexity of simplicial homology has been studied since the question was raised by Kaibel and Pfetsch, and clique homology was later shown to be $\mathsf{QMA}_1$-hard by Crichigno and Kohler. King and Kohler subsequently introduced the weighted, spectrally gapped version considered here and developed a reduction intended to establish the same hardness result in this setting. We give a complete proof of the hard direction. The earlier King-Kohler argument contains a gap in the analysis of the nontrivially folded local gadgets, so the required topological and spectral properties have to be re-established on the clique complexes actually produced by the reduction. This is not a local repair of the published proof: the argument requires a new analysis of the local topology, the filtered cochain complexes, and the interaction of many overlapping gadgets. Our proof uses exact finite certificates for the local combinatorial data and relative homology to determine the effect of each gadget on the encoded qubit space. We then prove the finite-dimensional filtered-Hodge statement needed to recover the corresponding rank-one low-energy penalty and derive a quantitative global estimate that transfers the source-Hamiltonian promise gap to the final weighted Hodge Laplacian. Consequently, for fixed efficiently computable $k(n)$ and inverse-polynomial $γ(n)$, weighted gapped clique homology is $\mathsf{QMA}_1(\mathcal G)$-hard. The previously known $\mathsf{QMA}$-containment argument is logically independent of the gap and remains unaffected.

Summary. This paper develops an efficient quantum state tomography protocol for $n$-qubit states whose structure is limited by a low stabilizer rank $k$. The authors achieve this by first proving a tight upper bound on the state's 'stabilizer extent' ($\xi(|\psi angle) \leq 2^{O(k)}$). Main problem. The paper addresses the development of an efficient quantum state tomography protocol for $n$-qubit states $|\psi angle$ that are known to possess a low stabilizer rank $k$.

Main result. It establishes that for such states, the stabilizer extent $\xi(|\psi angle)$ is bounded by $2^{O(k)}$, leading to a polynomial-time tomography protocol for states where $k=O(\log n)$.

Method. The approach involves bounding the stabilizer extent $\xi(|\psi angle)$ using induction and Pauli operator projections, which then allows the application of an existing tomography protocol for states with bounded extent.

Model / system. The system under study is general $n$-qubit quantum states $|\psi angle$ that are restricted to having a stabilizer decomposition involving at most $k$ stabilizer states.

Key observables. Stabilizer rank ($k$), Stabilizer extent ($\xi(|\psi angle)$), and the fidelity of the reconstructed state.

Important parameters / regimes. The time complexity depends polynomially on $n$, $k$, and $1/\epsilon$. The efficiency is highlighted for $k=O(\log n)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The protocol assumes the input state $|\psi angle$ is promised to have a stabilizer rank $k$, and it relies on a pre-existing tomography protocol for states with bounded stabilizer extent.

Paper structure. The paper first proves a key bound on the stabilizer extent $\xi(|\psi angle) \leq 2^{O(k)}$ for a rank $k$ state. It then utilizes this bound to construct a full tomography protocol, showing efficiency for low-rank states.

Abstract

In this note, we consider tomography of $n$-qubit quantum states $|ψ\rangle$ that are promised to have stabilizer rank $k$ i.e., admit a stabilizer decomposition of $|ψ\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^k c_i |φ_i\rangle$ where each $|φ_i\rangle$ is a stabilizer state. We give a tomography protocol for such states up to trace distance $\varepsilon > 0$ running in $\textsf{poly}(n,2^k,1/\varepsilon)$ time and thus can handle $k=O(\log n)$ efficiently. This follows from first showing that the stabilizer extent $ξ(|ψ\rangle)$, which is the minimal $\ell_1$ norm over the coefficients $\{c_i\}$ of any stabilizer decomposition of $|ψ\rangle$, can be bounded as $ξ(|ψ\rangle) \leq 2^{O(k)}$ and improving upon the previous best bound of $k^{O(k)}$. The protocol is then obtained by utilizing a tomography protocol for states with bounded stabilizer extent.

Abstract

Quantum Max-Cut is a well-studied local Hamiltonian problem for which the classical complexity of the bipartite case remains open. We give a polynomial-time classical randomized algorithm for the spin-$\tfrac12$ antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model, equivalently Quantum Max-Cut, on dense balanced bipartite expanders. This class includes $G(n,n,p)$ with high probability for every fixed $p>0$. The algorithm estimates the ground energy and any ground state edge correlation to additive error $\varepsilon$ in time polynomial in $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$. We construct a Markov chain on perfect matchings of the complete bipartite graph, each matching representing a product of singlets, that converges to the ground state, and use the expansion of the graph to show that polynomially many steps and samples of this chain suffice.

Constant-Rate Certified Deletion

Main result. The authors present a unified framework achieving the first constant-rate CD constructions for a wide range of primitives, including PKE, ABE, and FHE, and extending this to publicly verifiable schemes under the hardness of SIS.

Abstract

We present a unified framework for upgrading a broad class of cryptographic primitives to support constant-rate certified deletion. Previous constructions require a linear number of qubits per encrypted bit of certified-deletable plaintext. In contrast, we obtain the first constant-rate constructions in the plain model that achieve certified deletion while preserving everlasting security. Our approach applies to a wide range of "all-or-nothing"-type primitives based on BB84-style encodings, including commitment schemes, public-key encryption, attribute-based encryption, and fully homomorphic encryption. Beyond this class, we also obtain constant-rate certified deletion for primitives built from subspace coset states, such as blind delegation, secure software leasing, functional encryption, and differing-inputs iO, and CCA-PKE. Importantly, our framework does not introduce any additional assumptions beyond those required by the underlying certified deletion primitives. Finally, under the hardness of SIS, we show that public verifiability can be incorporated into BB84- and coset-based certified deletion. In combination with our constant-rate constructions, this yields publicly verifiable certified deletion schemes with constant rate.

Abstract

A method for solving the two-center Dirac equation using a Gaussian-product basis set tailored to the axial symmetry of diatomic systems is presented. For the ground state of H$_2^+$ at an internuclear distance $R=2.0~a_0$, we obtain a relativistic energy with relative precision at the $10^{-18}$ level. The properties of the basis set allow for an analytic Fourier transform of the wave function. As a first application we evaluate, for the first time, the zero-potential contribution to the one-loop self-energy for H$_2^+$ in Feynman and Coulomb gauges. Although this contribution is not separately gauge invariant, its calculation provides a proof of principle for the use of the present framework in prospective QED calculations.

Abstract

Quantum Fourier sampling may help audit the spectral learnability of delay-based physical unclonable functions (PUFs). We ask whether that promise survives access matching, a strong classical comparator, and oracle synthesis. Three gates structure the evaluation. Structure: low degree is not small support at reachable challenge lengths; for 4-XOR at $n=14$, degree $\le d_f(0.1)$ admits $91\%$ of all $2^n$ characters and the median $90\%$-mass set spans a third of the spectrum. Algorithmics: constructing the phase oracle logically implies classical membership access, making Kushilevitz--Mansour the correct baseline; across 45 tasks it exhausts each finite domain, and no 4-XOR ideal-sampling case reaches $90\%$ mass within $2^n$ calls. A quantum-kernel diagnostic appears more favorable, with geometric difference rising to $2.151$ at $N=512$ challenges, but it correlates $0.991$ with $1/\sqrt{λ_{\min}(K_C)}$ for the classical Gram matrix $K_C$, and the 4-XOR label-complexity ratio does not exceed a balance-preserving permutation null ($p=0.930$). Trace-normalized geometric difference can therefore grow through classical ill-conditioning alone, without task-label alignment. Implementation: a simulator-validated fixed-point phase oracle based on the quantum Fourier transform admits an $18.9\%$ routed-depth reduction, yet the least certified precisions have estimated durations of $1.18$--$1.55\times$ the median dephasing time $T_2$ of the mapped qubits on a static backend snapshot, without hardware execution. We find no end-to-end advantage in the evaluated regime, although ideal sampling does use fewer coherent calls on the thresholded task. The contribution is the Three-Gate Quantum Audit Protocol: a reproducible procedure separating an ideal query advantage from a realizable security benefit. This is not a claim about deployed silicon and not an impossibility result.
