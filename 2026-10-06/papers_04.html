Minimax entropy production for arbitrary processes

Authors: Mahran Yousef, Ben Ansbacher, David H. Wolpert
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03478v1

Summary. This work develops a framework to calculate the minimax entropy production cost for physical processes, ranging from discrete maps to continuous-time dynamics. It shows that the optimal prior minimizes the worst-case cost, which is governed by the maximum uncertainty remaining after the process. The results provide universal scaling laws for the short-time behavior of this cost in Markovian systems.

Why it may be interesting. The framework provides a rigorous information-theoretic measure (minimax cost) for quantifying the inherent thermodynamic uncertainty or irreversibility associated with implementing a physical transformation, which has implications for non-equilibrium thermodynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the optimal prior distribution ($q$) for a physical system implementing a given map ($G$) that minimizes the expected thermodynamic cost (Mismatch Cost) in the worst-case scenario over all possible actual input distributions ($p$).

Main result. For deterministic maps, the minimax cost is determined by the maximum size of the input preimages (islands). For continuous-time dynamics, the instantaneous cost diverges, but the finite-time cost scales universally with the largest escape rate ($\lambda_{\max}$).

Method. The analysis uses variational principles and bounds on the Mismatch Cost, decomposing the problem across equivalence classes (islands) for discrete maps and employing small-time expansions for continuous-time Markov chains.

Model / system. The study considers general physical processes modeled by discrete stochastic maps, continuous-time Markov chains (CTMC), and abstract computational circuits (like Boolean gates). The cost function relates to entropy production and mismatch divergence.

Key observables. Minimax Mismatch Cost ($ ext{F}^*$), Shannon Entropy ($S$), Instantaneous Mismatch Cost Rate ($\dot{MMCA}$), and the maximum escape rate ($\lambda_{\max}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Time $t$, the maximum escape rate $\lambda_{\max}$, and the size of the largest preimage set $|I_y|$.

Assumptions / limitations. For continuous time, the analysis assumes the map family $P_t$ is differentiable and the CTMC is irreducible. For discrete maps, the worst-case input is shown to be a point mass.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison of minimax costs for different circuit decompositions (AND gates) and show the predicted divergence of the instantaneous mismatch-cost rate for a three-state CTMC.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from analyzing deterministic maps to stochastic maps, then extends the framework to continuous-time dynamics. It establishes the minimax cost for discrete cases, derives the divergence of the instantaneous rate, and finally solves the short-time limit for CTMCs.

Abstract

For any physical process, varying the initial distribution over its states will change the resulting expected entropy production (EP). An initial distribution that minimizes the EP of a particular process is called the prior of that process. Varying the microphysical details of the process will change its prior.We consider the following question: if an engineer wants to design a system to implement a given map, what prior should they design into the system to minimize the EP for the worst-case actual distribution? We derive the closed-form solution to this problem when the map is deterministic. We also show that for stochastic maps, a worst-case actual distribution can always be chosen to be a point mass on a single state, and that the associated minimax prior resembles a Boltzmann-Gibbs equation, and can be found by a globally convergent numerical algorithm. We also show that the associated minimax mismatch cost is the maximum uncertainty about the input that remains after observing the output. Finally, we extend the analysis to the continuous-time regime, showing that the minimax instantaneous mismatch-cost diverges, while the corresponding finite-time minimax mismatch cost has a universal short-time scaling set by the largest escape rate of the dynamics.

Optimal kernel functions for linear combination of Hamiltonian simulation

Summary. This paper develops a mathematically rigorous theory for optimizing the kernel function used in Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation (LCHS). By applying Hardy space theory and complex analysis, the authors determine the minimum achievable query cost for simulating non-unitary quantum dynamics. This establishes a provably optimal resource scaling for quantum simulation protocols.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides rigorous, mathematically optimal bounds for simulating open quantum systems or non-unitary dynamics, which is crucial for designing resource-efficient quantum algorithms and quantum control protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper aims to characterize the optimal kernel function required for the Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation (LCHS) method to achieve asymptotically optimal quantum simulation of linear non-unitary dynamics.

Main result. They establish an LCHS approximation theorem and derive the asymptotic cost scaling for the minimum query-cost functional, showing an improvement factor of $\approx 2.1$ over previous empirical bounds.

Method. The work employs advanced mathematical tools, including Hardy space theory, convex analysis, and complex contour integration, to solve an infinite-dimensional optimization problem.

Model / system. The model involves simulating quantum dynamics governed by linear, time-dependent, potentially non-Hermitian matrices, $A(t)$, whose evolution is described by the Peano–Baker propagator. The simulation is approximated via the LCHS framework.

Key observables. The primary observable is the minimum value of the query-cost functional, which scales asymptotically as $\log(1/\epsilon) - \log\log(1/\epsilon)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The target error ($\epsilon$) is the key parameter driving the asymptotic analysis, as well as the time-dependent matrices $L(t)$ and $H(t)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies heavily on the properties of Hardy spaces ($H^1$) and the assumption that the error bound $E_{R,y_0}[f]$ is finite.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first defining the LCHS framework and the optimization problem (Problem 5), then proving the existence and uniqueness of the optimal kernel function (Theorem 6), followed by rigorous analysis of the resulting cost scaling and the construction of a spectrally convergent quadrature rule (Theorem 8).

Abstract

Linear combination of Hamiltonian simulation (LCHS) provides an asymptotically optimal framework for the quantum simulation of linear non-unitary dynamics. The LCHS approach approximately implements the Peano--Baker propagator through an integral representation determined by a kernel function. We establish an LCHS approximation theorem for a broadened class of admissible kernel functions, defined by natural complex-analytic conditions. In the standard time-independent block-encoding access model, we explicitly construct, for each target error $\varepsilon$, the unique kernel function in this class minimizing the query-cost functional and show that the minimum of this functional has the asymptotic expansion $\tfrac{2\mathrm{e}}π\big(\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+o(1)\big)$ as ${\varepsilon\downarrow0}$. The provably optimal leading coefficient $2\mathrm{e}/π$ improves by a factor of approximately 2.1 on the coefficient obtained empirically by Low and Somma through numerical minimization over their family of optimally scaling kernel functions. We construct a spectrally convergent quadrature with explicit bounds on operator error and linear combination of unitaries (LCU) subnormalization, proving that this asymptotic improvement persists after discretization into a finite linear combination of unitaries suitable for implementation. Our results are based on Hardy space theory and convex analysis.

Summary. This paper proposes a novel, multi-layered quantum error correction protocol for molecular spin systems to achieve fault tolerance. It addresses the limitation of single-spin encoding by combining dephasing-suppressing units with a multi-qubit code to handle residual relaxation errors. The work demonstrates that this hybrid architecture significantly enhances logical error suppression in molecular quantum processors.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles the physical implementation challenge of QEC in solid-state or molecular platforms, providing a concrete, multi-layered theoretical blueprint for fault tolerance beyond simple decoherence mitigation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving fault-tolerant quantum error correction (QEC) in single molecules, specifically addressing residual off-diagonal errors (relaxation) after initial dephasing correction.

Main result. The authors propose a hybrid encoding scheme using multi-spin molecules, which successfully addresses residual relaxation errors by concatenating dephasing-tolerant units with a second-level multi-qubit code.

Method. The approach involves a two-stage error correction protocol: first, using embedded codes to suppress pure dephasing, and second, applying a multi-qubit code to handle the remaining off-diagonal relaxation errors.

Model / system. The system utilizes molecular spin complexes (MNMs) whose spin dynamics are modeled using Hamiltonians and master equations. The error analysis distinguishes between pure dephasing (diagonal) and relaxation (off-diagonal) errors.

Key observables. Logical error quantified by entanglement infidelity (Perr) and average fidelity (F), and the decay of normalized transferred coherence.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio between $T_2$ (dephasing time) and $T_1$ (relaxation time) is critical; the performance is shown to be limited by finite $T_1$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the secular approximation in the Redfield theory and the assumption that the residual noise channel can be effectively analyzed using Pauli twirling approximations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the error decay as a function of $T_2$, showing saturation to a plateau set by residual relaxation for finite $T_1$. Other figures detail the structure of error channels (Kraus operators) for relaxation-driven errors.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the need to move beyond pure dephasing correction. It then details the hybrid encoding strategy, combining dephasing-tolerant units with a multi-qubit code. Finally, it numerically demonstrates the error suppression potential of this multi-spin architecture.

Abstract

We propose multi-spin molecules as a viable architecture for fault-tolerant quantum computing. To this aim, we introduce a correction protocol handling both diagonal and off-diagonal errors, typically associated with dephasing and relaxation. The scheme is based on a hybrid encoding which combines dephasing-tolerant units suppressing the leading pure dephasing error into a multi-spin molecule implementing a multi-qubit code for residual off-diagonal errors. Our proposal leverages peculiar properties of molecular spins, i.e. the strong hierarchy between different errors and the possibility to engineer multi-spin molecules at the synthetic level. Moreover, it addresses the important issue of the loss of coherences in anharmonic systems subject to off-diagonal errors, which hampers their correction at the single spin level. Thanks to the huge suppression of dephasing by the first-level code, we numerically demonstrate the potential performance of this strategy even with a limited number of spins per logical unit.

Correlated Metals, Metamagnetism, and Orbital Nematic Order in moiré Materials with Neural Quantum States

Authors: Wei Zhang, Ataç İmamoğlu, Tao Shi, Eugene A. Demler, Ivan Morera
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03145v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 2/5 · Frenkel-Kontorova 1/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 1/5

Summary. This work uses advanced quantum simulation techniques (NQS/VMC) to map the phase diagram of interacting electrons in moiré lattices. It reveals that strong correlations fundamentally alter the insulating state, leading to spin-dependent transitions between metallic and molecularly insulating/nematic phases. This highlights the crucial role of spin polarization in determining the ground state of correlated 2D materials.

Why it may be interesting. The explicit inclusion of spin-dependent transitions and the emergence of orbital nematic order in a correlated electron context provides rich physics relevant to moiré heterostructures and strongly correlated materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the interplay between strong electron correlations, external periodic potentials, and spin polarization in two-dimensional electron systems.

Main result. Strong correlations can suppress the conventional band insulator, leading to a correlated metallic phase, with the transition being highly dependent on spin polarization, resulting in distinct molecular insulating and nematic crystalline states.

Method. The authors employed Neural Quantum States (NQS) optimized via Variational Monte Carlo to map out the ground-state phase diagram.

Model / system. The model is a continuum description of 2D electrons interacting via long-range Coulomb forces, subjected to a triangular periodic potential at filling factor $\nu=2$. The analysis is extended to include short-range interactions.

Key observables. Ground-state phase diagram, magnetization curves, metamagnetic transitions, density profiles, and structure factors.

Important parameters / regimes. Periodic potential strength ($V_0$), Zeeman field, and spin polarization ($\zeta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis focuses on the Zeeman effect and uses a continuum model approach to handle strong correlations.

Figures summary. Not specified.

Paper structure. The paper develops the phase diagram by analyzing the system's response to varying potential strengths and magnetic fields, contrasting the behavior of unpolarized versus fully polarized states.

Abstract

In this work, we investigate a continuum model of two-dimensional electrons interacting via long-range Coulomb forces and subject to a triangular periodic potential at filling factor $ν=2$. Using neural quantum states based on message-passing neural networks optimized with variational Monte Carlo, we determine the ground-state phase diagram as a function of periodic potential strength and Zeeman field. Although the noninteracting system is expected to form a conventional band insulator, we show that strong correlations can qualitatively reshape the insulating state and, in part of the phase diagram, suppress the band insulator in favor of a correlated metallic phase, with a first-order transition separating the two regimes. Moreover, the metal--insulator transition exhibits a strong dependence on spin polarization: the unpolarized system becomes insulating at substantially weaker periodic potentials through the formation of an $s$-wave molecular insulator, where opposite-spin electrons bind near each potential minimum, whereas the fully polarized system requires a significantly deeper potential and instead forms a symmetry-broken orbital nematic molecular crystal. This spin-dependent molecular transition gives rise to discontinuous magnetization curves, metamagnetic transitions, and several multicritical points in the phase diagram, together with clear signatures in the density profiles, real-space correlation functions, and structure factors. To further elucidate the role of the long-range character of the Coulomb interaction, we also study short-range repulsive interactions, which can be realized with ultracold-atom platforms. We find that the qualitative structure of the many-body phase diagram remains unchanged, demonstrating the robustness of our results across different interaction models.

Fluctuation-induced magnetoresistance in graphene Hall bars at charge neutrality

Authors: Anna Grigoreva, Alex Levchenko, Eleftherios Kirkinis, Anton Andreev
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.02326v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 2/5 · methods for driven-dissipative 2/5

Summary. This work provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for fluctuation-induced magnetoresistance in graphene at charge neutrality. By treating the electron liquid hydrodynamically, the authors derive analytic expressions showing that MR results from the coupling between charge transport and thermal hydrodynamic fluctuations. The results highlight the strong dependence of this effect on the physical geometry of the graphene sample.

Why it may be interesting. The detailed treatment of non-equilibrium, fluctuation-driven transport phenomena in a 2D Dirac material provides advanced insights into mesoscopic transport physics, relevant for understanding quantum noise effects in solid-state systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a quantitative theory to explain the fluctuation-induced magnetoresistance (MR) observed at the charge neutrality point in graphene Hall bars.

Main result. The MR is shown to arise from the coupling between charge transport and hydrodynamic flow fluctuations, yielding functional dependencies sensitive to the Hall bar geometry and magnetic field strength.

Method. The analysis employs the hydrodynamic approximation, solving coupled Langevin-Navier-Stokes equations for density and velocity fluctuations in Fourier space.

Model / system. The system is graphene, modeled as an electron liquid exhibiting hydrodynamic behavior when electron-electron collision rates exceed momentum relaxation rates. The geometry is a Hall bar of width W and length L.

Key observables. Magnetoresistance (MR), fluctuation conductivity ($\sigma_{fl}$), and the dependence of MR on the magnetic field $H$ and geometry.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio of collision rates ($ au_{ee}^{-1} > \gamma_p$), the magnetic length ($\ell_H$), the aspect ratio ($W/L$), and the disorder strength ($\ell_G$).

Assumptions / limitations. The theory relies on the hydrodynamic regime ($ au_{ee}^{-1} > \gamma_p$) and linear response theory applied to fluctuation sources (Johnson-Nyquist noise).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the charge-density fluctuations and induced hydrodynamic flow in the Hall bar setup, showing $\sigma_{fl}(H)$ dependence on geometry and disorder.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the hydrodynamic framework and the origin of the advection term in the current, to solving the linearized fluctuation equations in Fourier space, and finally deriving asymptotic forms for $\sigma_{fl}$ in finite geometries.

Abstract

In the temperature interval $T\sim 50 - 100 K$, the rate of electron-electron collisions in graphene devices may exceed the momentum relaxation rate due to disorder and electron-phonon scattering. In this regime, the motion of the electron liquid may be described by the hydrodynamic equations. In the hydrodynamic approximation, at the charge neutrality point the electric current is decoupled from the hydrodynamic flow, and the conductivity of the system is equal to the intrinsic conductivity of the electron liquid, $σ_0$. It was recently shown that the coupling of charge transport and hydrodynamic flow induced by the thermal fluctuations enhances the macroscopic conductivity of the system and results in very strong positive magnetoresistance (MR) at relatively weak fields. Here we develop a quantitative theory of the fluctuation mechanism of MR at charge neutrality and obtain analytic expressions for MR in graphene Hall bars. The functional dependence of MR on the magnetic field $H$ is sensitive to the Hall bar geometry and orientation relative to the current. We also account for the effect of momentum relaxation on MR.

Optimizing quantum error correction through error attribution

Summary. This paper introduces an error attribution scheme to diagnose which physical noise sources most critically impact the logical error rate in quantum error correction codes. By quantifying the sensitivity of the logical failure rate to individual noise components, the authors identify 'hotspots' in the error circuit. Targeting noise reduction efforts on these few critical components yields substantial, non-trivial improvements in QEC performance compared to random efforts.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a practical, data-driven optimization tool for QEC hardware design, moving beyond simple benchmarking to guide resource allocation for maximum performance gain.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the limitation of using the aggregate logical error rate in Quantum Error Correction (QEC) by developing a method to pinpoint which specific physical noise components contribute most significantly to overall failure.

Main result. Targeted noise reduction on the top 5%-7% of most sensitive physical components can reduce the logical error rate by 14%-25%, significantly outperforming interventions on the same number of randomly selected components.

Method. An error attribution scheme is introduced to calculate the sensitivity of the logical error rate to individual noise strengths, using joint estimation from Monte Carlo samples to identify 'hotspots' in the error circuit.

Model / system. The study applies this framework to simulate QEC circuits, specifically using rotated surface codes and high-rate lifted product codes, which are modeled under a stochastic error framework.

Key observables. Logical error rate (P_L), component sensitivities (S_alpha), and linearized marginal risk (R_alpha).

Important parameters / regimes. Noise strengths (lambda), code distances (d=3, 5, 7), and the fraction of components targeted for intervention (e.g., 5%-7%).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes mutual independence of elementary noise components and relies on a linearized approximation of the marginal risk.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the error attribution framework, showing sensitivity maps that highlight 'hotspots' (critical components) and comparing the reduction in P_L achieved by targeting these hotspots versus random selection.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the error attribution framework and its estimators. It then applies this to surface codes, demonstrating that hotspots are pronounced. Finally, it quantifies the performance gains by comparing targeted intervention against random intervention across various code sizes.

Abstract

Logical error rate is a standard benchmark for quantum error correction, providing an aggregate measure of the overall effect of physical noise. In actual QEC architectures, errors at different circuit locations can have markedly different effects on logical failure even when their probabilities are equal. Here we introduce an error attribution scheme to quantitatively characterize the influence of individual circuit components on the logical error rate and use this refined information to guide targeted intervention and design for improved QEC performance. Our attribution estimator computes all component sensitivities jointly from the same Monte Carlo samples used to estimate the logical error rate. The resulting sensitivity maps identify hotspots where targeted noise reduction can most effectively improve logical performance. We test the scheme in circuit simulations of rotated surface code memories and high-rate lifted product codes: halving the noise strength on about 5\%-7\% of physical components selected by sensitivity reduces logical error rates by approximately 15\%-25\%, roughly twice the mean reduction from the same intervention on an equal number of randomly selected components. By connecting performance benchmarking to targeted optimization, our scheme provides a practical strategy for improving quantum error correction with limited resources.

Robust constrained optimization of nonequilibrium Casimir repulsion in a biased-semiconductor cavity

Summary. This work develops a robust optimization framework to maximize the repulsive Casimir-Lifshitz force generated in a biased semiconductor cavity. By treating the problem as a constrained optimization under fabrication and drive uncertainties, the authors determine optimal cavity parameters. The results establish a design bound for cavity-enhanced repulsion, demonstrating its superiority over simple far-field predictions.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a quantitative, robust design methodology for generating non-equilibrium forces, bridging quantum electrodynamics (Casimir effect) with semiconductor device physics and optimization theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of designing a semiconductor cavity to generate a robust, repulsive Casimir-Lifshitz force that remains strong despite uncertainties in fabrication gaps and external drive parameters.

Main result. The optimized design sustains a worst-case outward pressure of $2.257 ext{ mPa}$ at $300 ext{ K}$, which exceeds the corresponding far-field radiation-pressure plateau by a measurable amount.

Method. The core method is formulating the problem as a robust constrained optimization, maximizing the minimum outward pressure across a defined uncertainty set.

Model / system. The system is a planar structure consisting of two Perfect Electric Conductors (PECs) flanking a central, biased Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) semiconductor slab, creating a cavity.

Key observables. Outward outward pressure ($P_L, P_R$), which is decomposed into equilibrium and excess nonequilibrium contributions.

Important parameters / regimes. Gap uncertainties ($\pm 10 ext{ nm}$), drive uncertainty ($\pm 0.005$), and the operating temperature ($300 ext{ K}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The model assumes the semiconductor response is passive and that the photon chemical potential ($\mu_\gamma$) is spatially uniform over the active regions.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the geometry (PEC-vacuum-semiconductor-vacuum-PEC) and show energy-resolved pressure components, while captions detail the selection of slab thickness based on reduction-validity indicators.

Paper structure. The paper builds the theory by decomposing the total pressure into equilibrium and nonequilibrium parts, develops the robust optimization framework, applies the optimization to find optimal gap sizes, and validates the reduced model against full finite-slab scattering calculations.

Abstract

Nonequilibrium electromagnetic fluctuations offer a route to repulsive Casimir--Lifshitz forces. However, designs optimized only at nominal conditions may be fragile. We formulate repulsion in a planar PEC--vacuum--biased-GaAs--vacuum--PEC stack as a robust constrained optimization problem. The smaller of the outward pressures on the two conductors is maximized under fabrication and drive uncertainties. In the optically thick-slab limit, we prove that identical boundaries admit a symmetric optimizer, so equality of the optimal gaps emerges from the model rather than being imposed. At $300~\mathrm{K}$, with gap uncertainty of $\pm10~\mathrm{nm}$ and drive uncertainty of $\pm0.005$, the optimized design sustains a worst-case outward pressure of $2.257~\mathrm{mPa}$ and exceeds the corresponding far-field radiation-pressure plateau by $0.1812~\mathrm{mPa}$. At the nominal and active worst-case points, a full finite-slab scattering calculation agrees with the reduced model to within $1.60\times10^{-3}\%$. These results provide a design bound for cavity-enhanced nonequilibrium Casimir--Lifshitz repulsion and show how robust optimization can distinguish cavity enhancement from ordinary far-field radiation pressure.

SMP: A General Hyperedge-Based Framework for Circuit-Level Quantum Error Correction

Summary. This paper introduces Syndrome Motif Projection (SMP), a novel, lightweight framework designed to incorporate complex hyperedge fault information into existing, scalable matching-based quantum error correction decoders. By extracting local syndrome motifs, SMP enhances the ability to detect and correct correlated errors in surface and color codes. The results demonstrate that SMP provides a highly transferable method to boost fault tolerance in real-time quantum computation architectures.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on QEC, the development of lightweight, information-theoretic preprocessing steps (like motif extraction) that bridge complex physical phenomena (hyperedge faults) into computationally tractable graph structures is relevant to designing efficient control protocols for open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge addressed is accurately decoding hyperedge faults in circuit-level quantum error correction (QEC), as existing fast decoders are limited by pairwise representations.

Main result. The proposed Syndrome Motif Projection (SMP) framework significantly improves error correction performance across surface and color codes, achieving reductions in logical failure probabilities up to 77.8% and 35.5% compared to baselines.

Method. SMP is a lightweight front-end that extracts characteristic local syndrome motifs directly from measured syndromes, allowing this higher-order fault information to be incorporated into existing, scalable matching-based decoding algorithms.

Model / system. The work focuses on circuit-level QEC using stabilizer measurements on physical systems modeled by surface codes and color codes, aiming for fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Key observables. Logical failure probabilities ($P_L$), performance improvement over belief matching, and the detection of specific syndrome motifs.

Important parameters / regimes. Physical error probability ($p$), code distance ($d$), and the structure of the fault (edge vs. hyperedge).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the local syndrome motif provides sufficient evidence to effectively update the weights in the matching backend, allowing for a linear-complexity preprocessing step.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of hyperedges and the resulting syndrome motifs from circuit-level faults in a surface code patch. Other figures compare the logical failure probabilities of various decoding methods.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the SMP framework by defining the problem of hyperedge faults, details the mechanism of motif extraction and weight interpolation ($\eta_h$), and validates the approach by demonstrating performance improvements across surface codes, color codes, and logical gate circuits.

Abstract

Hyperedge faults remain a major challenge in circuit-level quantum error correction. Accurately exploiting hyperedge correlations often requires substantial computational resources, which complicates real-time decoding. Matching-based decoders are fast and scalable, but their pairwise graph representation limits the use of higher-order fault information. Here, we introduce Syndrome Motif Projection (SMP), a general framework for incorporating hyperedge information into matching-based decoding. The key idea is that a hyperedge fault produces a characteristic local syndrome motif. Observing such a motif provides evidence for the corresponding fault. SMP extracts this information directly from the measured syndrome with linear-complexity preprocessing while preserving the matching backend. It can therefore serve as a lightweight front end to existing scalable and real-time matching decoders. We demonstrate SMP across surface code memories, color code memories, and logical gate circuits. On Google's Willow experimental surface code data, SMP improves both standard and correlated matching and outperforms the tested belief matching baseline. For color code memories, SMP combined with Chromobius reduces logical failure probabilities by up to 77.8\%. For logical gate circuits, SMP reduces the failure probability of a six-CNOT circuit by 35.5\% relative to logical observable matching while achieving performance comparable to iterative belief matching. These results demonstrate that SMP provides a lightweight and transferable approach to hyperedge decoding, with a clear path toward integration into scalable real-time decoding architectures for fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Bogoliubov self-consistent $GW$ and second-order Green's function methods for attractive fermionic interactions

Authors: Mauricio Rodr{í}guez-Mayorga, Johannes Tölle, Pierre-François Loos
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03357v1

Summary. This work investigates advanced many-body techniques, specifically Bogoliubov self-consistent GW and second-order Green's function methods, to treat attractive fermionic interactions. By benchmarking these methods against the exact Full Configuration Interaction result using the attractive Hubbard model, the authors demonstrate that the GF2 approach is superior to GW, particularly as the attractive interaction strength increases.

Why it may be interesting. The comparison between GW and GF2 methods for strongly correlated systems provides valuable insight into the relative accuracy of different many-body perturbation theories when dealing with pairing mechanisms relevant to superconductivity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The central challenge is accurately describing fermionic systems with attractive interactions, such as superconductors and atomic nuclei, beyond the mean-field level.

Main result. The second-order Green's function method (Bog. scGF2) was found to provide a more accurate description of correlation energy than the fully self-consistent GW approach (Bog. scGW).

Method. The authors compare Bogoliubov fully self-consistent GW (Bog. scGW) and second-order Green's function (Bog. scGF2) methods, implemented in a unified imaginary-time and imaginary-frequency framework.

Model / system. The study uses the attractive Hubbard model as a benchmark system to test the theoretical methods. The formalism builds upon the Hartree–Fock–Bogoliubov (HFB) approach, which is extended to include dynamical correlation effects.

Key observables. Total energies, anomalous density ($\kappa$), and the generalized (Gorkov) density matrix ($\mathbf{R}$).

Important parameters / regimes. The strength of the attractive interaction, which is varied to test the stability and accuracy of the methods.

Assumptions / limitations. The HFB formalism is used as the starting point, which neglects dynamical correlation effects. The GW and GF2 methods are approximations used to recover these missing correlation terms.

Paper structure. The paper develops the theoretical framework by first establishing the HFB ground state, then introducing the GW and GF2 corrections, and finally comparing their quantitative results against the benchmark FCI calculation.

Abstract

Describing fermionic systems with attractive interactions, such as superconductors and atomic nuclei, beyond the mean-field level remains a central challenge. In this work, we investigate the performance of Bogoliubov fully self-consistent $GW$ (Bog.~sc$GW$) and second-order Green's function (Bog.~scGF2) methods, implemented within a unified imaginary-time and imaginary-frequency framework. Using the attractive Hubbard model as a benchmark against full configuration interaction (FCI), we find that Bog. scGF2 provides a more accurate description than Bog.~sc$GW$. In particular, Bog.~sc$GW$ systematically overestimates the correlation energy, with errors increasing rapidly as the attractive interaction strength grows. Abstract

In our previous paper on the matter, we proved that real quantum theory (RQT) cannot be experimentally falsified without the experiment falsifying quantum theory (QT) as well. Our result is in direct contradiction to the central claim of an article by Renou et al., published in Nature [600, 625--629 (2021)], reporting a bilocal network experiment that could falsify real quantum theory without falsifying quantum theory. The purpose of this note is to address several seemingly natural attempts to recover the experimental conclusion of Renou et al. We first explain how these results, although based on nonlocality experiments, are far from a Bell-like level of proof. Then we explain why the proposed experiment requires an experimentally untestable premise about independent preparations necessarily being represented as product states. Finally, we discuss some concerns that lie outside the scope of both results.

Efficient Block Encoding of Structured Hamiltonians by Separating Where and What

Authors: Alessandro Summer, François Jamet
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03686v1

Summary. This paper details a method to efficiently encode structured Hamiltonians for quantum simulation by separating the spatial support of an interaction from the local operation applied. This 'permute-act-unpermute' circuit structure significantly reduces the required non-Clifford gate count (T gates) by changing the cost scaling from multiplicative to additive. The results show substantial cost reductions for models like the Heisenberg ring, advancing the practical simulation of complex quantum materials.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides concrete, resource-aware quantum circuit techniques for simulating complex Hamiltonians relevant to strongly correlated materials, directly impacting the feasibility of quantum simulation algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to reduce the non-Clifford gate cost associated with fault-tolerant block encodings of structured Hamiltonians.

Main result. By separating the action ('what') from the support geometry ('where') using permute-act-unpermute circuits, the T-gate count scales additively with system size rather than multiplicatively with the number of terms.

Method. The authors construct specialized quantum circuits utilizing permute-act-unpermute sequences and introduce a 'bridged CSWAP' gate to optimize the gate count, especially for Pauli terms.

Key observables. T count (non-Clifford cost), CSWAP count, and the scaling factor of the cost relative to system size N.

Important parameters / regimes. Support geometry (determining the CSWAP density S), system size N, and the number of impurities/orbitals ($n_{ ext{imp}}$).

The paper develops the theory by first establishing the permute-act-unpermute framework, then optimizing the permutation cost based on support geometry, and finally applying this to specific models like the Heisenberg ring and Anderson impurity model to quantify the cost reduction.

Abstract

Fault-tolerant block encodings of structured Hamiltonians can reduce their non-Clifford cost in SELECT by selecting where an operator acts separately from what is applied. We construct permute-act-unpermute circuits whose CSWAP networks exploit the support geometry: a selected support is brought to a fixed target register, a shared local circuit acts there, and the permutation is undone. For supports of fixed size, the $T$ count of SELECT then grows with the system size rather than with the number of terms, without requiring translational symmetry or factorised coefficients. For two-site supports, we establish and attain the minimum CSWAP count within address-controlled networks of site transpositions. Compiled for Pauli terms, these circuits use $8SN+O(\log N)$ $T$ gates with $O(\log N)$ permutation work qubits, where $N$ is the number of system qubits and $S$ is determined by the support geometry, with $S=1$ for nearest-neighbour interactions and $S=3/2$ for all-to-all pairs. We also introduce a bridged CSWAP that retains and repairs a temporary AND across the target action. When the repair is Clifford, it halves the $T$ count of a matched forward-inverse CSWAP pair at the cost of one retained work qubit. For a Heisenberg ring, the complete block-encoding query reduces the $T$ count by a factor approaching 3 as the system size grows. For a five-orbital Anderson impurity model, which combines nearest-neighbour and all-to-all support geometries, the reduction is about 1.7 at large bath sizes. Both comparisons use the lowest-cost compiled baselines considered, at unchanged block-encoding normalisation and comparable peak ancilla counts.
