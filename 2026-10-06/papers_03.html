Symmetry-preserving quantum compilation

Authors: Maryam Mudassar, Zhi-Yuan Wei, Alexander Schuckert, Michael J. Lawler, Michael J. Gullans, Daniel Gottesman
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03624v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a novel, fault-tolerant compilation framework designed to simulate quantum dynamics while rigorously preserving continuous symmetries, such as SU(2). By circumventing theoretical obstructions using measurement and feed-forward, the method significantly reduces the required gate resources (T-count) compared to standard synthesis techniques. This has major implications for accurately simulating complex models like the Heisenberg chain and Fermi-Hubbard model.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses the resource scaling and accuracy limitations of quantum simulation for strongly correlated systems by ensuring that the computational error does not corrupt fundamental physical symmetries like SU(2).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is developing fault-tolerant quantum compilation methods that can accurately simulate time evolutions while strictly preserving underlying continuous symmetries.

Main result. The proposed symmetry-preserving compilation framework successfully reproduces expected physical exponents (like the transport exponent) for the 1D Heisenberg model at lower gate counts compared to standard, symmetry-agnostic synthesis methods.

Method. The authors develop a framework that circumvents number-theoretic obstructions by using measurement and feed-forward techniques within a Linear-Combination-of-Unitaries (LCU) protocol to maintain exact symmetry preservation.

Model / system. The primary testbed is the one-dimensional isotropic Heisenberg model, whose Hamiltonian possesses a global SU(2) rotational symmetry. The framework is also extended to lattice gauge theories and fermionic models like the Fermi-Hubbard model.

Key observables. Transport exponent (KPZ exponent), T-count (gate resource cost), and final-state fidelity.

Important parameters / regimes. The required T-count, the simulation time $t$, and the error tolerance $\epsilon$.

Assumptions / limitations. The simulation relies on approximating the time evolution $U(t) = e^{-iHt}$ via Trotterization, which is then compiled into the discrete Clifford+T gate set.

Figures summary. Not specified, but the notes reference comparisons of gate costs and fidelity between different synthesis methods.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from establishing the need for symmetry preservation in Trotterization, characterizing the number-theoretic obstruction, developing the LCU construction, benchmarking the method on the Heisenberg model, and finally extending the results to gauge and fermionic symmetries.

Abstract

Fault-tolerant quantum simulation requires compiling symmetry-preserving time evolutions into discrete gate sets. Standard Clifford+T synthesis can break continuous symmetries even when the target evolution preserves them, causing compiled simulations to lose essential features of the original system. We develop a fault-tolerant compilation framework that preserves SU(2) symmetry exactly. We characterize the number-theoretic obstruction preventing symmetry-preserving unitary Clifford+T circuits from approximating arbitrary SU(2)-symmetric evolutions and circumvent it using measurement and feed-forward. The resulting synthesis confines compilation errors to the symmetry-preserving operator algebra rather than introducing symmetry-breaking perturbations. We demonstrate the algorithm by simulating transport in the one-dimensional Heisenberg model. At a lower matched T-count, for instance, 54 T gates per two-site Trotter step, standard synthesis yields an incorrect transport exponent, whereas our symmetry-preserving compilation reproduces the expected exponent for the same expected T-count. If instead each approach is allocated whatever resources are necessary to estimate this exponent to the same accuracy, our construction reduces the T-count by approximately a factor of five. We further extend the framework to gauge symmetries in lattice gauge theories and to particle-number and spin symmetries in fermionic models relevant to quantum chemistry and the Fermi-Hubbard model. These results show that symmetry-preserving fault-tolerant compilation can qualitatively change the effects of synthesis error while substantially reducing the resources required for quantum simulation.

Consistent perturbation expansions in screened interactions

Authors: Abdallah Arthur El Sahili, Francesco Sottile, Lucia Reining
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03569v1

Summary. This paper addresses the theoretical inconsistency plaguing many-body perturbation theory by developing systematic, order-by-order expansions for electronic structure calculations. By focusing on a consistent framework derived from screened interactions, the authors propose that using ingredients derived from the Kohn-Sham system offers a robust path toward accurate energy and spectral predictions.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on systematic, rigorous diagrammatic expansions and the need for consistent approximations in correlated electron systems directly relates to advanced many-body physics techniques used in AMO and quantum optics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Many-body perturbation theory (MBPT) suffers from inconsistent results because different approximations lead to varying calculations for spectroscopic quantities and total energy.

Main result. The authors propose a consistent framework by deriving rigorous perturbation expansions, highlighting that the expansion based on exact Kohn-Sham ingredients is a promising route for accurate results.

Method. The work involves deriving systematic, order-by-order expansions of the Dyson equation using screened Coulomb interactions ($W$) up to the second order.

Model / system. The general many-body problem in condensed matter physics is studied, with the symmetric Hubbard dimer used as a specific model system for illustration.

Key observables. One-body Green's function ($G$), self-energy ($\Sigma$), total energy ($E_0$), and spectral functions ($A(\omega)$).

Important parameters / regimes. Correlation strength, the screened interaction $W$, and the order of the perturbation expansion (up to second order).

Assumptions / limitations. The authors focus on defining consistency by using a self-contained diagrammatic series, explicitly avoiding complex issues like self-consistency loops or Bethe-Salpeter equation approximations.

Figures summary. Not specified.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the Dyson equation, introduces screening via $W$, develops the perturbation theory for the self-energy ($\Sigma^{(1)}, \Sigma^{(2)}$), and discusses methods for calculating $G$ from $\Sigma$ to ensure theoretical consistency.

Abstract

Many-body perturbation theory is a powerful framework to calculate spectroscopic quantities, as well as the total energy. However, the field suffers from the fact that different flavors of approximations lead to a wide range of results. We present several in principle exact expansions and discuss how to use them consistently. We concentrate on the first two orders in the respective screened Coulomb interaction. Of special interest is a consistent expansion around the Kohn-Sham system. We illustrate the performance of the various choices using the symmetric Hubbard dimer over a wide range of correlation strength. In particular, the expansion based on exact Kohn-Sham ingredients stands out as a promising route towards good energies and good spectra.

Hamiltonian Eigenvalue Transformation by Tridiagonal Gadgets

Authors: Arthur Braida, Joseph Cunningham, Jérémie Roland
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03295v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · quantum measurements 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper introduces a novel 'gadget' construction using ancilla qubits attached to a local Hamiltonian $H$ to simulate non-local operations like $P(H)$. By analyzing the unique, analytic eigenvalues of these gadgets, the authors show they can synthesize any polynomial or filter specific eigenstates with controlled error decay. This offers a significant resource advantage over existing circuit-based methods.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, resource-efficient alternative to standard quantum signal processing techniques for implementing complex functions of local Hamiltonians, directly impacting the feasibility of quantum simulation and quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is realizing a polynomial function $P(H)$ of a local Hamiltonian $H$ using a single, time-independent, local Hamiltonian operator, since $P(H)$ is generally non-local.

Main result. The authors demonstrate a gadget construction using ancilla qubits that synthesizes any polynomial $P(H)$ or filters a marked eigenstate with controlled error decay, achieving this with improved locality compared to standard circuit models.

Method. The method involves attaching short, structured chains of ancilla qubits (tridiagonal gadgets) to the input Hamiltonian $H$. The polynomial is synthesized by taking a weighted sum of the unique, analytic eigenvalues derived from these chains.

Model / system. The model is an analog quantum device implementing a local Hamiltonian $H$. The core computational element is the 'gadget,' which is a short chain of ancilla qubits whose isolated eigenvalue $\lambda_0(x)$ is an analytic function of the input $x$ (representing $H$).

Key observables. The isolated eigenvalue $\lambda_0(x)$ of the gadget, which is an analytic function of the input $H$. The fidelity of the state filter or the accuracy of the polynomial reproduction are key diagnostics.

Important parameters / regimes. The degree of the polynomial ($\ell$), the locality of the input Hamiltonian ($H$), the required accuracy ($\epsilon$), and the number of ancilla qubits ($O(\ell^2)$).

Assumptions / limitations. The construction relies on the existence of a unique, analytic eigenvalue branch $\lambda_0(x)$ for the gadget, and the ability to synthesize the polynomial via a weighted sum of these branches.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the structure of the even gadget $E_{2m}(x)$ for $m=3$. Table 1 compares the resource costs (ancillas vs. depth) of the gadget approach versus Quantum Singular Value Transformation (QSVT).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the problem of non-locality, introduces the gadget gadget based on eigenvalue analysis (Theorem A), proves the ability to synthesize any polynomial (Theorem B), and details a superior, iterative method for state filtering (Theorem C).

Abstract

An analog device implements a local Hamiltonian H, but a polynomial P (H) is in general not local, so the device cannot implement it. We carry P (H), to any prescribed accuracy, as the action of a single time-independent local Hamiltonian on an explicitly described invariant subspace. Short chains of ancilla qubits are attached to H. A chain of 2m sites has a unique isolated eigenvalue that is an analytic function of the input vanishing to order exactly 2m, because the input must cross the chain and come back before it can shift the energy at the far end. Chains of different lengths therefore form a triangular family, and a weighted sum of them reproduces a prescribed polynomial term by term. Even chains give the even part and odd chains the odd part, so any polynomial is reached. We prove uniqueness, analyticity and coefficient bounds uniform in the chain length for inputs H of norm below any r < 1. Where the circuit model pays degree 2l in 2l sequential oracle calls, the result here is one Hamiltonian of locality two more than that of H, which pays the degree in O(l^2 + log2^(1/eps)) ancillas and in energy scale. As an application we filter a marked eigenstate of a local Hamiltonian. Composing a synthesised square along the Chebyshev doubling identity works, but its energy scale grows quasi-polynomially with the degree. Iterating instead the exact eigenvalue branch of the two-site chain k times gives a filter on k + 1 ancilla qubits whose error decays geometrically in k, at an energy scale polynomial in the inverse passband width and independent of k, the locality growing by one per stage. In adiabatic optimisation with a rank-one driver, the construction replaces that non-local driver, by a Hamiltonian with O(log n) ancillas and O(log n)- body terms, at an energy scale polynomial in n. Simulations including every ancilla reproduce the spectrum of the ideal algorithm.

Quantum approaches for particle-in-cell codes

Summary. This paper presents a novel approach to simulating complex plasma physics by replacing traditional Particle-in-Cell codes with tensor-network methods, specifically using Matrix Product States. This allows for solving the governing equations in high-dimensional phase space while mitigating issues like statistical noise and improving computational scaling. The framework is extended to model external drivers, such as injected laser fields, marking a significant step toward quantum-inspired plasma simulation.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly applies advanced tensor-network techniques, which are central to quantum information and many-body physics, to a classical, non-equilibrium field theory problem (plasma dynamics). The successful adaptation of MPS to handle complex field coupling is a significant methodological advance.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is solving the high-dimensional, nonlinear partial differential equations governing plasma physics, which is computationally intensive using standard methods.

Main result. The authors successfully demonstrated that tensor-network methods can model complex plasma phenomena, such as instabilities and laser-plasma interactions, while maintaining favorable computational scaling.

Method. They propose using tensor-network representations, specifically Matrix Product States (MPS), to discretize the full phase space grid, allowing for the simulation of the Vlasov equation.

Model / system. The model is plasma dynamics governed by the Vlasov-Maxwell kinetic equations. The simulation involves tracking particle distributions in phase space and coupling this to electromagnetic field evolution.

Key observables. Electron distribution snapshots in phase space, current density, and the propagation of electric field components ($E_y$).

Important parameters / regimes. The simulation involves parameters like Debye length constraints, time steps ($\Delta t = 0.0014$), and bond dimensions ($\chi$) required for MPS accuracy.

Assumptions / limitations. The key assumption is that the physics can be accurately captured by the tensor-network representation, and the method relies on careful boundary condition handling when injecting external fields (TFSF approach).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison of time complexity (PIC vs. Tensor Network), the MPS encoding structure, snapshots of electron distributions during instabilities, convergence tests for wave problems, and the setup for laser field injection.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the computational bottleneck of plasma simulations using PIC codes. It then introduces tensor-networks as a solution, detailing the MPS encoding of the Vlasov equation. Finally, it extends this framework to incorporate external electromagnetic fields using the Total Field Scattered Field (TFSF) approach for advanced simulations.

Abstract

Solving the high-dimensional, nonlinear partial differential equations that govern the rich physics of plasmas remains a major computational challenge. In fluid dynamics, quantum-inspired methods based on tensor-network techniques have recently emerged as a promising new paradigm, offering large degrees of data compression on conventional hardware and a natural pathway towards solving exponentially large problems on quantum computers. Early studies suggest that tensor-network methods may also help overcome key bottlenecks in computational plasma dynamics, particularly for industry-relevant scenarios where conventional simulations remain costly. In this work, we present recent progress in developing tensor-network methods compared to standard particle-in-cell codes and discuss their capability to overcome the bottlenecks of conventional approaches. We further extend the tensor-network formulation to support the insertion of electromagnetic fields at arbitrary spatial locations. This capability is demonstrated through the injection of a laser field to accelerate a plasma, providing a key step towards tensor-network-based simulation of externally driven plasma systems.

The exact LCU sampling overhead of collective diagonal unitaries: resonances and a continued-fraction dichotomy

Authors: Zhihui Wang, Sujit Roy, Manil Maskey, Rahul Ramachandran
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03595v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Tavis-Cummings & cavity-many-emitter 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work rigorously determines the minimum resource overhead required to simulate quadratic collective phase gates, such as those found in Kerr nonlinear optics or spin squeezing. The cost is found to be dictated by the arithmetic nature of the phase angle $\gamma/\pi$, yielding exact integer costs at rational angles and polynomial scaling otherwise. This establishes a fundamental link between quantum simulation complexity and number theory.

Why it may be interesting. It provides deep mathematical insights into the resource cost of simulating fundamental quantum dynamics (like Kerr nonlinearities) by connecting it to number theory (Diophantine approximation) and quantum resource theory.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper determines the minimal sampling overhead required to implement a specific quadratic collective phase unitary, $e^{-i\gamma K^2}$, using a Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU) sampled from a restricted set of gates.

Main result. The minimal overhead depends critically on the ratio $\gamma/\pi$: it is exactly $q$ for rational angles $\pi p/q$ (resonances), $\Theta(n)$ if $\gamma/\pi$ is badly approximable, and can be exponential if symmetry is broken.

Method. The analysis uses techniques from harmonic analysis and number theory, framing the problem as an atomic-norm minimization problem dual to a sup-norm bound on trigonometric polynomials.

Model / system. The target unitary is the quadratic collective phase $e^{-i\gamma K^2}$, which models physical processes like the Kerr phase in bosonic modes, the one-axis-twisting gate in spin squeezing, and the penalty layer in quantum optimization.

Key observables. Sampling overhead ($\Gamma$), the angle parameter ($\gamma$), and the register size ($n$).

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio $\gamma/\pi$, which dictates the cost structure via continued fraction analysis (rational vs. badly approximable).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the target unitary can be sampled as an LCU from a specific set of implementable single-qubit rotation layers.

Figures summary. The notes mention Figure 1 classifying $\Gamma_{\min}$ vs. $n$ and $\gamma$ into resonances, plateaus, and the discrete-Fourier bound.

Paper structure. The paper develops by establishing the cost as a norm (Theorem A), analyzing specific resonant cases (Theorem B), establishing the general scaling behavior (dichotomy in Theorem C), and finally providing a master formula for the deep-resonance regime (Theorem D).

Abstract

We consider the cost of implementing the quadratic collective phase $e^{-iγK^2}$, with $K$ a collective observable of evenly spaced spectrum, such as the permutation-symmetric Hamming weight. This gate is the cardinality-penalty layer of constrained quantum optimization, the one-axis-twisting gate of spin squeezing and the Kerr phase of a bosonic mode. Instead of two-qubit gates, it can be sampled as a linear combination of single-qubit rotation layers (an LCU) at an overhead $Γ$. We determine the minimal overhead. Ancilla-free sampling of the layers reproduces the target's outcome probabilities up to this factor, and no smaller factor works for every input; when $K$ is permutation symmetric, arbitrary single-qubit gates do no better. The minimal overhead's growth with the register size $n$ is set by the continued fraction of $γ/π$: rational angles $πp/q$ in lowest terms cost exactly $Γ=q$ for every $n\ge2q-2$; the cost is $Θ(n)$ if and only if $γ/π$ is badly approximable; and a master formula, proved two-sided near low-denominator rationals, reads it off the nearest one. Without the symmetry the cost over single-qubit gates can be exponential: for the binary-weighted $K=\sum_j2^jx_j$ the squaring phase of Fourier arithmetic on $m$ qubits costs at least $2^{0.557m-O(1)}$, beyond the $2^{m/2}$ ceiling of every cut bound, by a new certificate-lifting argument. The Fourier-based LCU of Carrera Vazquez, Egger and Woerner (2026), which guarantees $Γ\le n+1$, is optimal up to a constant factor at typical angles at fixed $n$; at rational angles the unique optimizer is the fractional-revival decomposition of Kerr optics, a recipe independent of $n$. In bosonic simulation, the Kerr-gate phase-shifter decomposition of Upreti, Quesada and Chabaud (2026) is the unique optimum over phase shifters, and a finite-cost one exists only at rational $γ/π$.

Summary. This paper proves that topological insulating phases can be engineered on arbitrary complex networks, moving beyond simple periodic lattices. By developing a sophisticated design framework, the authors show that network structure, especially small-world characteristics, enhances topological robustness and directional transport. This establishes network complexity as a key resource for realizing advanced quantum electronic functionalities.

Why it may be interesting. The focus on network topology and disorder robustness connects directly to understanding complex quantum systems, which is relevant for designing quantum devices or modeling quantum information flow in structured media.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The research aims to generalize the concept of topological insulators beyond simple periodic lattices to complex, arbitrary network structures.

Main result. The study demonstrates that complex networks, particularly small-world architectures, can host robust topological insulating phases, with network complexity acting as a resource for high-capacity and reconfigurable transport.

Method. A gradient-based, conflict-free multi-objective inverse-design framework is developed to tailor hopping phases and node positions to achieve desired topological invariants.

Model / system. The system models topological insulators on complex graphs, including high-degree Hofstadter lattices, regular, small-world, and random networks, governed by a generalized magnetic graph Hamiltonian.

Key observables. Local Chern index ($C_L$), local gap ($\mu_L$), chirality ($\eta$), and port-to-port transmission matrices ($T_{pq}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Node degree ($k$), rewiring parameter ($ ho$), and energy ($E$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on 2D embedding of abstract networks and uses spectral localizers and surrogate models for optimization.

Paper structure. The paper systematically develops a design framework, applies it to various network types (Hofstadter, small-world, random), and analyzes the resulting topological invariants, robustness against disorder, and directional transport properties.

Abstract

The landscape of topological insulators has expanded beyond its traditional domain of periodic lattices with short-range hopping. Related studies have explored topological phenomena under non-Euclidean geometries, disordered structures, and long-range hopping, progressively narrowing the gap between topological phases of matter and complex networks. Here we demonstrate that genuine complex networks, far beyond periodic lattices and their conventional variants, can themselves host topological insulating phases. By developing a nonconflicting design framework for multiple topological objectives, we realize topological insulators in high-degree Hofstadter lattices and across regular, small-world, and random networks. This generalization uncovers distinct network-specific features, including an expanded range of attainable topological characteristics, a degree-dependent transition from Hofstadter-type to Haldane-type Chern insulators, and, most notably, the superior robustness and reconfigurability of small-world topological insulators. Our graph-theoretic framework establishes network complexity as a resource for realizing high-capacity and reconfigurable topological insulators.

A Basis-Aware Approach to Quantum Sampling of the Fermi-Hubbard Ladder

Authors: Adam Leicester, Zixu Wang, Jack Mandell, Yangyang Xu, Osama Muhammad Raisuddin, Haimeng Zhang, Jian Shi
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03654v1

Summary. This work analyzes the efficiency of quantum sampling techniques for simulating the Fermi-Hubbard ladder, demonstrating that the choice of basis representation is crucial for success. By comparing momentum, position, and bonding bases, the authors show that the best basis changes systematically with interaction parameters. Ultimately, they argue that realizing scalable quantum simulation requires a holistic co-design approach involving the basis, the quantum circuit, and the classical reconstruction method.

Why it may be interesting. The findings directly address the resource scaling and feasibility of quantum simulation for strongly correlated materials, providing critical guidelines for designing quantum algorithms for condensed matter physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how the choice of basis representation affects the efficiency of sample-based quantum diagonalization (SQD) for simulating strongly correlated electron systems.

Main result. The optimal basis for sampling is highly dependent on the interaction strength and hopping anisotropy, suggesting that scalable quantum simulation requires co-designing the basis, quantum circuit, and classical reconstruction procedure.

Method. The authors compare various physically motivated one-body orbital rotations (e.g., momentum, bonding/antibonding) to determine which basis yields the most compressible representation of the ground-state wave function.

Model / system. The primary model studied is the Fermi-Hubbard Ladder, a minimal system relevant to cuprate superconductors, described by a Hamiltonian involving hopping ($t$) and onsite repulsion ($U$).

Key observables. Compressibility (minimum basis size to retain 99% ground-state weight), relative energy error in statevector SKQD calculations, and two-qubit gate count/depth for hardware mapping.

Important parameters / regimes. Interaction strength ($U/t$), hopping anisotropy ($t_{ ext{rung}}/t_{ ext{leg}}$), and the specific basis used for sampling (position, momentum, etc.).

Assumptions / limitations. The core assumption is that the target ground state is sufficiently compressible in the sampled basis; otherwise, the advantage of SQD is lost.

Figures summary. Figures compare Hamiltonian matrix structures in different bases, show the dependence of reconstruction error on the retained subspace fraction, and compare hardware overheads for different circuit mappings.

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the basis dependence of SQD efficiency, comparing multiple physically motivated bases for the Fermi-Hubbard ladder, followed by detailed hardware overhead and reconstruction quality analyses.

Abstract

Sample-based quantum diagonalization can reduce the classical cost of many-body calculations by restricting diagonalization to configurations identified through quantum sampling, but its efficiency depends on how compactly the target state is represented in the sampled basis. We investigate this dependence for sample-based Krylov quantum diagonalization of the half-filled Fermi-Hubbard ladder using physically motivated one-body orbital rotations. Across position, momentum, bonding/antibonding, and leg-mode representations, the most compressible basis varies systematically with interaction strength and hopping anisotropy, with momentum sampling favored in the weakly interacting regime and geometry- or interaction-adapted representations becoming advantageous as the dominant energy scale changes. Near intermediate interaction and isotropic hopping, however, even the most favorable representation remains poorly compressible. Statevector SKQD calculations further show that basis choice strongly affects sampling efficiency. Hardware calculations on systems containing up to 120 qubits retain qualitative signatures of this basis dependence while revealing increasing particle-number leakage and reconstruction sensitivity with system size. These results show that scalable sample-based diagonalization requires co-design of the sampled representation, quantum circuit, and classical reconstruction procedure.

An operational characterization of finite-dimensional quantum theory

Authors: Lionel J. Dmello, Xiangling Xu, Marc-Olivier Renou, David Gross
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.03694v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper develops a method to certify that a physical theory is quantum mechanical by analyzing correlations derived from multi-party entanglement swapping and iterated teleportation. By constructing a hierarchy of such tests, the authors demonstrate that satisfying these constraints forces the underlying probabilistic theory to match quantum theory for finite dimensions. This suggests a potential 'self-testing' capability for quantum theory.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, operational framework for testing the fundamental postulates of quantum mechanics using entanglement and teleportation, which is highly relevant for understanding the limits of quantum information processing.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the foundational question of whether quantum theory can be fully characterized by operational constraints, specifically by constructing a set of correlations that, if realized, force the underlying theory to be quantum mechanics.

Main result. The authors show that by analyzing correlations derived from a hierarchy of entanglement swapping and teleportation experiments, they can constrain the effective probabilistic theory to be quantum theory for finite dimensions.

Method. The methodology involves constructing and analyzing correlation matrices derived from multi-party entanglement swapping and iterated teleportation protocols, using the formalism of General Probabilistic Theories (GPTs).

Model / system. The focus is on characterizing finite-dimensional quantum theory, specifically for systems described by $(\mathbb{C}^d)^{\otimes n}$ (n-qudits). The analysis uses Pauli operators and generalized Pauli operators to define measurements and correlations.

Key observables. Correlation matrices $C^{(r)}_\mu$, joint outcome probabilities $p(\vec{\mu}|\vec{r})$, and the structure of teleportation maps $\Lambda(\vec{r})_\mu$.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimensionality $d$ and the number of parties $n$ are key parameters; the analysis involves testing stability across an increasing number of teleportation steps $N$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the existence of a General Probabilistic Theory (GPT) framework and, in some parts, relies on the assumption that the state space is compact or that the correlations are preserved under iteration.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental setups for entanglement swapping (e.g., three-party setups for qubits) and the structure of the teleportation hierarchy ($T_N$) used to test correlation stability.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first defining the problem in terms of GPTs, then constructing the correlation tests via entanglement swapping and teleportation hierarchies, and finally deriving strong constraints on the underlying theory from the stability of these correlations.

Abstract

A key goal in the foundations of quantum mechanics is to identify operational constraints characterizing physical theories. Bell inequalities do so for classical probability theory, while Tsirelson's bound provides a first step for quantum mechanics. Here, we address the dual question: Can one certify that all correlations predicted by quantum theory are actually realizable? In this work, we construct a finite number of two-body correlations such that the only probabilistic theory that (1) realizes them, and (2) does so in a way that is stable under iterated teleportation, is quantum theory. For $(\mathbb{C}^d)^{\otimes n}$, Condition (1) can be verified using $\operatorname{poly}(d,n)$ measurement settings. Condition (2) may be understood as a hierarchy of tests, one for each number $N$ of teleportation steps. There is thus a sense in which finite-dimensional quantum theory can be self-tested. In particular, one can certify the existence of Bell inequality violations larger than any that have been directly observed.

Covertness as a resource constraint in quantum target sensing

Summary. This paper develops a theoretical framework connecting the requirement of remaining covert during quantum sensing with the ultimate achievable estimation precision. By introducing the covertness-constrained quantum Fisher information (CCQFI), the authors quantify how the need to evade detection limits the available probe energy. They show that different quantum states, like TMSV, can maintain superior metrological advantages even when severely constrained by the covertness requirement.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly bridges quantum estimation theory (metrology) with quantum security/detection theory (covertness), providing a resource-constrained view of quantum sensing that is highly relevant to quantum optics and quantum information.

Main problem. The paper establishes a unified framework to quantify how the requirement of remaining covert (avoiding detection by an adversary) constrains the ultimate precision achievable in quantum target sensing.

Main result. The covertness constraint is identified as a quantitative resource that governs the metrological limits, showing that different quantum probes (GCS vs. TMSV) exhibit distinct scaling behaviors in the strong covertness regime.

Method. The authors use quantum information theory tools, including the covertness-constrained quantum Fisher information (CCQFI), to bound the estimation precision for target reflectivity.

Abstract

Quantum covert target sensing aims to detect low-reflectivity targets embedded in the thermal environment while avoiding discovery by an adversary. This covertness requirement constrains the admissible probe energy, thereby imposing a trade-off between covertness and sensing performance. However, how this resource constraint determines the ultimate estimation precision of the target parameter remains largely unexplored. To fill this gap, we establish a unified framework connecting covert target discrimination with quantum-enhanced parameter estimation. These results connect fault-tolerant quantum information with relativistic clocks and establish protected quantum memories as a platform for preserving proper-time information and for long-coherence tests of quantum evolution involving relativistic proper time.

Abstract

We initiate the study of weak Schur sampling (WSS) under noisy input. Many optimal quantum learning algorithms rely on this measurement, for instance in such fundamental tasks as quantum spectrum estimation (QSE) and quantum state tomography (QST). The standard analysis of WSS, QSE and QST assumes that the $n$ input copies of the target state $ρ$ are identical. We study what happens when they are not, a more realistic noisy scenario that breaks the very permutation symmetry on which WSS is built. In the simplest noise model, the copies are independent but not necessarily identical, i.e. they form a product state, and each is $ε$-close in trace distance to the $d$-dimensional target state $ρ$. A naive data-processing argument lets the per-copy errors accumulate to $nε$ on measurement output, growing with the input size. We prove that they do not accumulate: the expected output error in total variation is at most $ε+η(n,d)$, where $η(n,d)=O(d/\sqrt n)$ is the noiseless error, and the additive noise term $ε$ is optimal. Beyond noisy product states, for arbitrary inputs $ω$ we show that the outcome observables of WSS are $1/n$-Lipschitz with respect to the quantum Wasserstein distance $W_1$ of De Palma et al., so the error is at most $η(n,d)+\frac{1}{n}\|ω-ρ^{\otimes n}\|_{W_1}$. In particular, our results imply, for the first time, noise-robustness of Keyl and Werner's seminal algorithm for QSE, more than two decades after its introduction. Within our model of robustness, the promise cannot be weakened to closeness of single-copy marginals or to a global trace-distance budget alone, since in both cases some noisy inputs defeat every measurement and estimator. Our results are a necessary step towards optimal quantum learning in practice, and help make progress towards robustness of many other problems in quantum information theory.
