Intermittency in Quantum Graviton-Phonon Conversion

Authors: Yuna Gouin, Sugumi Kanno, Jiro Soda

Summary. This paper analyzes the conversion of gravitons into phonons by solving the full quantum dynamics exactly. It finds that while first-order perturbation theory predicts strong enhancement, the true quantum evolution exhibits intermittent bursts separated by suppression for both coherent and squeezed initial states. This non-perturbative behavior offers a unique signature for detecting single quanta in gravitational wave detectors.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides an excellent example of how non-perturbative quantum effects (like intermittency) manifest in coupled bosonic systems, offering insights applicable to open quantum systems dynamics beyond simple weak coupling limits.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates quantum graviton-phonon conversion beyond first-order perturbation theory to establish a robust, non-perturbative signature of the quantum nature of gravity for single-graviton detection.

Main result. For coherent and squeezed initial states, the exact quantum dynamics reveal intermittent conversion bursts separated by strong suppression, which is fundamentally different from the predictions of simple perturbative expansions.

Method. The authors solved the full quantum dynamics exactly using an algebraic method, reducing the problem to a set of coupled ordinary differential equations (ODEs) within the rotating-wave approximation.

Model / system. The system models the interaction between a graviton field and a phonon mode in a resonant bar detector. The Hamiltonian is derived under the rotating-wave approximation, coupling the two bosonic fields.

Key observables. Conversion probability ($P_{g o p}(t)$), satisfaction of the unitarity bound, and the observation of intermittent time evolution patterns.

Important parameters / regimes. The initial state type (coherent vs. squeezed) and the magnitude of the squeezing/coherence parameters ($\alpha$ or $\xi$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is performed within the rotating-wave approximation (RWA), which is noted as a limitation, and the derived ODE system has restrictions on its parameter range.

Figures summary. Figures compare perturbative predictions against exact quantum results for both coherent and squeezed states, explicitly showing where perturbation theory violates unitarity or fails to capture the intermittent behavior.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the physical model and Hamiltonian under RWA. It then develops the full quantum dynamics by solving the time-evolution equations into a closed system of ODEs. Finally, it analyzes the results for coherent and squeezed states, contrasting the exact findings with perturbative approximations.

Abstract

A graviton can be converted into a phonon in a resonant bar detector. First-order perturbation theory predicts a strong enhancement of this conversion for coherent and squeezed graviton states, but the probability can exceed unity when the coherent or squeezing parameter is large. Since a conversion probability must satisfy the unitarity bound, we solve the graviton-phonon quantum dynamics exactly within the rotating-wave approximation. For an initial coherent state, we find that the conversion occurs intermittently through narrow bursts separated by intervals of strong suppression. For an initial squeezed state, the departure from perturbative behavior occurs earlier, and the conversion is strongly suppressed after its initial growth. These effects may provide signatures of quantum graviton-phonon dynamics relevant to single-graviton detection.

Summary. This paper presents a theoretical scheme to generate vortex optical squeezing by exploiting Raman scattering within an atomic ensemble prepared in a coherent superposition state. By analyzing quantum fluctuations, the authors demonstrate that both incident control and generated signal fields can exhibit squeezing, offering a flexible alternative to conventional nonlinear optics methods for quantum information applications.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it proposes a new platform for generating non-classical light (squeezing) using fundamental atomic interactions (Raman scattering), which is a core topic in quantum optics and AMO physics. The ability to tune the squeezing via system parameters adds significant practical depth.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The authors theoretically propose an alternative method for generating vortex optical squeezing, distinct from established techniques like parametric down-conversion or four-wave mixing.

Main result. They show that using Raman scattering in a coherently prepared medium allows both the control and signal fields to exhibit measurable squeezing. Furthermore, they demonstrate that optimal squeezing values can be achieved by tuning system parameters.

Method. The analysis involves deriving and solving quantum fluctuation equations for the light fields coupled to an atomic ensemble, utilizing the mean-field approximation.

Model / system. The physical model is a three-level L-type atomic structure (potentially using $^{87} ext{Rb}$), where the ground state is coherently prepared. The interaction dynamics are governed by the system Hamiltonian and analyzed via Heisenberg-Langevin equations.

Key observables. Squeezing degree ($V$) for both the control and signal light fields, orthogonal variance ($\langle\Delta X_c \Delta X_s angle$).

Important parameters / regimes. Two-photon detuning ($ ext{s}_c d$), optical density ($\alpha$), topological charge ($l$), and ground-state relaxation rate ($\Gamma_G$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the weak interaction approximation and assumes that mode conversion devices are not necessary for this scheme.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the dependence of light intensities, signal field squeezing degree ($V$) versus two-photon detuning ($d$), and $V$ versus ground-state relaxation rate ($\Gamma_G$) for both control and signal fields.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the theoretical framework using a three-level system Hamiltonian. It then develops the quantum fluctuation equations, analyzes the squeezing observables by tuning parameters like detuning and density, and concludes by comparing this novel Raman scheme to existing methods.

Abstract

In this Letter, we theoretically propose an alternative scheme for generating vortex optical squeezing, based on the Raman scattering process in a coherently prepared medium, distinct from approaches such as parametric down-conversion and four-wave mixing. Our analysis reveals that both the input control field and the generated signal field can exhibit squeezing upon adjusting the relevant system parameters, with the control field exhibiting a greater degree of squeezing under identical conditions. We further demonstrate the existence of optimal squeezing values over a range of tuning parameters, highlighting the flexibility and robustness of the proposed scheme. These findings may offer a new reference for continuous-variable vortex optical squeezing and possess potential applications in domains such as quantum information processing, quantum precision measurement, and quantum sensing.

Summary. This theoretical study demonstrates that van der Waals heterostructures can be highly doped using noble gas fluorides ($ ext{XeF}_2$, $ ext{KrF}_2$) acting as chemical capacitors. DFT calculations confirm significant, non-contact charge transfer into TMDs like $ ext{WS}_2$ and $ ext{WSe}_2$. This offers a powerful new strategy for engineering electronic properties in 2D materials.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on solid-state doping, the ability to precisely tune electronic states via chemical potential differences mimics concepts relevant to open quantum systems where external chemical baths or coupling mediate state preparation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is achieving precise, stable, and controllable carrier density (doping) in atomically thin van der Waals materials without applying external electric fields or causing structural damage.

Main result. Noble gas fluorides ($ ext{XeF}_2$, $ ext{KrF}_2$) provide an efficient, non-contact chemical route to induce significant hole doping ($0.23 - 0.35 h^+$ per W atom) in TMD heterostructures.

Method. The study employs periodic Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations using advanced functionals (SCAN meta-GGA with rVV10 correction) to model charge transfer across van der Waals interfaces.

Model / system. The system modeled is the $ ext{TMD} | ext{hBN} | ext{OX}$ heterostructure, where TMDs are transition metal dichalcogenides ($ ext{WS}_2$, $ ext{WSe}_2$), $ ext{hBN}$ acts as a dielectric spacer, and $ ext{OX}$ is the electron acceptor (e.g., $ ext{XeF}_2$).

Key observables. Interlayer Charge Transfer ($ ext{CT}$), resulting hole concentrations ($h^+$) per W atom, band alignment type (Type III), bond lengths, and geometric polarization.

Important parameters / regimes. Hole concentrations up to $0.35 h^+/ ext{W}$ atom; $ ext{hBN}$ separation layer properties; chemical potential differences driving CT.

Assumptions / limitations. The concept extends the 'chemical capacitor' idea, assuming that charge injection is solely driven by the chemical potential difference between donor and acceptor materials.

Figures summary. Tables summarize bond length changes across different heterostructures (Table S1) and polarization/electronic structure details. Figures illustrate optimized structures and PDOS confirming type III band alignment.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the need for non-contact doping, introduces the $ ext{TMD} | ext{hBN} | ext{OX}$ model based on chemical capacitors, performs DFT calculations to confirm large charge transfer in specific noble gas fluoride systems ($ ext{WS}_2/ ext{XeF}_2$ and $ ext{WSe}_2/ ext{KrF}_2$), and details the resulting structural/electronic changes.

Abstract

Van der Waals heterostructures based on transition metal dichalcogenides, TMDs, provide a versatile platform for tailoring electronic properties through interlayer charge transfer, CT. Precise control of CT is essential because it directly determines the electronic structure and carrier concentration in atomically thin materials. Recently, the concept of a chemical capacitor has been proposed as a route to achieving exceptionally high carrier densities through CT across insulating separator layers. Here, we extend this concept to van der Waals heterostructures by investigating TMD hBN OX, oxidizer, systems using density functional theory, DFT. Following the screening of candidate TMDs and electron acceptors, XeF2 and KrF2 were identified as suitable acceptors exhibiting type III broken gap band alignment with WS2 and WSe2, respectively. Periodic DFT calculations of large supercells reveal CT corresponding to hole concentrations of up to 0.23 h+ and 0.35 h+ per W atom in WS2 hBN XeF2 and WSe2 hBN KrF2 heterostructures, respectively. The resulting charge redistribution demonstrates that noble gas fluorides provide an efficient route for noncontact engineering of carrier density in TMD heterostructures, offering a new strategy for tuning correlated electronic phases in two dimensional materials.

Summary. This work analyzes reliable quantum teleportation under realistic network constraints, specifically focusing on latency-induced entanglement degradation. By developing a unified framework incorporating stochastic resource generation and memory decoherence, the authors show that adaptive code puncturing is necessary to maintain high fidelity across varying operational delays. This highlights the need for resource-aware protocols in building functional quantum networks.

Why it may be interesting. It provides quantitative tools for understanding how practical network imperfections—like waiting times causing decoherence—limit the theoretical gains promised by quantum error correction codes in distributed quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper evaluates the reliability of quantum teleportation when constrained by latency, considering the interplay between entanglement availability, decoherence during storage, and the choice of error correction code.

Main result. Adaptive puncturing allows for optimal encoded teleportation performance across varying network latency regimes, demonstrating that resource-aware adaptation is crucial for reliable quantum networking.

Method. The authors developed a unified framework to model stochastic entanglement generation, memory decoherence, and the use of punctured Quantum Error-Correcting Codes (QECCs).

Model / system. The system models quantum teleportation using shared entangled pairs, where reliability is assessed by calculating the logical error probability ($P_L$) under realistic noise sources like storage decoherence.

Key observables. Logical error probability ($P_L$), entanglement fidelity ($F$), and the effective code length/distance.

Important parameters / regimes. Average entanglement-acquisition latency constraint ($L$), detection probability ($p_d$), and the rate of memory decoherence.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes a model for stochastic entanglement generation (Bernoulli trials) and an exponential decay model for storage-induced decoherence.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the latency–reliability tradeoff, showing how optimal code length selection shifts based on constraints; other figures map $P_L$ against varying latency or link quality parameters.

Paper structure. The paper builds a unified framework by first modeling resource limitations (latency/decoherence) and then applying this model to compare uncoded vs. various encoded teleportation schemes, culminating in the proposal of adaptive code puncturing strategies.

Abstract

Quantum teleportation is a key protocol for transmitting quantum information using entanglement and classical communication. Its reliability is constrained by both the availability and fidelity of shared entangled pairs, which are affected by stochastic generation and memory decoherence. In this work, we focus on encoded teleportation, in which quantum information is encoded using a quantum error-correcting code and transmitted as a codeword. We evaluate reliability in terms of logical error probability, considering latency-constrained settings where entanglement is accumulated over time and degrades while in memory. We develop a unified framework that captures the interaction between entanglement availability, decoherence, and coding decisions. Our results show that the benefits of longer codes depend on the availability and fidelity of entangled pairs, as acquiring additional resources introduces delays that can reduce their quality. To address this latency-reliability tradeoff, we leverage code puncturing to enable flexible encoded teleportation, allowing the effective code length to adapt across different latency regimes while preserving a common stabilizer structure. Numerical results show that encoded teleportation can provide substantial reliability gains over uncoded transmission under a common entanglement-acquisition latency constraint, and that selecting appropriate punctured codes improves performance across varying latency budgets. Overall, our results highlight the importance of resource-aware adaptation for reliable quantum networking.

Slack-Free Deep-Unfolded Combinatorial Optimization Solver for Inequality Constraints

Summary. This paper presents DU-UPOM, an advanced solver designed to tackle complex combinatorial optimization problems with inequality constraints for quantum annealers. It improves upon existing methods by combining Unbalanced Penalization with the Ohzeki method and using Deep Unfolding to learn adaptive update schedules. The results show that this learned approach achieves optimal solutions much faster than static or fixed-step alternatives.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on optimization algorithms rather than fundamental quantum dynamics, the reliance on sampling distributions (MCMC) and the connection to QUBO formulations makes it highly relevant for understanding practical implementations of quantum computation hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is efficiently solving Combinatorial Optimization Problems (COPs) subject to inequality constraints when mapping them onto quantum annealers.

Main result. The proposed Deep-Unfolded Unbalanced Penalization Ohzeki Method (DU-UPOM) significantly improves convergence speed and robustness compared to previous methods by learning the optimal step-size schedule adaptively.

Method. The authors combine the Unbalanced Penalization (UP) technique for inequalities with the iterative Ohzeki method, further enhancing it using Deep Unfolding (DU) to train the update step sizes.

Model / system. The work models COPs as QUBO problems suitable for quantum annealers. The mathematical framework involves penalty functions and iterative sampling updates derived from the Ohzeki transformation.

Key observables. Mean Squared Error (MSE) of the solution relative to the optimum, Optimal Instance Rate (fraction reaching the true optimum), and the learned step-size schedule $\{\eta_t\}$.

Important parameters / regimes. Penalty coefficients ($\lambda_1, \lambda_2$), learning rate/step size schedule ($\{\eta_t\}$), and problem instance parameters (e.g., knapsack weights $w_i$ and profits $p_i$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes that the expectation derivatives required for backpropagation can be estimated accurately from Monte Carlo samples drawn from the sampler distribution.

Figures summary. Figures compare convergence speed (MSE vs. iterations) showing DU-UPOM significantly outperforms fixed-step and baseline methods, and one figure illustrates the nonuniformly learned step-size schedule.

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the limitations of standard QUBO encoding for inequalities. It then details UPOM as an improvement over original UP by integrating Ohzeki's method. Finally, it extends this to DU-UPOM, training the update schedule using deep unfolding techniques and validating performance on knapsack problems.

Abstract

Quantum annealing (QA) is used to solve combinatorial optimization problems (COPs). When COPs are implemented on quantum annealers, they are typically encoded as quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problems, but constraint encodings often increase the number of qubits and the embedding overhead. This issue is particularly important for COPs with inequality constraints, where standard slack-variable formulations introduce additional binary variables. Unbalanced penalization (UP) avoids slack variables, but the original UP formulation requires tuning two penalty coefficients and contains a squared residual term that can increase the number of quadratic couplings. In this paper, we propose the unbalanced penalization Ohzeki method (UPOM), which combines UP with the Ohzeki method for inequality-constrained COPs. UPOM replaces the two static penalty coefficients of original UP with an auxiliary-variable update and removes the squared residual term from the Hamiltonian used for sampling. We further propose the deep-unfolded unbalanced penalization Ohzeki method (DU-UPOM), which learns the step-size schedule of the UPOM update from training instances. Numerical experiments on random knapsack problems show that UPOM improves over original UP and that DU-UPOM reaches optimal solutions in fewer iterations than fixed-step UPOM and other baseline. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework reduces the tuning and embedding burdens of UP while making the Ohzeki method trainable for inequality constraints.

Biased-noise qubits: a guide to efficient fault-tolerance using the hierarchy of errors

Authors: Diego Ruiz, Jérémie Guillaud, Christophe Vuillot, Mazyar Mirrahimi
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.20143v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 2/5 · measurement-induced transitions 1/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This review analyzes fault-tolerant quantum computing protocols designed specifically for qubits experiencing strongly biased noise. It demonstrates that the choice of available bias-preserving gates dictates the optimal code structure, allowing for tailored resource reduction. The work concludes by proposing a measurement-based architecture to extend these benefits across diverse physical platforms.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a detailed, actionable guide on how to tailor quantum error correction overheads by exploiting specific noise characteristics inherent in physical hardware.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Developing efficient fault-tolerance protocols for qubits operating under strongly biased noise, where phase-flip errors significantly outnumber bit-flips.

Main result. The availability of a bias-preserving CX gate allows the error hierarchy to be encoded into the code structure, enabling specialized correction strategies; otherwise, standard error correction may suffice or a measurement-based approach is necessary.

Method. Theoretical analysis comparing fault-tolerant protocols based on the set of available 'bias-preserving operations' (CZ vs. CX vs. QND measurements).

Model / system. The analysis covers general biased-noise qubits, including physical realizations like electron/nuclear spins and engineered cat qubits.

Key observables. Error rates for phase-flips versus bit-flips; complexity overhead of syndrome extraction gadgets.

Important parameters / regimes. Noise bias ratio ($\eta = p_Z / (p_X + p_Y)$); available elementary gates (CZ, CX, QND readout).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis hinges on the set of physically implementable, bias-preserving quantum operations.

Paper structure. The review analyzes protocols based on three architectural categories: CZ-based (showing limited benefit), CX-based (showing significant structural benefits), and Measurement-based (proposing an alternative for difficult platforms).

Abstract

Qubits with strongly biased noise, in which phase-flip errors are orders of magnitude more frequent than bit-flips, arise both naturally, as in electron and nuclear spins, and by engineering, as in stabilized cat qubits. This noise structure holds the promise of reducing the daunting hardware overhead of fault-tolerant quantum computing, but exploiting it requires physical operations that do not convert frequent phase-flips into rare bit-flips. In this review, we analyze the most prominent fault-tolerant protocols for biased-noise qubits, organized according to the available set of such bias-preserving operations. When this set is restricted to the CZ gate together with preparation and measurement in the X basis, we show that the complexity of the required syndrome extraction gadgets essentially cancels the benefit of the noise bias: at experimentally relevant error rates, one may as well ignore the bias and rely on standard error correction designed for depolarizing noise. The situation changes drastically when a bias-preserving CX gate is available: the hierarchy of errors can then be reflected in the structure of the code, with frequent phase-flips corrected by a dedicated high-threshold code and rare bit-flips by concatenation with a high-rate code. The same hierarchy also enables hardware-efficient preparation of magic states. Finally, as a bias-preserving CX is forbidden in naturally biased platforms and challenging in engineered ones, we present a measurement-based architecture in which a high-fidelity quantum non-demolition readout of multi-qubit Pauli Z operators takes its place, extending these overhead reductions to a much broader range of physical platforms.

Summary. This paper introduces an efficient method to model distributed resonators in superconducting circuits by replacing the entire structure with a simple lumped LC circuit. By fitting measured scattering parameters, this technique achieves high accuracy comparable to full electromagnetic simulations, significantly streamlining the process of Hamiltonian derivation for quantum hardware design.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial tool for circuit designers in quantum computing, allowing them to build accurate quantum Hamiltonians from complex physical layouts without running full EM simulations, which is vital for scaling up superconducting qubit architectures.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is developing simple, accurate lumped-element models for distributed resonators used in superconducting quantum circuits to reduce reliance on computationally expensive finite-element electromagnetic simulations.

Main result. The authors propose a novel method that accurately replaces a coupled, distributed resonator with an equivalent LC circuit model while preserving the scattering parameters and Hamiltonian predictions, even under strong coupling conditions.

Method. The technique involves simulating the full loaded transmission line using scikit-rf to obtain S-parameters, which are then fitted to determine effective lumped inductance ($L_{eff}$) and capacitance ($C_{eff}$).

Model / system. The system is superconducting quantum circuits containing distributed elements like coplanar waveguides (CPW) resonators coupled to other components. The goal is to derive an equivalent LC circuit model for the resonator.

Key observables. Scattering parameters ($S_{11}, S_{22}$), resonant frequency shift ($\Delta f$), and linewidth ($\kappa$).

Paper structure. Abstract

Superconducting quantum circuit design is reliant on accurately mapping design parameters to a quantum Hamiltonian. Designers typically rely on computationally intensive finite-element electromagnetic simulations. However, effective circuit-level models can in principle capture much of the relevant physics for these systems, reducing the need for finite-element simulations. A barrier to implementing these simpler models has been the prevalence of distributed elements such as coplanar waveguides resonators in device designs. Techniques for modeling these distributed elements have been developed, but may be difficult to scale, reduce intuition, and give inaccurate results under strong coupling. In this work we describe a simple effective circuit-level model that faithfully reproduces the scattering parameters, and subsequently can predict certain Hamiltonian parameters, for a coupled, distributed element within a broader two-port network even up to strong coupling. Our approach does not explicitly require an electromagnetic simulation. Our model, along with other common lumped models used in black box quantization, is publicly available to researchers via an open-source code package, $\texttt{simpleLOMs}$.

Summary. This paper analyzes how physical node volumes modify information flow dynamics (diffusion) on complex networks. It demonstrates that connectivity alone is insufficient, showing that the degree-volume ratio acts as the critical disorder parameter governing spectral localization. This provides a general framework for understanding how local physical constraints reshape global network transport properties.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on classical diffusion, the concept of localization control via an emergent ratio parameter ($k/v$) is analogous to how interaction strengths or geometric constraints can dictate quantum state confinement in engineered quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how physical heterogeneity, introduced via node volumes, alters the localization patterns of diffusion dynamics in complex networks beyond what is predicted by topology alone.

Main result. The relevant disorder parameter controlling spectral localization shifts from simple degree correlations to the degree-volume ratio ($d/v$), revealing a feature-rich mechanism for controlling network transport properties.

Method. The authors employ spectral analysis of the node-weighted Laplacian, using the Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR) to quantify mode localization in both solvable defect models and empirical networks.

Model / system. The system is modeled as a connected simple network $G$ with nodes possessing degrees ($k_i$) and associated volumes ($v_i$). The dynamics are governed by the node-weighted Laplacian operator, $L_P$.

Key observables. Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR) of extremal eigenvectors ($\psi_2, \psi_N$), and the Jaccard index comparing localization sets defined by degree, volume, or ratio.

Important parameters / regimes. The primary control parameter is the degree-volume ratio ($r = d/v$) or the correlation exponent $\alpha$ governing how volumes scale with degrees ($ ext{e.g.} v_i \propto k_i^\alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on solving an analytical defect model and comparing results across synthetic random graphs (ER, CL) and real-world physical networks.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate spectral shifts in the defect model based on $d/v$, showing localization thresholds; other figures compare IPR vs. correlation exponent $\alpha$ for different graph types, confirming the shift to ratio control.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the physical system and the limitations of topology-only analysis, through developing a solvable defect model using spectral theory, and finally validating these findings by analyzing empirical networks across varying degree-volume correlations ($\alpha$).

Abstract

Network diffusion underlies many transport phenomena, with Laplacian modes setting how information spreads and relaxes. In physical networks, however, connectivity alone is not enough: node volumes introduce local dwell times that regulate how flow is stored before being propagated. Here we show that physical heterogeneity reshapes topology-driven localization, with the degree-volume ratio emerging as the relevant disorder parameter. We solve an analytical model in which ratio detuning qualitatively reorganizes the Laplacian spectrum, and demonstrate in empirical networks how degree-volume correlations shift extremal eigenmodes away from the nodes selected by topology alone. Our results reveal a general feature-rich-driven mechanism for localization control, showing that physicality non-trivially reshapes the disorder landscape governing network dynamics.

Abstract

Abstract

Kernel methods are powerful tools in machine learning but commonly used full-Gram kernels face three key limitations: (1) quadratic scaling with training set size; (2) the use of fixed, non-trainable kernels; and (3) the absence of an intrinsic formulation for multiclass classification. We present McQuack, a trainable quantum kernel method for multiclass problems that achieves linear scaling in the number of training samples. This is accomplished by replacing the full training-set Gram matrix with a trainable sample-to-(class-centroid) fidelity matrix. We evaluate the model in simulation and on 124 qubits of two IBM devices, across more than 150 datasets. Finally, we study the trainability of the model and observe no evidence of barren plateaus in our experiments with up to 13 qubits, and highlight the importance of parameter initialization for successful optimization.

Abstract

The fractional angular momentum of a two-dimensional charge-flux composite is a well-established phenomenon usually derived from a semiclassical Hamiltonian. However, when the composite is treated as an isolated system in free two-dimensional space, the fundamental rotational and reflection symmetries of the $O(2)$ group demand that its total angular momentum is strictly quantized. We address this conceptual discrepancy by applying a full quantum electrodynamic (QED) approach combined with Noether's theorem. We demonstrate that the interaction between the charge and flux, mediated by the vacuum electromagnetic field, generates an intrinsic interaction angular momentum composed of both field momentum and hidden relativistic momentum. This gauge-invariant interaction angular momentum exactly compensates for the fractional part of the kinetic angular momentum. Consequently, the net angular momentum of the composite strictly follows the integer or half-integer quantization rule. Our formalism clarifies that the conventional fractional spin corresponds to the expectation value of the kinetic angular momentum within the perturbed QED ground state. It also elucidates why the standard classical field angular momentum definition fails to capture this in two dimensions, due to non-vanishing boundary terms.
