Summary. This paper presents a general framework to derive the reduced dynamics generator for local observables in noninteracting quantum chains by tracing out the environment. It successfully handles the complexity of subsystem-environment correlations, showing how coupling strength dictates whether the dynamics are Markovian or non-Markovian. This advances the theory of open quantum systems beyond simple weak-coupling approximations.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a rigorous, computationally tractable method to study non-Markovian dynamics in complex quantum systems by systematically handling the environment coupling, which is crucial for understanding realistic open quantum systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper develops a framework to derive a reduced description for local observables of a many-body quantum system by tracing out the environment, particularly for noninteracting quantum chains.

Main result. The framework successfully reconstructs the time-local dynamical generator for noninteracting chains with polynomial complexity, characterizing how subsystem-environment correlations affect local dynamics beyond weak-coupling approximations.

Method. The approach uses open quantum systems theory, assuming Gaussian environments and initially uncorrelated states to derive the generator $\mathcal{L}_t$ governing the subsystem evolution.

Model / system. The study focuses on noninteracting quantum chains (e.g., Kitaev chain), treating the total system as partitioned into a subsystem (S) and an environment (E). Dynamics are analyzed via the evolution of the correlation matrix $\Theta_t$.

Key observables. Reduced density operators ($ ho_S(t)$), correlation matrix ($\Theta_t$), and non-Markovianity measures like the Rivas-Huelga-Plenio (RHP) and Breuer-Laine-Piilo (BLP) measures.

Important parameters / regimes. Coupling strength, inverse temperature ($eta$), and the structure of the environment (e.g., fully connected free-fermion environment).

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation relies on assuming initially uncorrelated states ($\chi_0 = 0$) and Gaussian environments ($ ho_{E0}$), which allows for the reconstruction of the generator with polynomial complexity.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the time evolution of non-Markovianity measures and the time evolution of correlation matrix components ($\alpha(t), \Gamma_1(t), \Gamma_2(t)$) under varying coupling strengths and temperatures.

Paper structure. The paper builds the framework by defining the reduced dynamics generator, applying it to specific models like the Kitaev chain, and analyzing the resulting non-Markovian dynamics using established measures.

Abstract

When only local observables of a many-body quantum system are of interest, it is desirable to formulate a reduced description within the Hilbert space of the corresponding subsystem, with the remaining degrees of freedom traced out and acting as an environment. Assuming initially uncorrelated states and Gaussian environments, we develop a framework for reconstructing the local dynamical generator of noninteracting quantum chains, with polynomial computational complexity. As an application, we consider two representative models: a bipartitioned Kitaev chain and a Kitaev chain boundary-coupled to a fully connected free-fermion environment. In both models, strong subsystem-environment coupling leads to non-Markovian dynamics characterized by ballistic spreading of the Lindblad dissipator support within the subsystem. On the other hand, weak coupling to a fully connected environment yields predominantly boundary-localized, Markovian dissipation. Our work highlights the implications of subsystem-environment correlations on the generator of local dynamics, beyond the conventional weak-coupling approximations.

Summary. This paper introduces Collective Quantum Logic Spectroscopy (QLS) as a powerful, scalable technique for improving precision measurements in trapped-ion quantum sensors. Main problem. The paper addresses the challenge of scaling trapped-ion quantum sensors to large ensembles while overcoming the limitation that many target ion species lack closed cycling transitions for direct laser cooling and state detection.

Abstract

Scaling trapped-ion quantum sensors from single ions to large ensembles is a key challenge for next-generation precision measurements. At the same time, many ion species of interest for optical clocks and tests of fundamental physics lack closed cycling transitions required for direct laser cooling and state detection. Collective quantum logic spectroscopy addresses both limitations by coupling an ensemble of sensor, or spectroscopy, ions to one or more logic ions that provide sympathetic cooling and state readout. Here, we establish the fundamental performance limits and operating regimes of this protocol, identifying how the interaction strength, interrogation time, and logic-ensemble size govern sensitivity, dynamic range, and robustness to experimental imperfections. We show that quantum-limited sensitivity can be retained even with a single logic ion, while increasing the number of logic ions substantially improves readout efficiency and robustness. Beyond precision metrology, the same collective interface enables many-body measurements relevant to quantum information processing, including parity measurements and stabilizer-like syndrome extraction. Our results establish collective quantum logic spectroscopy as a scalable framework for optical clocks, quantum-enhanced sensing, and trapped-ion quantum information processing.

Summary. This paper reports experimental evidence and theoretical modeling showing that the diamagnetic interaction in ultrastrongly coupled electron-photon systems is nonlinear. Abstract

The quantum Hopfield model is widely used to describe ultrastrong light--matter coupling between cavity photons and collective bosonic excitations in solids, where the diamagnetic interaction is conventionally assumed to be a constant. We experimentally demonstrate that the diamagnetic response of Landau polaritons is reduced under strong terahertz field excitation. We show that this behavior originates from field-driven redistribution of electrons into the nonparabolic regime of the conduction band of GaAs, which reduces the plasma frequency and consequently the diamagnetic interaction strength. A microscopic hot-electron model reproduces the observed nonlinear response. Motivated by this microscopic picture, we propose a nonlinear extension of the Hopfield model with a Kerr-like interaction. Our results establish a route toward nonlinear cavity quantum electrodynamics and driven ultrastrong light--matter coupling beyond the conventional linear Hopfield description, which is capable of creating uniquely quantum optical effects such as squeezed light generation.

This allows for accurate, matrix-free simulation of complex dynamics, including cavity loss and disorder, in large molecular ensembles.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The computational cost of using Hierarchical Equations of Motion (HEOM) for simulating large open quantum systems, such as those involving large polariton ensembles.

Main result. A symmetry-adapted HEOM formalism is developed that significantly reduces computational complexity by exploiting molecular indistinguishability, making simulations feasible for much larger ensembles.

Method. The method employs a two-stage reduction: first, replacing redundant Auxiliary Density Operators (ADOs) related by molecule relabeling with canonical representatives; second, propagating only unique complex variables instead of full ADO matrices.

Model / system. The study focuses on large polariton systems modeled by the Holstein-Tavis-Cummings (HTC) model, which involves multiple two-level molecules coupled to a cavity mode and independent harmonic baths. The dynamics are governed by the HEOM formalism.

Key observables. Polariton populations ($P_+, P_-$), coherence terms ($X_\Lambda, Y_\Lambda$), and the decay of the surviving excitation population ($P_{ ext{surv}}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Hierarchy depth (L), number of bath correlation exponentials (m), cavity loss rate ($\kappa$), and static disorder strength ($\delta\epsilon_i$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method assumes identical molecular properties and relies on the existence of a relabeling-compatible hierarchy closure. It also incorporates extensions for static disorder averaging and cavity loss.

Abstract

Hierarchical equations of motion(HEOM) provide exact dynamics of open quantum systems coupled to harmonic baths, but their computational cost becomes prohibitive for systems with many independent local environments. In this work, we develop a symmetry-adapted HEOM formalism to significantly reduce the computational cost for the permutationally invariant Holstein-Tavis-Cummings (HTC) model. The method removes redundant information in two stages. First, all auxiliary density operators (ADOs) related only by relabeling identical molecules and their bath channels are replaced by a single canonical occupation-pattern representative. Second, molecules with the same local hierarchy occupation produce repeated matrix elements within each representative, allowing only the distinct complex variables to be propagated instead of the full (N + 1) $\times$ (N + 1) ADO matrices. The resulting matrix-free equations are evaluated using precomputed connections and molecular multiplicities. At fixed hierarchy depth L and number of bath correlation exponentials m, the number of canonical representatives becomes independent of the ensemble size for N $\geq$ L and the number of unique variables saturates for N $\geq$ L + 2. The formulation easily extends to multiple-exponential bath decompositions, arbitrary initial density operators, static disorders, and cavity loss. Our benchmarks reproduce conventional HEOM dynamics while requiring far fewer propagated variables and substantially less memory.

Abstract

Mobility links an applied force to the resulting motion and is generally positive near equilibrium. Far from equilibrium, however, internal energy input can reverse this response. Here we show that information feedback provides a distinct route to negative mobility. We consider an overdamped dimer consisting of a run-and-tumble particle coupled by a spring to a passive Brownian particle. Information enters through periodic measurements of the relative displacement, which are processed to reset the active polarity. Although the unloaded dimer has no net drift, an applied force biases its internal configuration, and an information-mechanical feedback amplifies and converts this bias into active propulsion against the force, producing negative mobility. We develop an analytical theory that captures this mechanism and yields a feedback-gain criterion for response reversal. Including the information-processing cost reveals a tradeoff: rapid feedback enhances reverse transport but incurs a growing informational cost, yielding an optimal finite feedback rate for information-inclusive efficiency. These results show that information can reshape nonequilibrium response by controlling how internally supplied energy is converted into macroscopic transport.

Abstract

Summary. This work analyzes the velocity of an interface driven by thermal fluctuations (entropic forces) when the system is subjected to a steady shear flow. The authors derive scaling laws showing that the velocity correction depends on the shear rate, exhibiting a characteristic $4/3$ power law at low shear rates. It provides a theoretical framework for understanding non-equilibrium transport in fluctuating interfaces.

Why it may be interesting. The study of non-equilibrium dynamics, fluctuation-driven transport, and the modification of thermodynamic forces by external fields (like shear flow) is highly relevant to understanding open quantum systems and non-equilibrium statistical mechanics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates how an imposed shear flow modifies the mechanism by which an interface propagates due to an entropic force arising from bulk thermal fluctuations.

Main result. At low shear rates, the interface velocity correction scales with the 4/3 power of the shear rate, and at high shear rates, the velocity is dominated by the modified bulk entropic contribution.

Method. The analysis uses a stochastic non-conserved order-parameter model, deriving the velocity via perturbation expansion in small parameters (temperature T and interface width h).

Model / system. The system is a planar interface separating two phases, modeled by a stochastic non-conserved order-parameter field $\phi$, subjected to a uniform shear flow $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r}) = (0, \dot{\gamma}x_1, 0)$.

Important parameters / regimes. Shear rate ($\dot{\gamma}$), Temperature ($T$), and the ratio of fluctuation amplitudes between phases.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on perturbation expansions in small parameters $T$ and $h$, and the calculation of the correlation function assumes a linearized bulk mode approximation.

Abstract

Thermal fluctuations can drive an interface when bulk fluctuation amplitudes differ between two phases. We study how shear flow modifies this mechanism using a stochastic non-conserved order- parameter model. For a planar interface parallel to the imposed shear, we derive its propagation velocity in the weak-noise and small-bias regime. The driving force comprises a shear-modified entropic contribution determined by bulk fluctuation spectra and a non-equilibrium contribution arising from the breaking of time-reversal symmetry. At low shear rates, the entropic term provides the dominant contribution to the velocity of an interface near the zero-velocity plane of the shear flow, and the shear-induced change in the velocity scales as the 4/3 power of the shear rate. At high shear rates, two-dimensional simulations show that the measured velocity is largely accounted for by the modified bulk entropic contribution.

Summary. This work analyzes how engineered dissipation controls boundary state localization in higher-order topological insulators. By projecting the system onto the edge subspace, the authors show that the dissipation couples uniformly to both edge modes, forcing them to localize together at a common dissipative domain wall. This mechanism offers a topological control knob for boundary physics without modifying the bulk band structure.

Why it may be interesting. The concept of dissipation controlling topological boundary physics is highly relevant to open quantum systems, as it provides a mechanism to engineer boundary behavior in systems where dissipation is unavoidable.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates how engineered boundary dissipation affects the localization of edge and corner states in Higher-Order Topological Insulators (HOTIs).

Main result. The dominant boundary dissipation couples to the kinetic term, causing both counter-propagating edge modes to localize at the same dissipative domain wall, which controls state localization without altering the bulk topology.

Method. The analysis employs projection onto the edge subspace, utilizing boundary transfer theory and fixed-energy Feshbach reduction techniques.

Model / system. The model is a Bernevig–Hughes–Zhang-type second-order topological insulator (SOTI) lattice, perturbed by inhomogeneous, spin-dependent boundary dissipation ($\delta H$).

Key observables. Edge state localization, corner state weight distribution, and the real transfer exponent ($\lambda_\pm$).

Important parameters / regimes. Dissipation strength ($\gamma_e(\ell)$), effective edge mass ($m_e(\ell)$), and the energy $E$.

Assumptions / limitations. The effective Hamiltonian is assumed valid when dissipation and mass terms are small compared to the bulk gap energy.

Figures summary. Not specified.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian and projecting to the edge subspace, developing the transfer theory for edge states, analyzing the zero-energy limit, and concluding with numerical confirmation of the localization mechanism.

Abstract

We study dissipation driven boundary localization in a Bernevig Hughes Zhang type second order topological insulator by introducing inhomogeneous, spin dependent boundary dissipation. After projection onto the edge subspace, the dominant component of the boundary dissipation lies in the same Pauli channel as the kinetic term. This channel gives both counter-propagating edge modes the same real transfer exponent. The two modes therefore accumulate at the same dissipative domain wall. For the corner states, the dissipative envelope competes with Jackiw Rebbi localization. Increasing dissipation moves their weight from the geometric corners to the dissipative domain wall. Propagating edge states also localize at this wall. Numerical calculations confirm the qualitative predictions of the edge theory. A fixed energy Feshbach reduction captures finite size effects. Inhomogeneous boundary dissipation thus controls boundary state localization without changing the bulk topology.

Summary. This paper analyzes the 'turnover effect' in quantum devices where energy currents unexpectedly drop to zero upon increasing coupling strength. Using scattering theory, the authors prove this blockage is due to total reflection preventing particle access to the interaction region. They propose a novel protocol involving adding an auxiliary energy level to bypass this limitation, thereby restoring and enhancing device performance.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses non-equilibrium transport phenomena in quantum devices, which is a major area of statistical mechanics and open quantum systems theory, providing concrete physical mechanisms for current limitation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper investigates the counterintuitive 'turnover effect' where energy or particle transport currents decrease to zero in strongly coupled non-equilibrium quantum devices.

Main result. The turnover effect is shown to be a consequence of total reflection impeding particle exchange, and this effect can be avoided and even overcome by engineering the system with an additional energy level.

Method. The study employs scattering theory, analyzing the system dynamics using scattering wavefunctions, and deriving steady-state currents via Pauli master equations.

Model / system. The model consists of a quantum system coupled to multiple baths (reservoirs) via localized potentials, analyzed in the context of non-equilibrium energy/particle flow.

Key observables. Heat current ($J_k$), wavefunction weight ($W_a$), transmission ($T_{lj}$), and reflection ($R_{lj}$) amplitudes.

Important parameters / regimes. Coupling strength ($g$), which is varied across weak to strong coupling regimes ($g o \infty$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the low-density regime for scattering events and utilizes scattering theory formalism.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the turnover of heat flow versus coupling strength, the suppression of wavepacket amplitude, and comparisons between current calculations for different numbers of coupled sites.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from defining the turnover effect using scattering theory, mathematically identifying total reflection as the cause, and finally proposing and demonstrating a protocol using an extra level to circumvent this blockage and enhance current flow.

Abstract

Under non-equilibrium conditions, energy/particle currents flow through a quantum system coupled to multiple baths. Although the fluxes increase in the weak coupling regime as the coupling strengthens, the reason why they decrease to zero for large coupling remains unknown. This counterintuitive behavior of energy exchange or transport phenomena is called the turnover effect. It has been predicted in several quantum systems such as photosynthetic complexes, mesoscopic junctions, quantum heat machines, and chemical networks without a single counterexample, and it results in constrained performance due to limited currents. Here, we use scattering theory to study the turnover effect produced by low-zdensity reservoirs of free particles scattered by a quantum system through localized potentials. We find that the turnover effect is a consequence of total reflection that impedes the reservoir particle from reaching the interaction region and exchanging energy with the quantum system. Moreover, we design a protocol based on quantum tunneling to avoid the turnover and to increase the maximum current. This strategy could be used to unleash the full potential of devices based on temperature/chemical potential gradients.
