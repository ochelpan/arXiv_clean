Programming anharmonic potentials in a superconducting harmonic oscillator

Authors: Clara Yun Fontaine, Mansi Somani, Kehui Yu, May Chee Loke, Jonathan Schwinger, Pak-Tik Fong, Ni-Ni Huang, Adrian Copetudo, Mustafa Bakr, Hoi-Kwan Lau, Tanjung Krisnanda, Yvonne Y. Gao

Summary. This paper presents a systematic method to program anharmonic potentials in a superconducting harmonic oscillator using a transmon qubit. By employing bosonic quantum signal processing, the authors successfully implemented and characterized various potentials, including cubic, double-well, and Morse forms. This establishes a practical, reconfigurable route for advanced continuous-variable quantum simulation.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete, hardware-validated pathway to engineer complex, non-Gaussian potentials in a superconducting circuit, which is crucial for simulating molecular dynamics and other non-linear quantum processes.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is realizing programmable non-Gaussian phase gates, which correspond to implementing anharmonic potentials, for continuous-variable quantum simulation.

Main result. The authors successfully demonstrated a systematic framework to engineer and characterize anharmonic potentials, including cubic, double-well, and approximate Morse potentials, on a superconducting circuit.

Method. They use bosonic quantum signal processing (QSP) to compile the target phase gate into a circuit of qubit rotations and oscillator displacements, followed by pointwise force reconstruction from coherent-state probes.

Model / system. The platform is a superconducting harmonic oscillator coupled dispersively to a transmon qubit (bosonic cQED). The system is used to simulate potentials $V(\hat{X})$ via the gate $e^{-iV(\hat{X})}$.

Key observables. Gate fidelity, Wigner negativity, and the reconstructed force profile $V'(\hat{X})$ are key diagnostics used to validate the engineered potentials.

Important parameters / regimes. The fidelity is high ($\ge 0.84$), and the reconstruction is validated by comparing the measured force profile against the target potential, especially for the cubic and double-well cases.

Assumptions / limitations. The reconstruction fidelity is limited by the finite bandwidth of the Gaussian deconvolution process, and the process relies on the dispersive coupling interaction.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the modular circuit diagram for implementing various non-Gaussian gates (cubic, double-well, Morse) and compare the reconstructed force profiles against the target potentials.

Paper structure. The paper systematically demonstrates the framework by first implementing a cubic phase gate, then engineering and validating double-well potentials relevant for tunneling, and finally achieving an approximate Morse gate, while also testing parity symmetry.

Abstract

Continuous-variable quantum systems offer a resource-efficient route to universal quantum information processing and analogue quantum simulation of real-world processes, such as molecular physics and chemical reactions. Realising these applications, however, requires non-Gaussian operations that implement anharmonic potentials, which are challenging to engineer on demand. Here, we demonstrate a systematic framework to implement programmable non-Gaussian phase gates $e^{-iV(\hat{X})}$, corresponding to the impulsive action of a potential $V(\hat{X})$, in a superconducting harmonic oscillator coupled to a transmon qubit. Using modular circuits derived from bosonic quantum signal processing, we realise a range of target anharmonic potentials on a single piece of hardware by varying a set of qubit rotations interleaved with a fixed calibrated control unitary. We first demonstrate a cubic phase gate, a key ingredient for universal quantum information processing. The resulting high-fidelity non-Gaussian states and the potential reconstructed using our pointwise force reconstruction method jointly confirm the cubic nature of the target gate. We then engineer a family of double-well potentials, relevant models of tunnelling and biased transfer processes, and experimentally validate the double-well topology and the tunable asymmetry. Finally, we engineer an approximate Morse gate, a step towards realistic potentials of molecular vibrational systems, and provide a concrete path towards high-quality engineering and reconstruction of the exponential form. Together, these results establish a practical and reconfigurable route towards continuous-variable quantum information processing and anharmonic quantum simulation.

Bell inequality violation with momentum-entangled massive particles

Summary. This experiment reports the first violation of a Bell inequality using the momentum states of massive particles, specifically metastable helium atoms. By employing a dual-resonant Bragg interferometer, the researchers achieved independent control over local measurement bases for momentum-entangled pairs. The measured Bell parameter $S=2.52$ confirms quantum nonlocality in the external degrees of freedom of atoms.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly extends quantum nonlocality tests from internal quantum variables (like spin) to the external, motional degrees of freedom of massive particles, providing a crucial testbed for quantum mechanics in the context of matter waves and potentially gravity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal was to demonstrate the violation of a Bell inequality using the motional (external) degrees of freedom of massive particles, an experimental frontier in quantum nonlocality testing.

Main result. The authors successfully measured a CHSH Bell parameter of $S = 2.52 \pm 0.17$, significantly violating the local realistic bound ($S \le 2$), confirming quantum nonlocality in atomic momentum states.

Method. The experiment utilized a matter-wave interferometer sequence (Rarity-Tapster type) applied to momentum-entangled pairs of metastable helium atoms.

Model / system. The platform involves ultracold metastable helium atoms ($ ext{He}^*$) prepared in a Bose-Einstein condensate. The key technical advance is the use of a dual-resonant Bragg lattice, which allows for the independent selection of local measurement bases for the two spatially separated momentum modes.

Key observables. The Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) Bell parameter ($S$), the Bell correlation function $E(\phi_A, \phi_B)$, and joint atom detection probabilities $P_{k,k'}$.

Important parameters / regimes. The measured value $S = 2.52 \pm 0.17$, the local realistic bound $S \le 2$, and the visibility $V = 0.93(2)$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the two visibility reduction effects (Higher-Order Fock states and finite detection window) are independent, and the scattering process is in the low-gain regime.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the experimental schematic, the Rarity-Tapster atom interferometer sequence, and measurements of the Bell correlation function $E(\phi_A, \phi_B)$ showing clear phase dependence.

Paper structure. The paper details the experimental setup, focusing on the dual-resonant Bragg lattice to enable independent phase control. It then describes the measurement of joint detection probabilities, constructs the Bell correlation function, and finally calculates the CHSH parameter to demonstrate the violation.

Abstract

Bell's theorem revealed the fundamental incompatibility between the predictions of quantum mechanics and local realism. Bell inequality violations have since demonstrated quantum nonlocality using photons and internal states of massive particles, but never using their motional states. Here we report the first Bell inequality violation in the motional states of massive particles. Using momentum-entangled pairs of metastable helium atoms manipulated by matter-wave interferometry, we measure a Clauser-Horne-Shimony-Holt (CHSH) Bell parameter of $S = 2.52 \pm 0.17$, violating the CHSH-Bell inequality ($S \le 2$). Our work completes a long-standing objective in quantum atom optics by extending Bell tests from internal quantum variables to the external degrees of freedom of massive particles, opening a new regime for exploring quantum nonlocality in matter waves and for investigating the interplay between quantum mechanics and gravity.

Thermodynamic optimization of thermal landscapes and energy barriers in a Brownian heat engine

Summary. This paper revolutionizes the study of Brownian heat engines by formulating an inverse-design problem: instead of calculating performance for a given temperature profile, it determines the optimal profile. It rigorously shows that the optimal design differs significantly between the ideal quasistatic limit and the realistic finite-current regime. This provides powerful tools for engineering non-equilibrium energy conversion devices.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a rigorous framework for optimizing non-equilibrium steady states by treating the thermal landscape itself as a control variable, which is a key concept in open quantum systems and energy harvesting.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper tackles the inverse-design problem in Brownian heat engines: determining the optimal temperature profile and energy barrier to maximize a chosen thermodynamic objective.

Main result. While the quasistatic limit yields an efficiency optimum using a hot-uphill/cold-downhill profile, finite current optimization requires a different profile, governed by a balance between thermal rectification gain and transport resistance.

Method. The authors derive exact functional gradients and box-constrained optimality conditions by analyzing the stationary current and probability density derived from the Smoluchowski equation.

Model / system. The system is an overdamped Brownian particle moving in a symmetric triangular periodic potential under a constant opposing load, subjected to a spatially varying temperature field T(x).

Key observables. Efficiency ($\eta$), stationary current ($J$), power, and configurational Shannon Entropy.

Important parameters / regimes. Temperature bounds ($T_c, T_h$), opposing load, and the ratio of cycle affinity to transport resistance.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the system can be controlled by setting the temperature profile T(x) and the barrier height $U_0$ over the admissible class.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the symmetric triangular potential and compares three canonical thermal landscapes (piecewise constant, etc.).

Paper structure. The paper moves from deriving the exact stationary current and density for arbitrary T(x) to analyzing the quasistatic limit, and finally to deriving the functional gradient and optimality conditions for finite current optimization.

Abstract

Spatial temperature fields in Brownian heat engines are commonly prescribed \emph{a priori}, and the resulting transport and thermodynamic properties are then calculated. Here we formulate the complementary inverse-design problem: determining the temperature profile and barrier height that optimize a chosen thermodynamic objective. We consider an overdamped Brownian particle in a symmetric triangular periodic potential under a constant opposing load and derive the exact stationary current and probability density for an arbitrary bounded temperature field, $\Tc\le T(x)\le\Th$. In the quasistatic limit, the efficiency becomes an exact functional of two inverse-temperature integrals over the uphill and downhill branches. Its rigorous global maximum under the pointwise temperature bounds is $η_{\max}=1-\Tc/\Th$, attained uniquely, up to sets of measure zero, by the hot-uphill/cold-downhill piecewise-constant profile. At finite current, however, the optimization changes qualitatively because the current is determined jointly by the cycle affinity and a nonlocal transport resistance. We derive the exact functional gradient and the corresponding box-constrained optimality conditions, showing that the current- or power-maximizing profile generally differs from the quasistatic efficiency optimum. For any prescribed temperature field, the current-maximizing barrier satisfies an exact balance between the marginal gain in thermal rectification and the marginal increase in transport resistance, with the characteristic estimate $U_0^*\simeq T_{\rm act}$, where $T_{\rm act}^{-1}=(2/L)\int_0^{L/2}\dd x/T(x)$.

Summary. This paper generalizes a classic random parking problem to model the random deposition of isosceles trapeziums on a strip. It derives an exact formula for the jamming limit and proves that the variance of the process converges to the same leading asymptotic density regardless of the specific geometry of the remaining substrate. This provides deep insights into the geometry-dependent nature of random packing processes.

Why it may be interesting. While the topic is classical statistical mechanics (packing/jamming), the rigorous mathematical techniques involving recursive relations, asymptotic analysis, and the study of limiting densities are highly relevant to understanding critical phenomena and non-equilibrium dynamics in quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper generalizes Rényi's car-parking problem to study the random sequential deposition of isosceles trapeziums on a strip.

Main result. An explicit formula for the jamming constant is derived, showing that the least efficient shape is a genuine trapezium, and the asymptotic variance for related processes is shown to be identical for two different substrate geometries.

Method. The analysis uses recursive constructions and moment calculations to derive finite-size asymptotics for the expected number and variance of deposited particles.

Model / system. The model involves depositing isosceles trapeziums randomly on a strip substrate, leading to a recursive process that generates two distinct, but asymptotically equivalent, substrate geometries: a parallelogram and a trapezium.

Key observables. Parking Constant ($ heta$), Expected Number ($\mu(x)$), and Variance ($\sigma^2(x)$) of deposited trapeziums.

Important parameters / regimes. The lengths of the two bases of the trapezium ($a$ and $b$), and the substrate size ($x$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the substrate is a strip and relies on the asymptotic equivalence of the two resulting substrate types ($P_x$ and $T_x$).

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the strip substrate, different deposited shapes (triangles, rectangles, trapeziums), and the two relevant recursive substrate geometries (parallelogram and trapezium).

Paper structure. The paper establishes the general problem, derives the explicit formula for the parking constant, calculates finite-size asymptotics for moments, and proves the asymptotic equality of the variance for the two underlying substrate types.

Abstract

In this article, we study a generalisation of Rényi's car-parking problem in which isosceles trapeziums are sequentially deposited on a strip. We derive an explicit formula for the parking constant in terms of the lengths of the two bases, recovering the classical rectangular and triangular models as special cases. We further obtain quantitative finite-size asymptotics for both the expected number and the variance of deposited trapeziums, with convergence rates that depend explicitly on the geometry of the deposited particle. In particular, although the recursive construction involves two different substrate geometries, their variances have the same leading asymptotic density. Finally, we show that the parking constant depends non-monotonically on the ratio of the two base lengths and possesses a unique minimiser, so that the least efficient shape is a genuine trapezium rather than a triangle.

Summary. This paper presents a sophisticated numerical simulation of quantum annealing dynamics using time-dependent variational Monte Carlo (t-VMC). By employing advanced sampling techniques like Parallel Tempering and Blurred Sampling, the authors successfully simulate spin-glass models on complex graphs. The results establish a rigorous classical benchmark for assessing the computational feasibility and accuracy of simulating quantum hardware performance.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a highly detailed, state-of-the-art classical benchmark for simulating quantum annealing dynamics, offering crucial insights into the numerical limitations and required resources for simulating quantum advantage experiments.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the computational challenge of accurately simulating the non-equilibrium dynamics of frustrated quantum spin systems, specifically those relevant to quantum annealing experiments.

Main result. The authors successfully simulate the D-Wave annealing protocol on complex topologies, achieving correlation errors comparable to the quantum annealer results, even for challenging instances where other methods fail.

Method. They employ Time-Dependent Variational Monte Carlo (t-VMC) using a structured correlator state ansatz, stabilized by advanced sampling techniques like Parallel Tempering and Blurred Sampling.

Model / system. The system is a transverse-field Ising model defined on various spin-glass topologies (e.g., 2D cylinder, biclique). The dynamics are simulated by evolving the variational state according to the time-dependent variational principle.

Key observables. Two-spin Ising correlations ($C_{ij}$), relative correlation error ($\epsilon_c$), and numerical diagnostics like MCMC acceptance rates and TDVP residuals ($R_2$).

Important parameters / regimes. Annealing times ($t_a = 7, 20$ ns), variational ansatz rank ($R$), and regularization parameters ($ ext{rcond}$).

Assumptions / limitations. The primary approximation is projecting the full quantum dynamics onto a variational manifold defined by the correlator product state ansatz. Numerical stability requires advanced sampling and ODE solvers.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the simulation setup, the annealing schedule, the ansatz structure, and diagnostic plots comparing correlation errors ($\epsilon_c$) across different topologies and the impact of advanced sampling techniques.

Paper structure. The paper details the theoretical framework (t-VMC, ansatz construction), presents the numerical implementation challenges (sampling difficulties, stiffness), and validates the method by simulating dynamics on increasingly complex and challenging spin-glass graph structures.

Abstract

Programmable quantum annealers can realize real-time dynamics of frustrated transverse-field Ising models on large, nontrivial graphs. Recent work by King et al. argued that the classical simulation of such experiments would require exponential computational resources for classical methods such as tensor networks and neural quantum states. Here, we numerically simulate the D-Wave spin-glass annealing protocol with time-dependent variational Monte Carlo (t-VMC) using a correlator state tailored to spin-glass dynamics. For the two-dimensional cylinder, three-dimensional dimer, diamond, and biclique instances considered, at annealing times of 7 and 20 ns, we show that systematically increasing the variational ansatz size enables t-VMC to approximate the final two-spin correlation errors of the quantum processing unit (QPU). We also perform an accurate large-scale simulation of a challenging biclique instance for which no other variational method is known to work, and we find close agreement with the quantum annealer. Through ablation studies, we identify poor Markov-chain mixing, high-variance local-energy estimators, and stochastic Runge-Kutta error estimates as the principal numerical failure modes. We address these numerical issues by using parallel tempering, blurred sampling and an importance-weighted differential equation solver, thereby clarifying the numerical requirements for stable, large-scale t-VMC simulations. Our results extend the frontier of classical simulation while providing a realistic assessment of the computational costs of simulating quantum dynamics at this scale.

Summary. This paper details a method to improve the sensitivity of axion dark matter searches in microwave cavities by exploiting the non-monochromatic nature of the axion signal. By carefully tuning the amplifier to operate symmetrically around the cavity resonance, the measured signal power is effectively doubled while the vacuum noise remains constant. This results in a significant reduction of the effective quantum noise limit, enhancing the search capability beyond the standard quantum limit.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant to quantum optics and open quantum systems, as it provides a concrete, quantum-limited technique to surpass standard noise constraints in a real-world detection scenario.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to enhance the sensitivity of axion dark matter searches conducted using microwave cavity haloscopes, which are currently limited by the Standard Quantum Limit (SQL).

Main result. By exploiting the non-monochromatic nature of the axion signal and operating the amplifier at the half-pump frequency, the effective noise limit can be reduced to $1/\sqrt{2}$ quanta after optimal matched filtering, improving the SNR by a factor of two.

Method. The authors use quadrature analysis (homodyne readout) on the output of a Josephson Parametric Amplifier (JPA) to combine signal and idler spectral components.

Model / system. The system involves axion-photon conversion measured in high-Q microwave cavities, amplified by a JPA. The analysis models the output Power Spectral Density (PSD) by considering the symmetric population of signal and idler bands.

Key observables. Output Power Spectral Density (PSD), Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), and the effective quantum noise limit (quanta per frequency bin).

Important parameters / regimes. High cavity quality factor ($Q_c \gg Q_a$), operating at the half-pump frequency, and the resulting noise reduction factor of $1/\sqrt{2}$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes the cavity quality factor is much larger than the axion's bandwidth ($Q_c \gg Q_a$) and relies on the signal symmetrically populating both signal and idler bands.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes, but the analysis compares the PSD in non-degenerate ($ u_c eq 0$) versus quasi-degenerate ($ u_c = 0$) resonance regimes.

Paper structure. The paper establishes the SQL constraint, develops the theoretical framework using JPA output PSD calculations, compares the non-degenerate and quasi-degenerate regimes, and derives the improved noise limit for the latter.

Abstract

The search for wave-like dark matter using microwave cavity haloscopes is constrained by the Standard Quantum Limit, which dictates that phase-preserving linear amplification results in a minimum of one quantum of total system noise for a narrow-band signal. We demonstrate that this limit is effectively halved for a non-monochromatic axion signal coupled to a high-$Q$ cavity. By operating a Josephson Parametric Amplifier such that the cavity resonance is centered exactly at the half-pump frequency, the axion signal symmetrically populates both the signal and idler bands. Through quadrature analysis of the homodyne readout, we show that the incoherent sum of these mirrored spectral components doubles the measured signal power while the vacuum noise remains constant. This operation yields an effective noise limit of 0.5 quanta per frequency bin, translating to an overall effective limit of $1/\sqrt{2}$ quanta after optimal matched filtering.

Summary. This work models a complex dissipative optomechanical system to demonstrate that a synthetic-flux phase can precisely control the degree of hyperchaos. Main result. The synthetic-flux phase successfully controls the hyperchaos order up to four unstable Lyapunov directions, but the matched-resource sensing protocol yields no flux-induced enhancement on the chaotic attractor.

Method. The analysis employs advanced dynamical systems diagnostics, including calculating the full Lyapunov spectrum, analyzing the Poincaré map via monodromy multipliers, and evaluating the matched Fisher information.

Model / system. The system is a dissipative optomechanical model comprising one cavity and two mechanical resonators, analyzed using semiclassical equations of motion derived from a Hamiltonian.

Important parameters / regimes. Synthetic-flux phase ($\Phi_{syn}$), Drive amplitude ($E$), Coupling strengths ($g_1, g_2$), and damping rates ($\kappa, \gamma$).

Abstract

Within a normalised six-dimensional model of dissipative optomechanics (one cavity + two mechanical resonators), a synthetic-flux phase $Φ_{\rm syn}$ selects the \emph{order} of a drive- and coupling-gated hyperchaos transition---up to four simultaneously unstable Lyapunov directions, beyond any reported single-mode benchmark---while the same matched-resource force-sensing protocol yields no flux-induced enhancement on the chaotic attractor. Building on the topology of Muthukumar \emph{et al.}~[PR Applied \textbf{24}, 014053 (2025)], a phase-consistent Floquet--Lyapunov protocol (cross-checked by monodromy multipliers, dissipative volume balance and a 180-run three-seed audit) localises a Neimark--Sacker bifurcation at $E^{*}=\num{1.060}$ ($θ=0$). At a weakly coupled reference the matched Fisher gain reaches at most $\num{1.32}\times$ (flux-off) and $\num{1.16}\times$ (single-mode), with Monte-Carlo median $\num{1.039}\times$ (90\,\% CI $[\num{1.025},\num{1.053}]$); on the chaotic attractor the identical protocol returns a null result ($\mathcal{G}_{A/B}=\num{1.039}\pm\num{0.014}$). Truncated-Fock and truncated-Wigner checks support the mean-field description at selected points. Both the hyperchaos classification and the sensing result remain strictly model-level: the strong-coupling sector explored here lies $\num{2542}\times$ beyond anchored silicon optomechanical couplings. Closing that gap requires a measured inter-resonator hopping $J_m$ and fixed bath temperatures.

Summary. This paper introduces a novel single-shot fidelity metric to rigorously evaluate Photon-Number-Resolving (PNR) detectors. It overcomes the limitations of standard ensemble averaging by explicitly separating hardware efficiency loss from errors caused by finite energy resolution. The resulting metric allows for the first direct, quantitative comparison of diverse detector technologies for use in photonic quantum computing.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a crucial, quantitative benchmark for quantum hardware, directly linking fundamental detector physics (resolution) to the operational requirements of quantum computation.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Conventional fidelity metrics for Photon-Number-Resolving (PNR) detectors are insufficient because they fail to quantify single-shot misidentification errors arising from finite energy resolution.

Main result. The authors introduce a single-shot fidelity metric that separates hardware efficiency loss from resolution-driven misidentification, allowing for a universal, direct comparison across different detector types.

Method. They combine an efficiency-based POVM with a resolution-driven confusion matrix to construct a single-shot measurement probability, $P_{ss}(n|k)$.

Model / system. The study focuses on PNR detectors essential for photonic quantum computing, comparing architectures like Transition-Edge Sensors (TES) and SNSPDs.

Key observables. Single-shot fidelity ($F_k$), detection efficiency ($\eta$), and the resolution confusion matrix ($\mathbf{R}_{ab}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Photon number ($k$), detection efficiency ($\eta$), and the acceptance region ($ au$) used to define measurement boundaries.

Assumptions / limitations. The framework assumes the detector output retains a gradient of confidence about the true photon number, and Gaussian approximations are used for modeling detector responses.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the construction of the single-shot POVM, showing how the composition of efficiency and resolution matrices yields the final fidelity, and compare $F_k$ vs. $k$ for different detector types and operating regimes.

Abstract

Photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors are essential for photonic quantum computing, where a single measurement outcome must reliably herald a specific quantum state. However, detector fidelity is conventionally evaluated using ensemble-averaged statistics obtained from many measurements, which can remain high even when individual photon-number assignments are frequently misidentified. Here we introduce a universal single-shot fidelity that directly quantifies the probability of correctly identifying a photon number in a single measurement. The framework combines an efficiency-based POVM with a resolution-driven confusion matrix derived from the detector response, allowing photon loss and photon-number misidentification to be treated separately and then recombined into a single operational metric. This distinction reveals two fundamentally different limitations. Detection-efficiency loss represents an unrecoverable hardware constraint, whereas resolution-driven misidentification can be reduced by introducing a rejection region, trading generation rate for confidence. Because the metric is defined independently of detector architecture, it enables direct comparison between energy-resolving detectors such as transition-edge sensors and multiplexed click-based detectors on the same footing. Applying the framework to calibration data from three distinct detector architectures, we demonstrate quantitative comparison across photon-number regimes relevant to both discrete-variable and continuous-variable photonic quantum computing. The resulting benchmark provides a common operational metric for evaluating photon-number-resolving detectors and connecting detector performance to photonic quantum-computing requirements.

Abstract

Teleportation performance is commonly characterized by the average teleportation fidelity, while its variation over input states provides additional information captured by the fidelity deviation. In controlled teleportation, the controller's measurement introduces an additional source of fidelity variation through its measurement outcomes. We investigate the fidelity deviation in controlled teleportation with three-qubit pure states. We derive a lower bound on the fidelity deviation and show that it is attainable together with the maximal average teleportation fidelity. Among the measurements attaining the maximal average fidelity, however, the fidelity deviation can vary depending on the controller's measurement. Abstract

Abstract

Simulating quantum many-body systems has been one of the most important applications of quantum computation. For simulation, the Hamiltonian of a physical system is compiled into quantum programs with native instructions for quantum hardware. In previous works, the Hamiltonian is represented as Pauli strings, then compiled and optimized based on the quantum circuit model. Such representation paradigm neglects the spatial topology of original physical models, which is vital information to reducing the overhead of compiling many-body systems Hamiltonians. To address such neglect, we introduce a spatial-topology preserving compiler for quantum many-body simulation. Using Pauli gadgets as the representations of the Hamiltonian, we introduce our intermediate representation -- GadIR, to preserve the spatial-topology information of original physical models. Our compiler frontend performs the group reduction algorithm based on Pauli gadget model, which is a hardware-independent optimization. Our compiler backend performs trotterization and scheduling on Pauli gadgets, then synthesizes the Pauli gadgets into hardware-native quantum programs. We evaluate our compiler on all the canonical quantum many-body system models, while achieving a significant reduction on compilation overhead regarding four major quantum architectures. Overall, our spatial-topology preserving IR exploits the compilation optimization space for quantum many-body systems Hamiltonian.
