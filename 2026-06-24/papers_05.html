Discovery of connectivity-trainability trade-off of IQP Circuits for Hamiltonian Optimization

Authors: Quoc Chuong Nguyen
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24264v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper analyzes how the connectivity structure of IQP quantum circuits affects their ability to solve complex Hamiltonian optimization problems. It establishes a crucial trade-off: highly expressive, fully connected circuits are prone to vanishing gradients (barren plateaus), while less expressive but stable circuits perform poorly. The authors conclude that an intermediate connectivity, like circular coupling, offers the most robust balance for near-term quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly addresses a critical bottleneck in variational quantum algorithms—the conflict between achieving high representational power (expressivity) and maintaining gradients that allow for efficient classical optimization (trainability).

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the fundamental trade-off between optimization performance (expressivity) and training stability (trainability) when using Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial-time (IQP) circuits for solving Hamiltonian optimization problems.

Main result. A clear trade-off exists: fully connected circuits offer high expressivity but suffer from rapid gradient decay (barren plateaus), while single-Z circuits are stable but under-expressive; circular connectivity provides the best balance.

Method. The authors systematically compared three IQP circuit architectures (single-Z, circular, full) by minimizing expectation values of benchmark Hamiltonians and analyzing gradient variance scaling with system size.

Model / system. The model uses IQP circuits as variational ansätze to approximate the ground state energy of various combinatorial Hamiltonians, such as Ising or MaxCut models. The analysis focuses on how circuit connectivity dictates the optimization landscape's properties.

Key observables. Relative approximation ratio (optimization performance), gradient variance decay rate (trainability/barren plateau susceptibility), expressibility (Jensen-Shannon distance), and bipartite entanglement entropy.

Important parameters / regimes. Circuit connectivity structure (Single-Z, Circular, Full); System size (number of qubits $n$); Optimization parameters ($ heta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the trade-off between expressivity and trainability is a fundamental constraint for near-term quantum advantage in optimization tasks.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate how connectivity affects expressibility (approaching Haar-random behavior) and entanglement entropy, while gradient variance plots show full connectivity decaying fastest, indicating barren plateaus.

Paper structure. The paper systematically introduces IQP circuits for Hamiltonian optimization, analyzes performance metrics across different connectivities using benchmark Hamiltonians, and concludes by mapping out the necessary balance between expressivity and trainability required for practical quantum algorithms.

Abstract

Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial-time (IQP) circuits are promising candidates for near-term quantum advantage due to the conjectured classical hardness of their sampling task. However, their capabilities for optimization remain largely unexplored. We present a systematic investigation of the performance and trainability of IQP circuits for Hamiltonian optimization. Our results reveal a trade-off between optimization performance and circuit connectivity, demonstrating that the circuit structure plays a key role in determining the ability of IQP circuits to reach low-energy states.

On the Limits of Stretching Quantum Pseudorandomness

Authors: Boyang Chen, Andrea Coladangelo, Yao-Ting Lin, Nikos Skoumios, Justin Tysdal, Yiming Wang
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24736v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: entanglement & information structure 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This paper rigorously proves a black-box separation demonstrating that quantum pseudorandom states cannot be stretched to arbitrary polynomial lengths. By constructing an adversary oracle, the authors show that 1PRS outputs are fundamentally limited in how much their output length can increase relative to their input key size. This result places strong theoretical constraints on the efficiency and scalability of quantum randomness sources.

Why it may be interesting. While highly abstract, this work establishes fundamental computational limits on quantum randomness generation, which underpins many theoretical models for quantum computation security.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining if quantum pseudorandom states (1PRS) can be stretched to arbitrarily large polynomial output lengths in a black-box setting, analogous to classical PRGs.

Main result. The authors prove a fundamental black-box separation showing that 1PRS cannot be stretched from a short length ($m(n)=1.1n$) to an arbitrarily long one ($\Omega(n^{2+\epsilon})$).

Method. The proof constructs a quantum oracle and utilizes advanced techniques involving the Common Haar Random State (CHRS) model, SWAP tests, and OR testers to distinguish between 1PRS of different output lengths.

Model / system. The work operates within the framework of quantum information theory, focusing on Single-Copy Secure Pseudorandom States (1PRS) generated relative to a Common Haar Random State (CHRS).

Key observables. Distinguishing advantage between 1PRS outputs and Haar random states; the required output length $m(n)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The stretch factor, comparing linear growth ($1.1n$) against super-polynomial growth ($\Omega(n^{2+\epsilon})$); the security parameter $n$.

Assumptions / limitations. The separation is established relative to a constructed quantum oracle and holds under specific black-box access models (e.g., isometry access).

Figures summary. Figure 1 visually categorizes different theoretical cases (Case A and Case B) based on the lengths of available CHRS states ($i$) and the number of copies ($\ell(i)$) used by the generator.

Paper structure. The paper builds its argument through several stages: first, establishing the general black-box separation; second, analyzing specific cases (A and B) based on resource availability; and finally, combining these analyses into a unified adversary that proves the impossibility of stretching.

Abstract

Pseudorandom states, introduced by Ji, Liu, and Song (CRYPTO '18), are quantum analogues of classical pseudorandom generators. A fundamental property of classical pseudorandom generators is that their output can be stretched to arbitrary polynomial length. Whether an analogous stretching property holds for quantum pseudorandom states remains unclear. In this work, we prove the first black-box separation between single-copy secure pseudorandom states ($\mathsf{1PRS}$) with different output lengths. Specifically, we construct a quantum oracle relative to which $\mathsf{1PRS}$ with output length $m(n)=1.1n$ exist, but $\mathsf{1PRS}$ with output length $m(n)=Ω(n^{2+ε})$ do not, for any $ε>0$. Our proof leverages the Common Haar Random State (CHRS) model introduced by Chen, Coladangelo, and Sattath (EUROCRYPT '25), and introduces a technique to bound the effective number of resource CHRS states utilized by any $\mathsf{1PRS}$ generator in this model.

Summary. The paper introduces a novel framework using differential moment relaxations to rigorously certify the limitations of finite-time quantum control. It calculates upper bounds on achievable fidelities and lower bounds on minimum required times for tasks like state preparation and gate synthesis. This provides 'hardware-aware' speed limits, distinguishing true physical constraints from mere optimization shortcomings.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides powerful, mathematically rigorous tools for quantum control that move beyond purely numerical optimization by providing certified limits on performance, which is crucial for designing fault-tolerant quantum hardware protocols.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is developing rigorous mathematical certificates for determining the maximum achievable fidelity (reachability) and the minimum time required to reach a target state or gate in constrained quantum control.

Main result. The authors establish differential moment hierarchies that provide computable upper bounds on terminal fidelities and corresponding lower bounds on optimal control times, thereby certifying dynamical limitations.

Method. The framework employs moment relaxations applied to noncommutative polynomial optimization problems with implicitly time-dependent differential constraints, formulated as Semidefinite Programs (SDPs).

Model / system. The work analyzes controlled quantum dynamics governed by Hamiltonians $H(t) = H_0 + \sum u_j(t)H_j$, applied to tasks like state preparation (Bell/GHZ states), gate synthesis ($ ext{SU}(2)$ evolution), and excitation transfer in spin chains.

Key observables. Terminal fidelities ($F_{\Phi^+}, F_{ ext{gate}}, F_{ ext{tr}}$) are the primary observables, alongside the optimal control time $T^\star$.

Important parameters / regimes. Control constraints ($\sum u_j^2 \le U_{\max}^2$), relaxation order ($d$), and target fidelity threshold ($\eta$).

Assumptions / limitations. The method relies on moment relaxation techniques, which provide upper bounds; the quality of these bounds depends on the chosen relaxation order $d$.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the geometry of reachable sets, showing how nested convex outer approximations (from the hierarchy) bound the true set, and compare certified lower/upper bounds for state preparation fidelity vs. time.

Paper structure. The paper develops a unified framework by first establishing the mathematical machinery (moment relaxations with differential constraints), then applying it to specific physical tasks: state preparation, gate synthesis, and state transfer, demonstrating how structural symmetries reduce computational complexity.

Abstract

Finite-time control is central to quantum technologies, yet rigorous limits on reachable targets and optimal control times remain largely unknown. We develop a framework for finite-time reachability and optimal-time certificates in constrained quantum control based on moment relaxations with implicitly time-dependent differential constraints. For fixed control horizons and control constraints, the method yields rigorous upper bounds on achievable terminal fidelities, lower bounds on the optimal control times required to reach them, and certificate gaps for benchmarking explicit control pulses. We demonstrate the versatility of our framework in three use cases: entangled-state preparation in two and three qubits, one-qubit gate synthesis across different control geometries, and excitation transfer in an $N$-qubit $XX$ chain. Our work establishes differential moment hierarchies as a practical tool for certifying reachability limits and optimal control times in quantum control, providing hardware-aware quantum speed limits while highlighting structure exploitation as a key ingredient for scalable certification.

Summary. This paper presents Structured Concept Evolution (SCE), an LLM-guided evolutionary framework designed to overcome the difficulty of constructing good quantum LDPC codes. By allowing the LLM to mutate high-level algebraic concepts rather than raw matrices, the method discovers diverse and competitive code families over both abelian and non-abelian groups. The results demonstrate a powerful AI tool for advancing the theoretical design of fault-tolerant quantum computation.

Why it may be interesting. While focused on quantum error correction codes rather than AMO dynamics, the underlying principle of using advanced AI (LLMs) to navigate complex, high-dimensional combinatorial search spaces for physical constraints is highly relevant to designing optimal quantum control or simulation protocols in AMO physics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary challenge is discovering high-performing quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes because the search space for their construction is vast and discrete.

Main result. The authors successfully used an LLM-guided evolutionary framework to discover diverse, competitive families of qLDPC codes spanning both abelian and non-abelian group structures.

Method. They introduced Structured Concept Evolution (SCE), which pairs a Large Language Model with a structured algebraic mutation grammar to evolve code concepts at three hierarchical levels: local entries, protograph shape, and base group algebra.

Model / system. The focus is on constructing CSS quantum LDPC codes using the lifted-product construction based on finite groups G. These codes are evaluated for error correction capability against depolarizing noise.

Key observables. Logical error rate ($\epsilon_L$) vs. physical error rate ($p$), encoding rate ($k/n$), and fitness scores derived from decoding performance (BP+OSD).

Important parameters / regimes. Physical error rates $p \in \{0.05, 0.06, 0.07\}$; code parameters $[[n, k, d]]$; stabilizer weight threshold $\le 14$.

Assumptions / limitations. The search relies on LLMs (GPT-5.4 variants) acting as intelligent mutation operators constrained by a structured algebraic grammar.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the SCE framework flow, showing parent-to-child mutations across three levels (L1, L2, L3), and plot the block-length dependence of logical failure rates for various discovered code families.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem, details the SCE methodology involving structured concepts and hierarchical mutation grammar, presents results showing diverse code discovery spanning abelian to non-abelian groups, and concludes with performance comparisons across different noise models.

Abstract

Quantum computers could outperform classical machines on important problems, but only if the errors that pervade quantum hardware can be corrected at scale. Quantum low-density parity-check (qLDPC) codes offer a promising route to this goal by combining sparse parity checks with finite encoding rate and growing distance, but their construction remains a challenging discrete design problem. Here we introduce structured concept evolution (SCE), a search framework that pairs a large language model with a structured algebraic mutation grammar to discover lifted-product code families, a class of CSS qLDPC codes. Instead of asking the LLM to design codes from first principles, SCE evolves structured concepts consisting of algebraic specifications paired with executable programs that realize them, using hierarchical mutations that modify the group algebra, protograph geometry, or base space. Running SCE, we discover a diverse set of competitive code families, ranging from abelian constructions to families over non-abelian groups beyond those underlying standard designs such as bivariate-bicycle codes, and characterize them under code-capacity depolarizing noise with BP+OSD decoding. These results are obtained with lightweight models (GPT-5.4-mini and GPT-5.4-nano).

Summary. The paper introduces Augmented Imaginary-Time Evolution (AITE) to accelerate the notoriously slow convergence of standard imaginary-time evolution algorithms. By incorporating local geometric information derived from energy distribution statistics, AITE achieves superlinear convergence leading to finite-time error extinction. This provides a significant algorithmic improvement for simulating ground states in quantum many-body systems.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful algorithmic acceleration technique for finding ground states in strongly correlated systems, directly impacting computational methods used in condensed matter physics simulations.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Standard imaginary-time evolution (ITE) for ground-state preparation converges only asymptotically, leading to slow convergence rates.

Main result. The proposed Augmented Imaginary-Time Evolution (AITE) framework achieves superlinear convergence and exhibits finite-time extinction of the energy error, significantly outperforming standard ITE.

Method. AITE augments the standard gradient flow by incorporating local geometric information derived from higher-order statistical moments (like skewness) of the instantaneous energy distribution into the descent direction.

Model / system. The methods are applied to general quantum many-body systems, with specific numerical tests performed on the H8 chain and the Fermi-Hubbard model. The evolution is governed by imaginary time ($ au$) under a Hamiltonian $\hat{H}$.

Key observables. Energy error $E( au) - E_{ ext{GS}}$, energy variance ($\mu_2$), and skewness coefficient ($\kappa$).

Important parameters / regimes. The spectral gap ($\Delta = E_1 - E_{GS}$), the imaginary time $ au$, and the target precision $\epsilon^*$.

Assumptions / limitations. Practical implementation restricts the search for optimal descent operators to the second-order Krylov subspace, and mean-field approximations are used for higher moments.

Figures summary. Figures compare energy error vs. time ($ au$) curves, showing AITE's power-law extinction against ITE's asymptotic exponential decay across various models (H8 chain, Fermi-Hubbard).

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem with standard ITE, develops the AITE framework by generalizing the flow using geometric information ($\hat{O}( au)$), analyzes the convergence rate improvement leading to finite-time extinction, and discusses practical approximations like mean-field moment estimation.

Abstract

Imaginary-time evolution (ITE) underpins a broad family of algorithms for ground-state preparation in quantum simulation and quantum many-body physics. In these methods, convergence is governed by the energy variance of the instantaneous state, causing the flow to approach the ground state only asymptotically. We introduce an augmented imaginary-time evolution (AITE) framework that replaces the standard gradient flow on the energy landscape with a geometrically informed descent along locally optimal directions, which are identified by exploiting the higher-order statistical structure of the instantaneous energy distribution. The resulting flow strictly outperforms standard ITE throughout the entire evolution and exhibits two qualitatively distinct regimes: a superlinear convergence regime, followed by an extinction regime in which the energy error vanishes exactly at a finite imaginary time, in sharp contrast to the asymptotic exponential decay of ITE. Standard ITE is recovered in the zero-skewness limit of AITE, implying that the acceleration extends naturally across the broader ITE algorithmic family.

Summary. The paper rigorously analyzes the effective channel impairments in free-space CV-QKD when light passes through atmospheric turbulence and is coupled into an SMF. It demonstrates that while the standard protocol (GG02) can be accurately modeled using a scalar coupling law, protocols utilizing a reference signal require tracking differential phase mismatches, which introduces a distinct, measurable noise penalty.

Why it may be interesting. This work is highly relevant as it bridges complex wave optics simulations with information-theoretic security bounds, providing concrete physical constraints (the need for differential observables) that must be accounted for in practical quantum communication system design.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The core problem is determining the physically relevant effective channel variable for Continuous-Variable Quantum Key Distribution (CV-QKD) when atmospheric turbulence and Single-Mode Fiber (SMF) reception are involved.

Main result. For standard GG02, a realistic scalar law for SMF coupling efficiency suffices; however, pilot-assisted protocols require an additional protocol-dependent reference observable due to signal-pilot phase mismatch.

Method. The authors employ full wave-optics simulations (split-step propagation) followed by deriving effective Gaussian channel closures based on analyzing the necessary statistical moments of the optical observables.

Model / system. The system is a free-space CV-QKD link propagating through atmospheric turbulence, which is modeled via random phase screens and subsequent finite-aperture collection onto an SMF guided mode. The analysis compares simple mean-loss approximations against more rigorous moment closures.

Key observables. Effective coupling efficiency ($\eta_{smf}$), relative phase ($\Delta\phi$), complex coherence indicator ($ u$), and the resulting key rate (bits/pulse).

Important parameters / regimes. Atmospheric turbulence strength ($C_2^n$), propagation distance ($L=1000$ m), and signal-pilot time delay ($\Delta t$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on developing effective physical models (closures) for the channel statistics, rather than providing a complete composable security proof. The mean-loss model is used as a deliberately simple control case.

Figures summary. Figures compare full optical model results against simplified approximations (mean loss vs. scalar surrogates) for GG02, and show that pilot-assisted key rates degrade significantly with increasing signal-pilot delay ($\Delta t$) due to differential phase variance.

Paper structure. The paper progresses by first modeling the full wave optics, then establishing effective closures for the standard GG02 setting using scalar surrogates. It extends this analysis to pilot-assisted architectures, where it introduces and quantifies an additional protocol-dependent reference noise penalty based on differential observables.

Abstract

We study free-space continuous-variable quantum key distribution (CV-QKD) with single-mode fiber (SMF) reception under atmospheric turbulence. The optical channel is modeled by split-step propagation through random phase screens, followed by finite-aperture collection and projection onto the guided receiving mode. We first examine the standard GG02 setting and ask which receiver-side observable is sufficient for effective key-rate prediction. We show that a mean-loss description is generally too optimistic, whereas a scalar effective law for the SMF coupling efficiency provides an accurate downstream Gaussian-channel description within the effective model considered here. We then extend the optical model to a pilot-assisted architecture in which the signal and pilot propagate through correlated but non-identical turbulent realizations generated by a frozen-flow construction. In this case, the signal coupling law alone is no longer sufficient: signal--pilot phase mismatch and loss of post-coupling coherence produce an additional protocol-dependent reference-noise penalty. The results distinguish two regimes: a scalar coupling description is largely adequate for GG02, while transmitted-reference architectures require an additional differential reference observable beyond the signal coupling statistics.

Summary. The paper develops a deterministic quantum algorithm based on Gray codes to prepare complex multi-qudit quantum states within specific weight subspaces. By decomposing the state preparation into sequential controlled rotations, it successfully applies this method to generate exact eigenstates of Hamiltonians relevant to strongly correlated physics, such as Bethe and Dicke states.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a concrete quantum computational blueprint—a universal gate sequence—for preparing highly entangled, symmetry-protected many-body eigenstates (like Bethe or Dicke states) that are central to understanding quantum magnetism and interacting systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to formulate a deterministic quantum algorithm for preparing arbitrary multi-qudit states within a specific definite-weight subspace.

Main result. A concrete, gate-based procedure using Gray codes and controlled rotations (Gray gates) is established to prepare these complex entangled states, including Bethe and Dicke states.

Method. The method maps the state preparation problem onto traversing an ordered sequence of basis states defined by a multiset permutation Gray code. Each transition requires applying a specific controlled unitary rotation (the 'Gray gate').

Model / system. The system involves $n$-qudit systems using $d$-dimensional qudits, whose states are characterized by weight vectors $\vec{k}$. The target states are eigenstates of various Hamiltonians, such as the SU(3)-invariant Heisenberg Hamiltonian.

Key observables. State amplitudes ($a_w$) defining the superposition coefficients, and the eigenvalues of the relevant symmetry-preserving Hamiltonians (e.g., $E = - rac{1}{2}n(n-1)$ for Dicke states).

Important parameters / regimes. The weight vector $\vec{k}=(k_0, \dots, k_{d-1})$, the qudit dimension $d$, and the deformation parameter $q$ (for $ ext{SU}_q(d)$ generalizations).

Assumptions / limitations. The algorithm assumes that a Gray code ordering exists for the multiset permutations such that adjacent states differ by exactly one controlled swap, allowing the state preparation to be decomposed into sequential two-qudit rotations.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the structure of the required Gray gate circuit (Figure 1) and optimized tree structures ensuring the single-swap property for multiset permutations (Figure 4).

Paper structure. The paper first introduces the general framework using Gray codes to reduce state preparation to controlled rotations. Abstract

We formulate a deterministic algorithm for preparing arbitrary multi-qudit states in a definite-weight subspace. By ordering the corresponding computational basis states according to a Gray code for multiset permutations, the state-preparation task is reduced to performing a sequence of controlled 2-qudit Gray rotations. We use this algorithm to prepare exact eigenstates of the SU(3)-invariant Heisenberg Hamiltonian, whose Bethe ansatz is nested. In particular, we describe the preparation of the Bethe states, which are SU(3) highest-weight states, as well as their lower-weight descendants. We also consider the preparation of $SU(d)$ Dicke states and their q-deformations.

Summary. Main problem. The core challenge is achieving real-time, adaptive quantum control by generating robust, high-fidelity control pulses across a continuous range of varying experimental parameters.

Abstract

Real-time adaptive control of quantum systems requires rapid generation of robust, high-fidelity pulses across a continuous range of operating conditions. Standard optimization algorithms such as gradient-ascent pulse engineering (GRAPE) solve each instance independently, discarding information between runs and requiring costly reinitialization when parameters change. Demonstrated on a single-spin (two-level) system, the trained networks produce pulses for arbitrary rotation angles over a range of pulse durations, detunings, and field inhomogeneities in milliseconds, at fidelities comparable to multi-seed GRAPE. The framework is inherently adaptable: any parameter entering the Hamiltonian can serve as a network input, extending the approach to different systems and control settings. Beyond speed, the network reveals structure in the control landscape: it discovers the same structured phase profiles that appear in GRAPE solutions -- made identifiable through fidelity-invariant symmetry transformations -- but more consistently than independent optimization. This consistency enables smooth interpolation across the entire trained parameter space.

Density-wave order enhances the phonon thermal Hall effect in a trilayer nickelate

Authors: Qiaochao Xiang, Enkang Zhang, Xiaokang Li, Xiaodong Guo, Mengfei Zhu, Jun Zhao, Guang-Ming Zhang, Liang Li, Zengwei Zhu
Type: experiment · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24125v1

Summary. The authors report observing a large, enhanced phonon thermal Hall effect in the nickelate $ ext{La}4 ext{Ni}_3 ext{O}$ below its density-wave transition temperature. By measuring multiple transport properties and ruling out electronic or simple phonon-drag contributions, they attribute the signal to magnon-phonon hybridization driven by spin-lattice coupling. This reveals a crucial interplay between magnetic order and lattice dynamics in these superconducting materials.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides experimental evidence for how electronic order (density waves) can couple strongly to lattice vibrations (phonons), leading to exotic thermal responses. This coupling mechanism is relevant to understanding emergent quantum phenomena in correlated materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the role of lattice degrees of freedom in the normal state of Ruddlesden–Popper nickelates, specifically focusing on the origin and enhancement mechanism of the phonon thermal Hall effect.

Main result. The enhanced transverse thermal conductivity ($\kappa_{xy}$) below the density-wave transition is strongly attributed to phonons coupled via magnon-phonon hybridization driven by spin-lattice coupling. This signal significantly exceeds contributions from electronic carriers or simple phonon drag mechanisms.

Method. The research employs transport measurements, measuring longitudinal and transverse thermal conductivities ($\kappa_{xx}, \kappa_{xy}$), magnetoresistance, and Seebeck/Nernst coefficients as a function of temperature and magnetic field.

Model / system. The physical system is the trilayer nickelate $ ext{La}4 ext{Ni}_3 ext{O}$, which exhibits density-wave order. The underlying physics involves charge-lattice coupling, where hybridization between apical oxygen $2p$ and Ni $3d_{z^2}$ orbitals creates local dipoles that interact with magnetic fields.

Key observables. Thermal Hall angle ($ abla T_y / abla T_x$), transverse thermal conductivity ($\kappa_{xy}$), longitudinal thermal conductivity ($\kappa_{xx}$), Seebeck coefficient ($S$), and Nernst coefficient ($\alpha_{xy}$).

Important parameters / regimes. Density-wave transition temperature ($T^* \approx 140$ K); characteristic energy scales ($\sim 3.2 - 4.1$ meV); magnetic field strength (up to 10 T).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis assumes that the electronic contribution can be estimated via the Wiedemann–Franz law, and it estimates an upper bound for phonon-drag using $S_{xx}T\alpha_{xy}$.

Figures summary. Figures show $\kappa_{xy}$ enhancement below $T^*$, confirming phononic origin over electronic contributions. They also compare measured $\kappa_{xy}$ against the calculated phonon-drag limit, showing a large discrepancy.

Paper structure. The paper systematically measures various transport coefficients ($ ho, \kappa, S, \alpha$) under varying magnetic fields and temperatures to isolate the source of transverse heat transport, culminating in a comparison that rules out simple models like phonon drag.

Abstract

Ruddlesden--Popper nickelates have emerged as a promising platform for high-temperature superconductivity, yet the role of lattice degrees of freedom in their correlated normal state remains largely unexplored. Here, we report the observation of a finite phonon thermal Hall effect in the trilayer nickelate La$_4$Ni$_3$O$_{10}$ at ambient pressure. Remarkably, the thermal Hall response is strongly enhanced below the density-wave transition at $T^*\approx140$ K, exhibiting two distinct plateaus in the thermal Hall resistivity. The characteristic energy scale extracted from the thermal Hall response ($\sim4.1$ meV) closely matches the magnon--phonon crossing span energy ($\sim3.2$ meV), pointing to magnon--phonon hybridization as the primary mechanism enhancing the thermal Hall effect. These results provide new insight into the interplay between lattice and spin excitations in nickelates, with implications for understanding both their superconductivity and the multiple possible origins of insulating thermal Hall signals.

The unperturbed Hamiltonian is initially constructed by neglecting electron-electron interactions, treating the system as three independent hydrogen-like electrons to yield a zeroth-order energy baseline of -275.51 eV. The antisymmetric fermionic nature of the exact wave function is rigorously enforced through the Slater determinant formalism. First-order perturbation theory is applied to evaluate static inter-electronic repulsion using exact Coulomb and exchange integrals, refining the energy state to -192.01 eV. To account for dynamical electronic correlation, second-order perturbation theory is computed numerically for virtual single-electron s-orbital transitions, leading to a total perturbative energy of -196.36 eV. A brief discussion of two-electron excitations is also included to encapsulate further physical realism within the framework. Furthermore, a non-orthogonal two-parameter variational approach is employed to model the shell-specific shielding effect. By optimizing the effective nuclear charges, the variational method establishes a superior upper bound energy of -201.187 eV. The results of both methods are comprehensively contrasted against each other and the reference baseline to provide critical insights into the nature of electron correlation and screening in multi-electron atoms.

Abstract

We review here two different viewpoints on the Berry-Keating operator $H_{BK}$, whose connection to the Riemann hypothesis remains an intriguing and not yet fully understood question, despite considerable attention in the recent literature. The Vector and Canonical Components of the Momentum Operator in 3D Euclidean Space Spanned by General Curvilinear Coordinates

Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24572v1

Summary. This paper develops a rigorous theoretical framework to define the momentum operator in general curvilinear coordinates. By relating the momentum to the velocity operator via the Heisenberg equation, the authors construct Hermitian vector and canonical components that correctly account for geometric curvature effects. This provides essential mathematical machinery for quantum mechanics on curved spaces.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides fundamental mathematical tools necessary for correctly formulating quantum mechanics on curved manifolds, which is crucial when modeling physical systems whose geometry cannot be approximated by flat Euclidean space.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The construction of the Hermitian vector and canonical components of the momentum operator ($\vec{p}$) in 3D Euclidean space when expressed using general curvilinear coordinates (GCC's).

Main result. A unified, natural approach is developed showing that while individual coordinate derivatives are problematic, the full momentum operator $\vec{p}$ remains Hermitian due to cancellation of boundary terms.

Method. The method utilizes the Heisenberg equation of motion applied to the position operator ($\vec{r}$) and identifies $\vec{p} \propto m\dot{\vec{r}}$ to derive corrected, Hermitian vector components ($\vec{p}_H$) before projecting them onto canonical forms ($p_H^a$).

Model / system. The study is purely theoretical, focusing on the mathematical structure of quantum mechanics in curved coordinate systems. The underlying physical system involves a particle whose Hamiltonian includes the Laplace-Beltrami operator.

Key observables. Hermiticity of $\vec{p}$ and its components; canonical commutation relations $[q^a, p_H^b] = i\hbar \delta^a_b$.

Important parameters / regimes. Planck's constant ($\hbar$), mass ($m$), metric tensor ($g_{ij}$).

Assumptions / limitations. Standard physical boundary conditions (e.g., square-integrability) are assumed for the necessary integration by parts to prove Hermiticity.

Figures summary. Not specified in the notes, but the text develops explicit forms for operators like $\vec{p}_H$ and $p_H^a$ in specific coordinate systems (e.g., polar/spherical).

The paper first establishes the problem by noting that naive momentum components fail Hermiticity; it then derives a general procedure using the Heisenberg equation to construct $\vec{p}_H$; this is demonstrated explicitly for simple cases like polar coordinates before generalizing the formalism to arbitrary GCC's.

Abstract

We construct the Hermitian vector and canonical components of the momentum operator in 3D Euclidean space spanned by general curvilinear coordinates (GCC's) using a simple, natural and unified approach based on identifying the momentum operator in any coordinate system as mass times the velocity operator. When this latter is calculated by applying the Heisenberg equation of motion, it returns ($-i\hbar$ times) the gradient operator plus an additional zero-valued sum, which when distributed among the components of the gradient, it makes each the Hermitian vector component of the momentum operator in GCC's. The canonical components follow immediately upon symmetrizing each of these vector components in the corresponding base vector. For accessability by wider audiences, we first develop the formalism for the simple polar coordinates and then we develop the case for GCC's.
