Unmodeled states and uncertain action outcomes in agentic scanning tunneling microscopy

Summary. This work demonstrates an LLM-based agent system, 'quailbot,' capable of autonomously performing complex, multi-step scientific tasks like STM tip conditioning. By integrating the LLM into the instrument's feedback loop, the agent learns to adapt to physical uncertainties and hidden experimental states. It advances the field by showing how AI can manage real-world, unpredictable physical measurements.

Why it may be interesting. While the focus is on AI control, the underlying physical measurements (STM, lattice structure, correlated materials) are highly relevant to condensed matter physics, particularly in characterizing electronic states.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Autonomous scientific agents must interpret the consequences of their interventions when operating with incomplete knowledge of the experimental state, especially when interventions alter hidden states unpredictably.

Main result. LLM agents successfully completed end-to-end STM tip conditioning after apprenticeship, demonstrating adaptation to unexpected physical conditions.

Method. The authors developed 'quailbot,' an agent harness that embeds an LLM into the instrument feedback loop to link physical interventions with experimental readbacks.

Model / system. The physical system is Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM) tip conditioning on FeTe at 4 K. The agent interacts with the instrument using CLI tools to control physical parameters.

Key observables. BraggZ score, topographs, Fourier transforms, and tunneling current are used to diagnose tip quality and surface structure.

Important parameters / regimes. The experiment operates under conditions of hidden states (e.g., tip apex configuration) and unmodeled latent contingencies.

Assumptions / limitations. The primary limitation is that the agent's autonomy is constrained by the observability and action limits of the real instrument, and it struggles when states are hidden or unmodeled.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the comparison of public vs. hidden states in tip conditioning, the architecture of the quailbot harness, and the sequence of agent actions during a successful run.

Paper structure. The paper introduces the problem of action-outcome uncertainty in physical experiments, details the quailbot agent harness and its apprenticeship training, presents results showing autonomous completion of STM tip conditioning, and concludes by discussing the necessity of inferring hidden states.

Abstract

In physical experiments, interventions can alter hidden experimental states in ways that cannot be predicted in advance. Autonomous scientific agents must therefore interpret the consequences of their interventions while operating with incomplete knowledge of the experimental state. Here we investigate this problem using scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) tip conditioning, traditionally a human-expert-intensive task governed by inaccessible tip apex conditions and uncertain action outcomes. We introduce quailbot, an agent harness that places the LLM inside the instrument feedback loop by linking physical interventions with experimental readbacks. After a brief apprenticeship with a human expert, frontier LLM agents autonomously completed end-to-end tip conditioning on an STM and passed an independent verification. The contingencies that arose during the experiments further exposed the limits of LLM agentic autonomy when relevant experimental states or dynamics were hidden or unmodeled. Our results show that future autonomous physical experimentation requires agents to infer hidden and unmodeled experimental states, track the outcomes of their actions, and operate within the observability and action limits of real instruments.

Certifying fermionic Gaussian states (and a little more) with optimal precision dependence

Authors: Ninnat Dangniam, Laphas Premcharoen, Metrasit Sripech, Thiparat Chotibut
Type: theory · Category: statistical mechanics · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28233v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · analog quantum simulation 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This work develops an efficient, adaptive protocol to certify fermionic Gaussian states by analyzing the spectral gap of an associated Markov chain walk. It achieves optimal sample complexity scaling ($\epsilon^{-1}$) using only single-qubit measurements. This provides a rigorous theoretical tool for characterizing complex quantum states relevant to condensed matter physics.

Why it may be interesting. The connection between quantum state verification complexity and the spectral gap of a Markov chain is a powerful theoretical tool, providing quantitative bounds on the resources needed for quantum characterization.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the quantum state certification problem, specifically for fermionic Gaussian states, aiming to find measurement protocols with optimal sample complexity.

Main result. They propose an adaptive protocol that certifies any $d$-mode pure fermionic Gaussian state using $O(d^2\epsilon^{-1})$ copies, achieving optimal $\epsilon^{-1}$ scaling with single-qubit measurements.

Method. The approach involves establishing a spectral gap lower bound for a 'certification walk' (a Markov chain) associated with the state, linking efficiency to the Markov chain's relaxation time to equilibrium.

Model / system. The focus is on fermionic Gaussian states (fGSs), which are ground states of quadratic fermionic Hamiltonians, relevant for quantum simulation of fermionic systems. The analysis uses concepts from Markov chain theory, such as spectral gaps, applied to the certification process.

Key observables. The spectral gap ($\gamma(L)$) of the certification walk, and the sample complexity $N$ required for certification.

Important parameters / regimes. Precision $\epsilon$, number of modes $d$, and the target fidelity $\delta$.

Assumptions / limitations. The protocol relies on the spectral gap of a $d \leftrightarrow d-2$ down-up walk, and the analysis compares results against known bounds for Haar-typical states.

Figures summary. Table II compares computed median and minimum spectral gaps ($\gamma(L)$) for random ensembles against the theoretical bound $ rac{2}{d(d-1)}$ for various dimensions $d$.

Paper structure. The paper builds from general QSV techniques, develops the connection between certification and spectral gaps via homogenization to a down-up walk, and concludes with detailed spectral gap calculations for various physical ensembles.

Abstract

Fermionic Gaussian states are the workhorse reference states for qubit-based quantum simulation of fermionic systems, yet existing certification protocols either proceed via fidelity estimation, leading to suboptimal sample complexity in the target precision $ε$, only apply to Haar-typical states, or require adaptive measurements on a large fraction of qubits. We give an adaptive protocol that certifies any $d$-mode pure fermionic Gaussian state using $O(d^2ε^{-1})$ copies, single-qubit measurements with only one qubit measured adaptively per copy, and $O(d^3)$ classical processing time per copy. The $ε^{-1}$ dependence is optimal for the certification problem, even among strategies using entangled measurements. The sample complexity is controlled by the spectral gap of a $d\leftrightarrow d-2$ down-up walk---a Markov chain studied in the theory of high-dimensional expanders, equivalent to a two-site Glauber dynamics in statistical physics---relating certification efficiency to relaxation time to equilibrium. The worst-case bound is tight for this protocol and is attained by physically relevant states, including ground states of the fully dimerized Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain and BCS pair states. In contrast, numerics up to $d=14$ suggest that $O(d ε^{-1})$ copies suffice to certify Haar-typical Gaussian states, a factor of $d$ improvement over the worst-case bound. Finally, because the bound depends only on the target's computational-basis distribution, the protocol extends to efficiently phase-dressed states---states obtained by injecting an efficiently computable diagonal phase to Gaussian states---with the same sample complexity. The class includes a continuous family of four-mode non-Gaussian magic states for matchgate computation.

Summary. This work provides a rigorous mathematical and computational framework for evaluating anisotropic Epstein zeta functions, which are crucial for modeling long-range interactions in quantum and classical lattice systems. By developing stable algorithms, the authors overcome previous limitations in handling anisotropic kernels, providing tools for advanced simulations in condensed matter physics.

Why it may be interesting. While the focus is mathematical, the direct application to dipolar interactions in magnetic materials and lattice models with long-range interactions makes it highly relevant for condensed matter theorists studying correlated electron systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the fundamental computational challenge of precisely and efficiently evaluating large-scale lattice sums involving power-law kernels, especially in the anisotropic case.

Main result. The authors derive a stably computable representation for the anisotropic Epstein zeta function and develop a high-precision numerical algorithm for its evaluation across various lattice geometries and anisotropy orders.

Method. The methodology involves generalizing Epstein zeta functions, utilizing wave vector derivatives of lattice sums, and connecting the results to a generalized Euler-Maclaurin summation formula.

Model / system. The theory applies to simulating classical and quantum systems characterized by long-range, anisotropic interactions, such as dipolar interactions in magnetic materials or in the context of topological superconductivity.

Key observables. The anisotropic Epstein zeta function, its regularized form, and the coefficients derived from the singular Euler-Maclaurin expansion.

Important parameters / regimes. Lattice structure ($\Lambda$), power-law decay exponent ($ u$), and anisotropy order ($\alpha$).

Assumptions / limitations. The derivation relies on analytic continuation and the assumption that the function can be related to derivatives of theta functions and established identities.

Paper structure. The paper is structured analytically, first defining the problem and deriving the stable representations, followed by developing the computational algorithm, and concluding with applications and benchmarking.

Abstract

The precise and efficient evaluation of large-scale lattice sums involving power-law kernels is a fundamental computational problem in the simulation of classical and quantum systems with long-range interactions. While methods for spatially isotropic kernels, some based on Epstein zeta functions, have advanced considerably in recent years, the anisotropic case has lagged behind, despite its broad relevance to both fundamental and effective interactions such as the dipole interaction in magnetic materials. In this work, we solve this issue by defining and analyzing anisotropic Epstein zeta functions for which we derive stably computable representations obtained from wave vector derivatives of lattice sums over isotropic interaction kernels. These functions find direct application in the analytical and numerical study of anisotropically interacting lattice systems. Going further, they provide the correction term in an exact equivalence between discrete lattices and their continuous analogs in a recent generalization of the classical Euler-Maclaurin summation formula to lattices and summands involving power-law kernels. Their connection to high-order derivatives of zeta functions can be used to improve convergence rates of numerical algorithms, for instance in micromagnetics, or to provide rapidly convergent expansions suitable for precomputations of generalized zeta functions. We derive a stably computable representation of anisotropic Epstein zeta functions, including the possibility for analytically removing Rayleigh--Wood singularities, and we develop a numerical algorithm for their stable evaluation for any lattice, power-law decay exponent and anisotropy order. We benchmark the algorithm against closed-form identities, direct summation and multi-precision results, obtaining machine precision across various lattices, power-law exponents, and anisotropy orders.

By achieving high spatial resolution and sensitivity, the method can detect subtle magnetic signatures associated with early-stage material fatigue and microstructural damage. This advances the application of quantum sensing from fundamental physics into practical materials engineering.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses the need for non-destructive testing (NDT) techniques capable of imaging microstructural changes in miniaturized materials, particularly those related to fatigue damage.

Main result. A widefield NV-center magnetic imaging technique was developed, achieving high sensitivity and spatial resolution to detect characteristic magnetic stray field changes indicative of early fatigue damage in materials like electrical steel.

Model / system. The physical system is the NV center in diamond, which acts as a quantum sensor whose spin state is sensitive to the local magnetic field ($\vec{B}$). The measurement relies on mapping the magnetic stray field distribution, which is influenced by the material's magnetic microstructure and defects.

Key observables. Magnetic stray field distribution, Zeeman splitting maps, and characteristic changes in the magnetic field pattern after cyclic loading.

Important parameters / regimes. Magnetic sensitivities below $10 \mu ext{T}/\sqrt{ ext{Hz}}$ and a spatial resolution of $1-2 \mu ext{m}$.

Abstract

Due to progressing miniaturization, material fatigue is expected to gain importance on microscopic scales. Nevertheless, most non-destructive testing techniques are optimized for macroscopic scales. Therefore, new approaches applicable to miniaturized samples need to be explored. High spatial resolution imaging of a material's magnetic stray field enables sensitive detection of microstructural changes due to the local interplay of magnetic properties, strain, and defects. Up to now, this approach has rarely been used for materials testing since high-resolution magnetic sensing is traditionally challenging. Novel quantum sensing techniques offer the potential to close this gap. One of the most prominent quantum sensors, the nitrogen vacancy center in diamond, is investigated for non-destructive testing applications within the scope of this work. An experimental system based on a widefield sensing approach was developed to image magnetic stray field distributions within seconds to minutes over areas up to 1 x 1 $mm^2$. Magnetic sensitivities below 10 $μ$T/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ and a spatial resolution of 1-2 $μ$m were achieved. The technique offers high mechanical stability, which is crucial for non-destructive testing applications. Comparison with magneto-optical Kerr effect measurements showed that the magnetic field maps are strongly correlated with the material's surface domains while containing additional information from deeper inside the sample. Characteristic changes in the magnetic stray field distribution were detected in an electrical steel sample after cyclic loading. A potential marker for early fatigue damage was deduced from the splitting gradient distribution, while 2D Fourier transform analysis provided additional insight.

Quantum Metrology for Signals with an Unknown Causal Structure

Authors: Gwon Ryul Han, Hyukgun Kwon, Seok Hyung Lie
Type: theory · Category: other · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28066v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: quantum measurements 3/5 · interference shaping light 2/5

↔ Scroll figures horizontally

Summary. This theoretical paper develops a framework for quantum metrology when the causal structure of the interacting signals is unknown. It proves that the measurement must be interferometric and establishes a universal spacetime Heisenberg limit for the achievable precision. This result is significant because it provides an order-independent bound for sensing parameters in complex physical systems like radar or scattering experiments.

Why it may be interesting. This work provides a powerful, order-independent generalization of quantum metrology bounds, which is crucial for interpreting data from complex, non-sequential physical measurements where causality cannot be assumed.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper addresses quantum metrology for signals where the causal structure (the order and timing of interactions) is unknown beforehand, a scenario common in experiments like radar or scattering.

Main result. The authors derive a universal upper bound, the spacetime Heisenberg limit, for the Fisher information that is independent of the unknown event ordering, demonstrating that causal agnosticity forces the measurement scheme to be interferometric.

Method. The analysis utilizes the 'quantum state over spacetime' formalism ($\Omega$) and derives bounds on the Fisher information by analyzing interference terms associated with local unitary interactions.

Model / system. The model involves quantum sensing using interferometry, described by a spacetime state $\Omega$ acting on multiple discrete events $A_k$. The formalism generalizes density operators to arbitrary spacetime regions to handle unknown causal structures.

Key observables. Fisher information ($F_ heta$), Interference Term ($I_\Omega$), Spacetime Heisenberg Limit ($\mathcal{F}_ heta$), and the shared reference system coherence.

Important parameters / regimes. The number of sensing events ($N$), the signal parameter ($ heta$), and the local generator norms ($|h_{k, heta}|_\infty$).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis is based on the assumption of causal agnosticity, meaning the measurement protocol must work regardless of the true causal ordering of the signals.

Figures summary. Figure 1 illustrates the general setup for causally agnostic interferometry and probing spacetime states using local interventions.

Paper structure. The paper builds from establishing that causal agnosticity mandates an interferometric measurement scheme, deriving the universal bound (spacetime Heisenberg limit), and then analyzing saturation conditions using both general spacetime states and specific cases like classical mixtures or fixed causal orders.

Abstract

Quantum sensing protocols usually presuppose where and when each signal acts, yet this information is unavailable beforehand in experiments such as scattering and radar. We show that causal agnosticity forces a measurement scheme to be interferometric even when only discrete interactions are allowed. Describing the sensing events in the recently developed quantum state over spacetime formalism, we then prove that the Fisher information of an interferometer is bounded by the squared sum of the local generator norms, independently of how the events are ordered. Nonunitary and spatiotemporally correlated signals can be explained as well when their physical dilation has a spacetime state on the enlarged events. Notably, the bound holds without presupposing a background spacetime that orders the events whenever the full collection of target and auxiliary events has a spacetime state. We show that this bound yields the spacetime Heisenberg limit and characterize its saturation through marginals of the spacetime state `dressed' by the signal. The $N^2$ scaling of the Fisher information with $N$ sensing events arises from the coherence of a single reference system shared across spacetime, whereas independently repeated interferometry obeys the standard quantum limit.

Robust Strange Metallicity across Attractive and Repulsive Hubbard Models

Authors: Xiaoyue Ma, Emily Z. Zhang, Thomas P. Devereaux
Type: theory · Category: strongly correlated electrons · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.26971v1
Analysis basis: full PDF text, analyzed in chunks Topic relevance: analog quantum simulation 3/5 · Keldysh / 2PI / non-Gaussian methods 1/5 · non-equilibrium universality 1/5

Summary. This work uses DQMC simulations to compare charge transport in attractive and repulsive Hubbard models at strong coupling. The authors find that both models exhibit strange-metal behavior ($ ho \propto T$) over a wide temperature range, suggesting this linear resistivity is a generic feature of incoherent transport rather than being dictated by the system's ultimate low-temperature state. The distinction between the two models only becomes apparent at much lower temperatures.

Why it may be interesting. The finding that transport linearity is robust across different low-energy correlation types suggests a universal transport constraint in the incoherent regime, which is a general concept applicable to many-body dynamics.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study investigates the origin of 'strange metallicity,' characterized by linear-in-temperature resistivity, in strongly correlated electron systems.

Main result. The linear-in-temperature resistivity observed in both attractive and repulsive Hubbard models is robust and appears insensitive to the microscopic nature of the low-energy correlations.

Method. The research employs Determinant Quantum Monte Carlo (DQMC) simulations combined with the Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) method for analytic continuation of current-current correlators.

Model / system. The system is the two-dimensional Hubbard model, analyzed in both attractive ($U<0$) and repulsive ($U>0$) regimes at strong coupling ($|U|/t=6$).

Key observables. DC longitudinal resistivity ($ ho_{xx}$), charge compressibility ($\chi_c$), and charge diffusivity ($D$).

Important parameters / regimes. Strong coupling regime ($|U|/t=6$), temperature dependence across intermediate and high-temperature regimes, and comparison between $U<0$ and $U>0$.

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the Nernst–Einstein relation connecting resistivity to compressibility and diffusivity, and the high-temperature behavior is analyzed via moment expansion.

Figures summary. Figures illustrate the temperature dependence of resistivity ($ ho_{xx}$) for both interaction signs, showing the common linear-T behavior, and also plot the temperature dependence of $\chi^{-1}$ and $D^{-1}$ separately.

Paper structure. The paper compares transport properties in the attractive and repulsive Hubbard models across various temperatures, establishing the common linear-T resistivity in the incoherent regime, and then analyzing the low-temperature deviations where the models separate.

Abstract

Using determinant quantum Monte Carlo simulations, we compare charge transport in the two-dimensional attractive and repulsive Hubbard models at strong coupling, $|U|/t=6$. Although the interaction with opposite signs generates qualitatively different low-energy spin, charge, and pairing correlations, both exhibit approximately linear-in-temperature resistivity over a broad intermediate- and high-temperature regime. At asymptotically high temperature this common behavior follows from the moment expansion of the conductivity, whose leading contribution is even in $U$. More strikingly, the similarity persists to temperatures well below $|U|$, where strong interaction-dependent correlations have already developed, and also irrespective of whether resistivity crosses the MIR limit. Using the Nernst--Einstein relation, we find that the common linear-in-temperature resistivity is primarily associated with an approximately Curie-like charge compressibility and weakly temperature-dependent diffusivity. The two models separate only at lower temperatures, where the attractive model develops a pronounced feature in the charge diffusivity correlated with signatures of pair formation. These results show that linear-in-temperature incoherent transport in the Hubbard model can be remarkably insensitive to the microscopic nature of the low-energy correlations. Our results suggest that transport in the incoherent regime appears insensitive to what the system will ultimately become at low temperature; the distinction between competing low-energy states becomes visible only when their characteristic correlations acquire sufficiently long spatial or temporal coherence.

Summary. This paper investigates the reliability of Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) results obtained on current quantum hardware by analyzing finite-shot selection bias. Using drift-differenced remeasurement, the authors demonstrate that the apparent accuracy gains from optimization are often artifacts of the measurement process. They conclude that hardware-reported best-case energies must be treated with caution and require rigorous, independent validation.

Why it may be interesting. This work directly tackles the practical limitations of near-term quantum hardware for quantum chemistry, providing crucial diagnostics on how measurement noise and optimization procedures can mislead users into overestimating computational accuracy.

Main problem. The paper addresses the 'winner's curse' in hardware Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) calculations, where finite-shot optimization can lead to artificially accurate energy estimates.

Main result. Independent evaluation using drift-differenced remeasurement showed that most of the apparent advantage gained by the optimizer disappears, suggesting that accuracy claims based on best-observed hardware VQE values require independent verification.

Method. The authors use a drift-differenced estimator to compare later remeasurements of optimized parameters against the initial best-observed values, effectively canceling common epoch shifts to quantify selection bias.

Model / system. The study benchmarks molecular ground-state energies for six molecules ($ ext{H}_2$, $ ext{LiH}$, $ ext{HF}$, $ ext{O}_2$, $ ext{BeH}_2$, $ ext{H}_2 ext{O}$) using active-space Hamiltonians.

Abstract

Finite-shot optimization can make noisy variational quantum eigensolver energies look artificially accurate. We studied six molecules on two IBM Heron r3 processors in eight replicated cells. Seven optimizer-selected values fell below the exact active-space eigenvalue by up to mHa. Independent later evaluation of the selected and final parameter points, differenced to remove a common epoch shift, showed that most of the selected advantage disappeared: the primary recovery was (approximate 95% session-level interval). Across the reported aggregation choices, recovery estimates ranged from 0.892 to 0.989. The measured contrast was consistent in scale with finite-shot selection, while calibration drift and other sources of variation remain possible. Accuracy claims based on best-observed hardware VQE values therefore require an independent check.

Abstract

Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) states are widely studied as a bosonic encoding for fault-tolerant quantum computing because small displacement errors can be identified and corrected through syndrome measurements. However, fault-tolerant operation requires substantially greater GKP squeezing than is currently available in optical platforms. It is therefore important to identify quantum-information tasks that can be realised with finite-squeezing GKP states. Here, we study Bell nonlocality in finite-energy GKP Bell states under experimentally motivated measurement constraints. Although finite-energy GKP Bell states support Bell nonlocality even at low squeezing, logical Pauli measurements alone cannot reveal it. Accessing the maximum CHSH violation supported by the state generally requires logical non-Clifford rotations. We instead show that the required non-Gaussian resource can reside in the measurement itself. Photon-number-resolving detection combined with Gaussian preprocessing and adaptive feed-forward closely approaches the maximum violation of the CHSH Bell inequality without implementing logical non-Clifford gates. The same measurement principle strongly enhances Bell violations for cat-code Bell pairs and entangled coherent states, demonstrating that its usefulness extends beyond the GKP lattice. Our results identify non-Gaussian measurements as a practical route to revealing Bell nonlocality in finite-energy bosonic states.

On Relationship Between Circuit Depth and Trainability of VQAs

Authors: Jan Michálek, Martin Friák, Petr Vašík
Type: theory · Category: quantum information and computing · PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.27488v1

Summary. This work theoretically analyzes the trainability of Variational Quantum Algorithms by mapping their loss landscapes to Wishart Hypertoroidal Random Fields. Using the Kac-Rice formula, the authors predict a phase transition governing whether local minima approximate the global minimum. The key finding is that symmetry reduction can artificially lower the effective complexity, enabling the theoretical study of larger quantum systems.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous mathematical framework (random field theory) to predict when and how VQAs will fail or succeed in finding ground states, which is crucial for designing practical quantum algorithms.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The paper analyzes the training efficiency (trainability) of Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) by studying the geometry of their loss landscapes.

Main result. A phase transition exists in the local minima distribution, where local minima concentrate near the global minimum when the overparameterization factor exceeds a critical threshold.

Method. The VQA loss landscape is mapped to a Wishart Hypertoroidal Random Field (WHRF), and the distribution of critical points is analyzed using a reformulated Kac-Rice formula.

Model / system. The analysis uses the Fermi-Hubbard model Hamiltonian, which is mapped onto a VQA cost function $E( heta)$. The complexity is analyzed on the hypertorus $\mathbb{T}^p$.

Key observables. Expected number of critical points ($\mathbb{E}[ ext{Crit}]$), the overparameterization factor ($\gamma$), and the degrees of freedom ($m$).

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio between the problem Hamiltonian degrees of freedom and the number of independent VQA parameters ($p$).

Assumptions / limitations. The mapping to WHRFs is a statistical model, and the analysis relies on the Kac-Rice formula, which assumes the function is a Morse function.

Figures summary. Figure 1 shows the exponential scaling of the degrees of freedom parameter $m$ with system size for the Fermi-Hubbard model.

Paper structure. The paper first establishes the connection between VQA trainability and random field theory, then develops the WHRF framework using the Kac-Rice formula, and finally simulates the phase transition using the Fermi-Hubbard model, proposing symmetry reduction techniques to mitigate exponential scaling.

Abstract

The training efficiency of Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) is dictated by the geometry of their loss landscapes, which can be formally analysed by mapping these functions to random fields on manifolds. Instead of VQAs, we can then directly study the corresponding random fields, in our case, the Wishart Hypertoroidal Random Fields (WHRFs). We are mostly interested in the distribution of critical points (especially local minima) of WHRFs. For this purpose, the Kac-Rice formula is presented, reformulated, and simulated. The findings identify a phase transition in the distribution of local minima. Beyond a specific threshold, local minima concentrate near the global minimum in function value, meaning that even local minima are good approximators of the global one. The threshold is governed by the ratio between the problem Hamiltonian degrees of freedom and by the number of independent parameters in the VQA. Since the degrees of freedom parameter scales exponentially, we propose symmetry reduction operations to lower the degrees of freedom. This mathematically reduces the dimension, scaling down the required parameter threshold and enabling to solve bigger problems.

Abstract

Strong light-matter coupling offers a powerful framework for modifying molecular structure, dynamics, and optical response, yet achieving this regime at the single-molecule level remains a central challenge. Scanning Tunneling Microscope Break Junctions (STM-BJ) provide a promising route by combining single-molecule addressability with extreme nanoscale field confinement. However, the metallic environment that enables such confinement also introduces substantial plasmonic and excitonic losses, raising the question of how strong coupling can be realized in a metal-molecule-metal junction. Here, we identify the voltage as the key control parameter. We show that the applied bias not only drives the formation of an interfacial exciton through resonant charge transport but also suppresses its coupling to metallic loss channels. Simultaneously, the plasmonic cavity retains extreme mode confinement while the availability of modes is moderately tuned by voltage. Together, these results establish voltage-driven STM-BJs as electrically tunable single-molecule plasmonic cavities and open new routes for using the powerful STM-BJ toolbox, including quantum transport, tracking chemical dynamics, optoelectronics, and molecular-scale quantum control under strong light-matter coupling.

Abstract

We study the high-dimensional recovery of a signal vector $\mathbf{x}$ in the presence of sparse Wishart-like noise. We define an $N \times N$ matrix $A = J+(θ/N)\mathbf{xx}^{\top}$, where $\mathbf{xx}^{\top}$ is the rank-one deformation of the random noise matrix $J$. We consider a Wishart-like matrix $J={X}^{\top} X$, where $X$ is a sparse $M \times N$ random matrix with entries $X_{ij} = c_{ij}W_{ij}$, with $c_{ij}$ regulating the density of non-zero elements, and $W_{ij}$ the bond weights. Using the replica method, we compute analytically the top eigenpair statistics of $A$, and their dependence on the signal strength $θ$, the rectangularity ratio $α=\sqrt{M/N}$, and the average connectivity of the noise. The spectral observables are expressed in terms of a system of Recursive Distributional Equations, which are efficiently solved via a Population Dynamics algorithm. They allow us to compute the average largest eigenvalue $\langleλ_1\rangle_{A}$, the average top eigenvector component density, and the average overlap between the top eigenvector of $A$ and $\mathbf{x}$. We identify a critical threshold $θ_{\mathrm{crit}}$--depending on the average connectivity of the noise--that marks a BBP-like phase transition: below this value, $\langleλ_1\rangle_{A}$ is unaffected by the signal, and the overlap vanishes. Thus, the signal is not recoverable from the top eigenvector of $A$. For $θ>θ_{\mathrm{crit}}$, the signal-related outlier eigenvalue becomes $\langleλ_1\rangle_{A}$ and the overlap is nonzero, allowing for recovery of the signal. The results are in excellent agreement with numerical diagonalisation. We show that in the dense limit, the recovery threshold and eigen-statistics converge to the results predicted by the classical BBP transition for additive rank-one deformations of dense Wishart matrices.
