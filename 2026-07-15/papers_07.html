Summary. This paper investigates the origin of magnetization plateaus in SrCu2(BO3)2 by synthesizing results from NMR and tensor-network simulations. The authors argue that low-field plateaus are best described by Wigner crystals of spin-2 bound states, a conclusion robustly supported by analyzing local magnetic fields measured via Cu and B NMR spectroscopy.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a concrete example of how combining high-field spectroscopy with advanced quantum simulation techniques can resolve fundamental structural puzzles in strongly correlated materials.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The study aims to determine the microscopic origin of magnetization plateaus observed in SrCu2(BO3)2, particularly those at low fractions like 1/8 and 2/15, which defy standard theoretical explanations.

Main result. NMR spectroscopy strongly supports a model where these plateaus are stabilized by Wigner crystals formed by spin-2 bound states (pinwheel structures), rather than simple crystal arrangements of triplets.

Method. The research combines advanced numerical simulations using iPEPS with detailed experimental analysis of Cu and B nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra across multiple high magnetic fields.

Model / system. The system is the layered antiferromagnet SrCu2(BO3)2, modeled by a 2D spin Hamiltonian on a Shastry-Sutherland lattice. The investigation probes local spin environments and magnetic superstructures responsible for quantized magnetization plateaus.

Key observables. Local magnetic fields ($H_i$) derived from NMR spectra (Cu sextuplets), spin polarization histograms, and line splittings in B NMR spectra.

Important parameters / regimes. Magnetization fractions (1/8, 2/15, 1/3); hyperfine coupling constants ($A, C, B$ for Cu; $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4$ for B).

Assumptions / limitations. The analysis relies on the assumption that NMR measurements accurately reflect local spin polarization and that specific magnetic stacking models (ABAB vs. ABC) can be distinguished by spectral features.

Figures summary. Figures show iPEPS calculated spin structures, histograms comparing different spin configurations, raw Cu NMR spectra at various fields corresponding to plateaus, and plots detailing line splittings in B NMR versus spin polarization differences.

Paper structure. The paper progresses from identifying the puzzle (unexplained plateaus) to applying multiple diagnostic tools: first, iPEPS simulations suggest Wigner crystals; second, Cu NMR constrains local fields using the 1/3 plateau; and third, detailed B NMR analysis distinguishes between competing magnetic stacking models (ABAB vs. ABC).

Abstract

The sequence of magnetization plateaus at 1/8, 2/15, 1/6, 1/4, 1/3, 2/5, and 1/2 of the saturation observed in SrCu$_2$(BO$_3$)$_2$ remained a puzzle until tensor-networks-based numerical simulations suggested that the low-magnetization plateaus are stabilized as Wigner crystals of spin-2 bound states, and not as one of the standard configurations (semiclassical up-down, or crystals of triplets). We report on $^{63,65}$Cu nuclear magnetic resonance spectra up to 41.9 T: Based on constraints deduced from the 1/3 plateau, we show that the spectra in the 1/8 and 2/15 plateaus indeed agree with the prediction for the spin-2 bound states, while being incompatible with a crystal of triplets. This adds the formation of Wigner crystals of bound states as an alternative fundamental paradigm in the theory of magnetization plateaus.

Spectral gap of Lee-Yang Hamiltonians

Summary. This paper proves that Lee-Yang Hamiltonians possess a robust spectral gap proportional to the applied magnetic field strength. By leveraging the zero-freeness of their partition function, the authors establish exponential decay of imaginary-time correlations. This result is significant because it provides an efficient quantum algorithm for calculating ground state energies in this important class of models.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a rigorous theoretical link between algebraic properties of partition functions (zero-free regions) and fundamental physical quantities like the spectral gap, which is crucial for understanding quantum phase transitions and computational complexity.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is proving a uniform lower bound on the spectral gap of Lee-Yang Hamiltonians when subjected to an external Z-field.

Main result. The ground state energy exhibits a spectral gap of at least h/4, independent of system size or coupling strengths. This also yields a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for computing this energy.

Method. The proof chains together results: establishing zero-freeness (Lee-Yang theorem) implies exponential decay of imaginary-time correlations, which in turn guarantees the spectral gap.

Model / system. The study concerns Lee-Yang Hamiltonians acting on n qubits, perturbed by a uniform Z-field ($H_h = H - h \sum Z_i$). The analysis uses concepts from quantum statistical mechanics, including partition functions and imaginary-time evolution.

Key observables. Spectral gap of the ground state energy; decay rate of two-point connected correlation functions $f_S(t)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The external field strength $h$; system size $n$; inverse temperature $eta$.

Assumptions / limitations. The proof critically relies on the zero-freeness property derived from the Lee-Yang theorem and careful error bounding when taking the continuum limit of Trotter approximations.

Paper structure. The structure moves logically from establishing the necessary mathematical prerequisites (zero-freeness) to proving correlation decay via Trotterization bounds, culminating in the final spectral gap proof and algorithmic implication.

Abstract

The Lee-Yang theorem and its quantum extensions state that, for a broad class of Hamiltonians on any graph, the partition function's zeros in the complex magnetic field plane lie only on the imaginary axis. For these Hamiltonians, we prove that under a uniform Z-field of any strength h, the ground state has a spectral gap of at least h/4, independent of the system size and of the coupling strengths. The proof uses the zero-freeness of the partition function as given by Asano and Suzuki-Fisher to show exponential decay of the imaginary-time correlations for any product of Z-operators. Our result gives a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for computing the ground state energy of any Lee-Yang Hamiltonian.

Summary. This paper analyzes how Restricted Boltzmann Machines fail when presented with Out-of-Distribution data. By augmenting the training set with random noise, the model undergoes an 'effective-rank collapse' in its learned weight structure. This spectral reorganization successfully forces the model to reject OOD inputs while maintaining high accuracy on known data.

Why it may be interesting. The work provides a rigorous mathematical framework linking information geometry (spectral rank) to generalization performance in deep learning models, which has parallels with understanding effective dimensionality in complex quantum many-body systems.

Detailed structure

Main problem. Restricted Boltzmann Machines (RBMs) used for classification are fragile under Out-of-Distribution (OOD) inputs, tending to absorb novel samples into learned class basins instead of rejecting them.

Main result. Training the RBM with auxiliary random binary images causes an 'effective-rank collapse' in the visible-visible interaction matrix J. This reorganization successfully enhances OOD rejection capability without degrading performance on in-distribution data.

Method. The analysis uses spectral properties of the induced weight matrix $J=WW^T$, quantified by an entropy-based effective rank ($ ext{reff}$), and examines the free energy landscape to diagnose structural changes.

Model / system. The model is a Restricted Boltzmann Machine (RBM) defined over visible and hidden configurations. The analysis focuses on the spectral properties of the induced interaction matrix $J = WW^T$, comparing training protocols using only in-distribution data (MNIST) versus augmented data including random binary images.

Key observables. Effective rank ($ ext{reff}$), eigenvalue spectrum of $J$, OOD rejection accuracy, and free energy landscape $F(v_x, v_y)$.

Important parameters / regimes. The ratio of spectral weight to the Marchenko–Pastur reference, number of Gibbs sampling steps ($k$), and hidden layer size ($N_h$).

Assumptions / limitations. The failure mode is structural and related to the spectral properties of $J$; $ ext{reff}$ is used as a robust measure of effective dimensionality.

Figures summary. Figures compare eigenvalue spectra evolution (Fig. 2) showing rank growth vs. collapse, free energy landscapes (Fig. 6) showing OOD samples shifting toward rejection basins, and performance metrics across different training protocols (Figs. 1, 3).

Paper structure. The paper systematically analyzes the spectral properties of RBMs trained on in-distribution data versus augmented data. It first establishes the failure mode using spectral analysis ($ ext{reff}$), then uses free energy calculations to show landscape reorganization, and finally confirms robustness across varying training parameters like $k$ (CD steps).

Abstract

Restricted Boltzmann machines (RBMs) represent data by shaping an energy landscape over visible and hidden configurations, but their discriminative use is fragile under out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs: samples outside the training distribution can be absorbed into one of the learned class basins rather than rejected. Here, we analyze this failure mode through the spectrum of the induced visible--visible interaction $J=WW^{T}$, where \(W\) is the visible--hidden weight matrix. Relative to a Marchenko--Pastur random-matrix reference, conventional training spreads spectral weight into many weak, bulk-compatible directions, increasing the effective rank of $J$. When auxiliary random binary images are assigned to a rejection label during training, the learned interaction undergoes effective-rank collapse: weak bulk-like modes are depleted, spectral weight concentrates into fewer dominant eigendirections, and the effective rank of $J$ approaches that of the empirical data covariance matrix. The resulting RBM rejects structured OOD image datasets while preserving MNIST classification accuracy, showing that random auxiliary exposure can reshape both the interaction spectrum and the free-energy landscape of an energy-based classifier.

Abstract

Can a small quantum computer forecast a changing signal better than an ordinary classical method? Many studies say yes, but the classical methods they compare against are often left in a basic, untuned state while the quantum model is carefully optimised. We ask what happens when the classical competitor is given exactly the same care: the same size and the same amount of tuning effort. We study two popular reasons a quantum reservoir is thought to help, using exact simulations of small quantum systems (up to eleven qubits) on prediction tasks. In both cases the quantum advantage disappears once the comparison is fair. Summary. This paper presents GroupFunctions.jl, a Julia library designed to compute individual matrix elements of irreducible representations of the unitary group U(d). The tool significantly streamlines calculations compared to assembling full representation matrices.

Why it may be interesting. Since quantum optics and photonic QI often involve calculating transition amplitudes or overlaps between complex states (which are governed by unitary transformations), having a dedicated tool for computing these matrix elements symbolically is highly valuable for theoretical modeling in this area.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The primary goal is to develop a computational tool for calculating individual matrix elements (group functions) of irreducible representations of the unitary group U(d). This avoids the need to compute the entire, potentially massive, representation matrix.

Main result. The authors introduce GroupFunctions.jl, a Julia library that allows for both symbolic and numerical computation of these specific matrix elements using Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns as an enumeration basis.

Method. The calculation relies on the Grabmeier-Kerber formula to express the group function as a sum of monomials in input parameters. The framework supports translating between various bases, including occupation-number kets.

Model / system. The mathematical structure is based on the unitary group U(d), which has physical relevance in quantum optics (e.g., transition amplitudes through linear optical networks) and boson sampling contexts.

Key observables. Group functions $\langle ext{out} | ext{D}(\lambda)( ext{U}) | ext{init} angle$, the character of the representation, and associated Schur polynomials.

Important parameters / regimes. The dimension $d$ of the unitary group, the representation label $\lambda$, and parameters defining the input unitaries U.

Assumptions / limitations. The current implementation is single-threaded, and a computational bottleneck exists due to iterating over double cosets, which can scale factorially ($N!$).

Paper structure. The paper introduces the library, details the mathematical formulation using Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns, provides examples of symbolic and numerical calculations for specific groups like U(10), and discusses computational scaling.

Abstract

GroupFunctions.jl is a Julia library for computing individual matrix elements of irreducible representations of U(d). These matrix elements, called group functions, can be evaluated symbolically or numerically. For SU(2), they reduce to the Wigner D-functions. The library computes these matrix elements in a carrier-space basis enumerated by Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns. It can also compute entire representation operators, construct input unitaries from parameterisations common in quantum optics, translate Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns into occupation-number kets, and compute the associated Schur functions. Results can be exported in a form compatible with Mathematica.

Authors: Spencer Topel

Summary. This paper analyzes the optimization challenge of Quantum Data Re-uploading Classifiers, identifying 'Fourier locking' as a spectral misalignment issue rather than simple barren plateaus. The authors propose and demonstrate that solving this requires a frequency-staged homotopy protocol that paces the target frequency during training to guide the quantum state into an optimal regime.

Why it may be interesting. It provides a concrete physical mechanism (spectral misalignment) for optimization failure in quantum machine learning models, offering a structured, physics-informed protocol (frequency pacing) to guide training away from poor local minima.

Detailed structure

Main problem. The optimization of Data Re-uploading Parameterized Quantum Circuits (DRU-PQCs) is hindered by 'Fourier locking' (FL), a structural failure mode causing local minima that resist gradient descent.

Main result. FL is fundamentally a spectral misalignment problem, which can be mitigated by implementing a frequency-staged homotopy protocol that paces the target frequency during training.

Method. The authors diagnose FL by tracking multiple Fisher diagnostics ($F_x$, FDR) rather than relying solely on gradient magnitudes, and propose solving it via curriculum learning based on frequency pacing.

Model / system. The model uses 4-qubit DRU-PQCs parameterized by encoding weights ($\omega$) and entangling layers ($ heta$). The circuit structure is fixed, but the optimization difficulty arises from the non-linear coupling between these parameters.

Key observables. Input-space quantum Fisher information ($F_x$), Fisher discriminant ratio (FDR), and Parameter-space Quantum Fisher Information Matrix (QFIM).

Important parameters / regimes. Target frequency range ($f: 1.0 o 3.0$); total trainable parameters $N=50$.

Assumptions / limitations. The failure is attributed to spectral misalignment rather than a loss of geometric sensitivity, which is confirmed by the QFIM remaining non-degenerate in trapped circuits.

Figures summary. Figure 5 details the 4-qubit DRU-PQC architecture with encoding and entangling blocks; Table I lists hyperparameters like qubit count (4) and total parameters (50).

Paper structure. The paper first defines FL using multiple diagnostics, proves that QFIM collapse is not the cause of failure, then introduces spectral homotopy as a remedy by pacing the target frequency, culminating in experimental results showing improved escape rates.

Abstract

Data re-uploading parameterized quantum circuits (DRU-PQCs) are universal function approximators, yet their expressivity produces oscillatory, non-convex loss landscapes that resist gradient-based optimization. We show that the primary optimization bottleneck in DRU-PQCs is not insufficient capacity but a structural failure mode we term Fourier locking (FL): because encoding weights and entangling layers are nonlinearly coupled, random initialization on high-frequency targets collapses the encoding parameters into spurious local minima. Two Fisher diagnostics characterize FL. The input-space quantum Fisher information $F_x$ measures the effective frequency content of the encoded state; the Fisher discriminant ratio of the measured features measures their alignment with the class labels. In two independent 50-seed experiments, the locking is literal: trapped circuits hold $F_x$ frozen for the entire run, while escaping circuits migrate their frequency content (direct training: $r_{pb} = -0.48$; curriculum: $d = 1.34$; both $p < 0.001$). The replicated signature is this spectral mobility, not any endpoint value of $F_x$, and trapped circuits retain a fully non-degenerate parameter-space QFIM ($r_{pb} \approx 0$): the failure is spectral misalignment of a responsive state, not a loss of geometric sensitivity. A frequency-staged homotopy protocol that paces the target frequency ($f: 1.0 \to 3.0$) convexifies the early loss landscape; escaping circuits raise $F_x$ in step with the curriculum, and the escape rate triples (18% vs. 6%). Fourier locking is a frequency-alignment problem, and its remedy is frequency pacing.

Abstract

We prove the existence of a nematic phase in a model of $l\times w$ hard rectangles on the square lattice with two allowed orientations and a large aspect ratio $k:=l/w$. The proof is based on a two-scale cluster expansion method developed previously by Disertori--Giuliani for hard rods in 2D and Disertori--Giuliani--Jauslin for hard plates in 3D. Abstract

Color codes are promising alternatives to surface codes for universal fault-tolerant quantum computing due to their simpler lattice-surgery protocols and the transversal implementation of logical Clifford gates. Abstract

We study scalar and vector bosons in the Bonnor--Melvin--$Λ$ spacetime within the Duffin--Kemmer--Petiau (DKP) formalism. By employing Umezawa's projection operators, we separate the physical spin-0 and spin-1 sectors and derive the corresponding exact second-order equations in the full curved spacetime, without relying on the conical approximation. For the scalar sector, the radial equation reduces to a Schrödinger-like equation with a trigonometric Pöschl--Teller effective potential. In the vector sector, the longitudinal mode is governed by the same effective potential, whereas the transverse polarizations are described by generalized trigonometric Pöschl--Teller potentials. Because the me-tric function vanishes at a discrete set of radial points, the radial dynamics is naturally formulated as a singular Sturm--Liouville problem on a fundamental interval, with the physical radial domain fixed by the Friedrichs self-adjoint extension of the corresponding singular radial operators. As a result, all physical sectors exhibit purely discrete radial spectra, and their eigenfunctions are obtained in closed form. These results provide a unified exact treatment of scalar and vector bosons in the Bonnor--Melvin--$Λ$ spacetime, complement previous analyses based on the conical approximation, and clarify the role of the global geometric structure of the background in shaping confinement and spectral properties.
